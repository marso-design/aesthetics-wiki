# Wuxia

_Also known as: Chinese Warriors_

**Wuxia** (武俠 lit. martial heroes) is a genre of Chinese historical fiction focusing on stories of martial artists and warriors in ancient Chinese settings. Wuxia works, while historically comprised mostly of literature, have grown to include Chinese opera, films, television series, manhua (comics), and video games. The genre boasts great popularity in Chinese-speaking areas around the world and is considered a prominent aspect of Asian popular culture.

Wuxia stories follow a hero of some kind, typically of a lower social class, as they fight evil and injustice. The heroes in wuxia works usually follow a form of chivalric, martial code, which differs between the storyteller and era the story is told in and acts similarly to Japanese samurais' *bushido*, and often belong to a secret warrior society related to the *jianghu* (rivers and lakes, or the greater world of outlaw martial artists)*.*

## History

### Development of "Xia"

*Shiji (史记)*, early history of China written about 85 bce, first used *Xia (侠)* as a category of historical figures, defining them as "their words were trustworthy, and their actions were resolute (其言必信，其行必果)". During the Wei, Jin, and Southern and Northern Dynasties, rudimentary stories of *Xia* began to appear in mythological tales. The ancient Chinese ballad of *Hua Mulan (花木兰)* was written around this time.

Scholars generally agree that *Wuxia* fiction developed mature narrative patterns and artistic characteristics during the 9th-century Tang dynasty. A wealth of "*Haoxia*" (豪侠) *Chuanqi* (传奇) short stories from this period — such as *Hongxian* (红线, lit. 'Red String'), considered China’s first true *Wuxia* novel, along with *Nie Yinniang* (聂隐娘) and *Qiuranke Zhuan* (虬髯客传)—remain classics today. Characters became more vivid and began to incorporate mystical elements like flying swords, laying the foundation for the *Jianghu (江湖)* in later literature. These works established the core themes and concept of the *Xia* figure in literature, setting a classic template for over a thousand years of *Wuxia* to follow.

From Song dynasty to Qing, Chinese fiction shifted from classical Chinese to vernacular language. Storytelling scripts from the Song and Yuan dynasties, along with full-length novels from the Ming and Qing, began placing *Xia* within broader social contexts. In the Qing dynasty, novels that combined detective stories with *Xia* tales created a character model that merged *Xia* with upright judical officials. This model later influenced the way martial arts fiction portrayed right and wrong.

### Modern Wuxia

During the Republic of China period, *Wuxia* novels had matured into a modern popular literary industry in terms of scale, dissemination, and writer community, marking a creative peak. A large number of writers and works emerged, forming a vast market, along with highly influential writer groups, distinct schools of style, and their representative figures and works. Lin Shu (林纴) was the person who coined the name of this genre. He first used *Wuxia* as a genre label for his short story *Fu Mei Shi (傅眉史)* published in *Xiaoshuo Daguan (小说大观)* in 1915. The 1928 film *Huoshao Hongliansi* (火烧红莲寺) was the first *Wuxia* film.

1950s to the 1980s was the golden age of "New School *Wuxia*". Represented by Hong Kong and Taiwan authors like Liang Yusheng, Jin Yong, and Gu Long, this period marked the modern transformation of martial arts literature. *Wuxia* fiction integrated historical depth, human complexity, and philosophical reflection. Since the 1990s, with the rise of *Wuxia* films, TV series, and games, *Wuxia* has become more visual and symbolic. The Wuxia fad arrived in the US with Chinese ethnic enclaves. With Bruce Lee, a Chinese-American martial artist, the aesthetic was popularized among American youth.

Although traditional *Wuxia* is declining today, its spirit and values have deeply influenced many areas of entertainment.

## Themes

The aesthetic is mainly focused on the fandom and general genre of films, books, and folklore that tell of Chinese warriors or martial artists fighting for what is right, usually in an ancient Chinese setting. Anyone who has an appreciation for the genre could be considered a part of the aesthetic, although actual martial artists are considered to be 'figures' of the aesthetic.

Centeral to the Wuxia genre are the Xia characters. They are portrayed as morally driven, skilled, and often detached from ordinary society, following their own path regardless of others' opinions or criticism. They may live by a personal code of honor, showing discipline, independence, and disdain for material gain. Loyalty and upholding justice over selfish interests, are central values. A fundamental duty of the Xia is to help those who cannot protect themselves – the poor, the oppressed, or the wrongly accused. This theme emphasizes compassion and active intervention. Some Wuxia narratives resemble detective stories where a Xia investigates crimes, catches villains, and restores fairness. The hero often operates outside official law, but delivers moral verdicts. The hero might believe their actions serve a higher moral order called Tianyi (天意, lit. “Heaven’s will”).

Martial skills are based on real-life Chinese martial arts, but are often depicted as highly refined, almost supernatural in execution:

* **Qing-gong** (轻功, lit. 'skill of weightlessness') allows the Xia to perform extraordinary feats of agility, such as running across water, leaping over walls, or gliding between treetops.
* **Qi-gong** (气功, lit. 'skill of breath') is the practice of harnessing and circulating chi within the body, enabling feats like enhanced strength, rapid healing, or striking without physical contact.
* Swords that obey their owner's will appear in many stories. **Yujian** (御剑, lit. 'sword controlling') refers to the ability to control a sword with one's mind or chi, allowing the user to make it fly through the air, attack enemies from a distance, or even stand on it for aerial travel.
* Striking specific **acupoints** on an opponent’s body with a finger can temporarily paralyzing them, stopping their movement, or even rendering them unconscious.

A major concept in Wuxia is the *Jianghu (江湖, lit. "rivers and lakes")*. It is an imagined sub-society of fighters, often parallel to historical China. It has its own rules, hierarchies, and codes. Key features include:

* Martial sects called Menpai (门派) or Zongmen (宗门): organized schools or clans (e.g., Shaolin, Wudang) with unique styles.
* Martial arts manuals: secret books containing powerful techniques, often sought after or fought over.
* Cycles of gratitude and revenge: relationships are driven by debts of kindness, demands for vengeance, and promises.
* Cathartic revenge: the hero repays both kindness and injury directly, without legal procedure. This brings emotional satisfaction and restores personal honor.

Many Chinese novel websites, such as Qidian, have their own distinct visual styles for wuxia book covers that often include white backgrounds and Chinese characters.

### Philosophy

Wuxia originated as folk literature. The stories were passed down orally among common people or written by scholars and officials who are not highly regarded or employed by the court. Today, as a popular literature mass-produced for entertainment, Wuxia is still rooted in lower/middle-class sensibilities.

Wuxia often rebels against several traditional constraints. The Xia is anti‑authority, dismissing excessive Confucian formalities, defying the imperial court, corrupt officials, or rigid martial sects, and preferring direct action. The protagonists refuse to accept predetermined fate, and carve their own paths through skill and will. Because of this Wuxia also breaks the gender norms, with the ealiest stories featuring female Xia characters or Mulan‑like narratives. Some Wuxia celebrates grassroots, where the protagonist is an orphan or a child of commoners.

A hero’s identity is frequently fluid or contested. Orphanhood and root‑seeking is a classic trope: protagonists like Yang Guo or Zhang Wuji lose their parents early and spend years searching for their origins. Masks and disguise are literal and metaphorical—characters hide their true faces, names, or intentions. Finally, revenge and self‑definition are intertwined: the pursuit of vengeance (e.g., for a murdered master or family) becomes a journey through which the hero discovers who they truly are.

Wuxia draws on several traditional Chinese philosophies while also echoing Western ones:

* Confucianism – loyalty (zhong 忠), filial piety (xiao 孝), benevolence (ren 仁), and ritual propriety (li 礼), though often questioned；
* Daoism – effortless wandering (xiao-yao 逍遥), non‑action (wu-wei 无为), and living naturally. Secluded from imperial rule, the Xia form their own codes and alliances in the Jianghu. This slightly mirrors anarchist ideals of voluntary association and decentralised order.
* Buddhism – karma, letting go of attachments, and saving others;
* Mohism – using power for benefitting everyone (jian-ai 兼爱) and not for aggression (fei-gong 非攻);
* Nationalism – resistance against foreign invaders and defence of the homeland.

Xiayi (侠义, lit. "Xia's Righteousness") is the most important philosophy of Wuxia. Often mistranslated as chivalry, Xiayi sums up most of the themes and philosophies of Wuxia, and can be understood as the code of the Xia - the moral and spiritual compass that guides a true Xia.

## Visuals

The visuals of Wuxia is based on the systematic use of Chinese culture.

Using traditional Chinese colors like **cinnarba red**, **malachite teal**, or **ocher tan** makes the visuals more convincing, but the platte is not fundamental to the Wuxia aesthetics and vary among works. **Black** and **white** are used to symbolize yin and yang. Since the 1990s was when Wuxia films and TV series were most popular, **lowering resolution** adds some Wuxia vibe.

Most Chinese martial arts fit in the Wuxia world. Influenced by the concept of Qing-gong, Wuxia's martial art visuals emphasize swift movement — **soaring leaps, spinning blades, and flowing sleeves**. Combat is sometimes balletic, especially in more recent works. **Slow-motion**, **rapid cuts**, and other action-film editing techniques are adapted.

The Jianghu features Chinese-painting-like sceneries, such as **snowy mountains, bamboo forests, peach blossom islands, cascading waterfalls, Gobi deserts** and **endless rivers** . Often shrouded in **fog, rain,** or **twilight**, these settings reflect the insignificance of individuals against nature’s grandeur, and **pavilions** hint the residence of a hermit. **Plum, orchid, bamboo, chrysanthemum, pine, cypress, lotus, cicadas, wild geese** and **cranes** symbolise nobility. People of the Jianghu live out of imperial power's reach in isolated **temples, pagodas,** or **Siheyuan** (四合院, 回-shaped building with a yard in the middle). The inside is furished with **screens, blinds, lanterns, calligraphy, paintings, redwood tables, chairs, benches, stools** and **a weapon rack**, with a lit **incense** to measure time. Make friends through drinking and playing **zithers** or ***go*** together.

Temporary stops at **run-down inns, taverns,** and **teahouses** are made between **horseback** journeys. **Alcohol**  is brewed and stored in large **wine jars** and is drank from bowls while **tea** is drank from handless **teacups**. The rustling of the bushes might be someone unwanted, or it might be a hungry **tiger** or pack of **wolves**. Poisonous **snakes, scorpions, centipedes** are fatal threats. Pills are made with **alchemy furnaces** and medicine is made through cooking **herbs**. Those who know their trade can find martial arts **manuals**  in the form of **scrolls, books**, or **scrolls of bamboo slips** and **magical artifacs** in the bustling **market**. Pay with **bronze coins** or **silver fragments.** Hide in the **boat** as it leaves the **dock** to evade pursuit.

## Fashion

*images needed*

The costume styles can be roughly divided into three categories according to the story background: China before Qing, Qing Dynasty, and Republican China. The latter two has generally historically accurate costumes, while stories set in ancient China have more imaginative costumes. The following paragraphs describes Wuxia costumes in settings before Qing, which is symbolised by **[jiaoling youren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garment_collars_in_hanfu#Jiaoling_youren)** .

Costume and makeup in Wuxia productions blend traditional Chinese clothing with stylized adaptations for action and character expression. Common traits include layered silhouettes and functional elements that allow free movement for fight scenes. Colors, textures, and accessories sometimes symbolize moral alignment, martial rank, or faction identity. Materials range from plain cotton and linen to silk and leather, sometimes with distressed or patched details used to convey ruggedness or humility.

### Weapons

Chinese medieval weapons are used. The hero usually uses a ***[jian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jian)* sword**, their companions might use **broadswords, spears,** or **bows**. Qi-gong requires fighting with **palms open** instead of holding fists. The villain probably also uses a sword, but his minions might use **battle axes, maces, poisoned darts**and **meteor hammers** (流星锤). Weapons are decorated with **red tassel**. **Mythical creatures** probably won't show up, but they appear on weapons and **scabbards**, along with abstract **cloud** patterns and **inscriptions**  in seal script in the carvings. **Folding fans, musical instruments,** and **dusters** can also be used as weapons.

### Tops

* Layered Hanfu jiaoling (交领, lit. 'cross-collar') collars
* Rolling up narrow sleeves allow combat mobility
* Wide sleeves and long clothes hints a character skilled in Qing-gong
* Frog buttons appear in Qing or Republican settings.

### Bottoms

* Loose trousers, usually tapered leg
* Trouser legs are fastend with puttee wraps or tucked into boots,

### Outerwear

* Long robes; Taoist robes or monk’s robes
* *Pifeng* (披风, lit. 'draped wind') capes, hooded capes, scarves
* *Chang* (氅) cloaks
* *Banbi* (半臂, lit. 'half-sleeve') vests
* *Huxin-jia* (护心甲, lit. 'heart-protecting armor) cuirasses
* straw raincoats
* [*Feiyufu*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feiyufu) (飞鱼服) is the uniform of the *Jinyiwei* (锦衣卫) imperial secret police of the Ming Dynasty

### Shoes

* Boots, sometimes iron-tipped; the *zao-xue* (皂靴) are black boots with white sole worn by government officals
* cloth shoes, sometimes with cross-bound straps
* straw sandals and wooden clogs

### Accessories

* Belts: cloth sashes, leather belts with metal hook, silk cords
* Wrist guards (leather/metal), bangles, finger sheaths
* Jade pendants, pouch, gourd (containing liquds or pills), knife sheath; all hung on the belt
* face veils, eye patches, masks

Headwear

* Li (笠) hats; the most symbolic hat of Wuxia
* Wei-mao (帷帽, lit. 'veiled hat'), worn by mysterious women
* Guan (冠) hats; has various styles and identifies the wearer as a scholar, official, or noble
* Zan (簪) hairpins; used by men and women of all social classes
* Xiaoyao-jin (逍遥巾, lit. 'carefree scarf'); hat worn by Daoist figures
* Hairbands are made with cloths

### Hair and Makeup

Both men and women have long hair, often tied into a bun or a ponytail; (not cutting one's hair is considered "[filial piety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filial_piety "wikipedia:Filial piety")" in the Confucian ideology)

* Loose, disheveled hair often signals outlaw status or inner turmoil
* precise buns and straight-edged brows convey discipline or heroism.
* Buddhist monks have their head shaved

* Different from history, many characters are **clean-shaven**
* Sanliu-ran (三绺髯, lit. 'three-strand beard') represents a refined, scholarly, and upright character, often a wise elder or a strategist
* Luosai-hu (络腮胡, lit. 'cheek-netting beard') symbolizes roughness, boldness, and unbridled strength. Such characters are often hot-tempered heroes or brutal, bandit-like villains.

* Jian-mei Xing-mu (剑眉星目, lit. 'sword-like brows and star-like eyes') is the traditional portray of a striking, noble, and spirited appearance.

## Subgenres

➔ Main article: Xianxia

*Xianxia* is a subgenre of *wuxia* where the characters are written in the mold of the Taoist *xian* or immortal, a demigod-like ideal that humans could supposedly strive to be in some branches of Taoism. Usually, the characters are quite powerful and use chi for energy-based attacks, and often, there is a multiverse and [biopunk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Biopunk "Biopunk") elements.
