**Reggae** is a Jamaican music genre that emerged in the late 1960s, developing from earlier styles like ska and rocksteady and quickly becoming the island’s dominant popular music. Defined by a moderate tempo, it has a strong, steady four-beat rhythm and a distinctive emphasis on the offbeat (often called the “skank”), typically played by guitar or keyboards while a deep, prominent bass line anchors the sound. It draws on influences from rhythm and blues, calypso, and other Afro-Caribbean and African-derived traditions.

Beyond its musical traits, reggae functions as a cultural and political voice, often associated with Rastafarian spirituality and themes of social justice, resistance, and the struggles of marginalized communities. Through iconic artists such as Bob Marley, the genre moved from Kingston’s ghettos to global stages in the 1970s, becoming an international symbol of Jamaican identity and broader anti-oppression movements.

## History

Reggae originated in Jamaica, where local musicians fused Afro‑Jamaican mento and spiritual drumming traditions with imported American blues and jazz. In the 1950s and early 1960s, this mix produced ska, a unique musical style with a walking bass and an offbeat guitar/piano. As social tensions and urban poverty grew, ska slowed into rocksteady, though it retained the offbeat rhythmic feel. This gradual deceleration, combined with experiments in studio rhythm and sound system culture, set the stage for a new lyrical focus that would be called reggae.

Producers such as Lee “Scratch” Perry and King Tubby, along with musicians like Jackie Mittoo and Winston Wright, played a role in solidifying the reggae beat. Early landmark singles include “Nanny Goat” by Larry Marshall and “No More Heartaches” by the Beltones. "Do the Reggay", a single released by Toots and the Maytals in 1968, was the first hit song to use the term. The new sound dropped some of rocksteady’s soul in favor of a room-shaking bass coupled with a funk‑adjacent pattern.

While still in its relative early years, reggae evolved into roots reggae, a phase marked by slower tempos and lyrics centered on diverse themes, like critiques of political and economic oppression. The 1966 visit of Ethiopian emperor Haile Selassie I to Jamaica galvanized the Rastafari movement. Many reggae musicians framed Selassie as a messianic figure (and Africa as a spiritual homeland), cementing the connection between the faith and the music.

Reggae’s international breakthrough came thanks to Bob Marley and The Wailers, who initially started as a ska and rocksteady group before embracing reggae’s emerging sound. With the support of Island Records founder Chris Blackwell, Marley’s albums, featuring songs like "Get Up, Stand Up" and "No Woman, No Cry", reached rock audiences in Europe and North America.

Reggae influences soon began appearing in mainstream music, primarily pop and rock. The Beatles’ “Ob‑La‑Di, Ob‑La‑Da” and Johnny Nash’s “Hold Me Tight” are considered early examples. British punk and new wave bands also borrowed these reggae rhythms.

By the 1980s, reggae split into multiple subgenres. Dub, for instance, used studio effects, remixing, and radical manipulations of bass and drums to turn reggae tracks into experimental soundscapes that would heavily influence hip‑hop, electronic music, and later remix culture. Dancehall, meanwhile, came with digital instrumentation and toasting (rhythmic talking).

Around the turn of the millennium, reggae had become a symbol of Jamaican national identity and a worldwide musical language. The United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO), for example, has recognized reggae as an element of the “intangible cultural heritage of humanity", while initiatives such as Reggae Month have helped establish the music’s legacy.

## Visuals

Reggae is strongly associated with the Rastafarian palette of red, gold (yellow), and green, typically paired with black or earthy tones. Each color carries a deeper meaning; red is frequently read as blood or struggle, gold as wealth or the sun, and green as the land or Africa. Iconography will often include, for example, the peace sign and depictions of the late Bob Marley. Posters, murals, and record sleeves have bold, hand‑painted lettering and slightly worn textures that evoke Kingston’s urban environment.

## Fashion

Reggae fashion grows out of Jamaican street culture, Rastafarian spirituality, and the political edge of the music itself. Usually, it favors outfits suitable for the Caribbean climate, like loose T‑shirts and simple cotton dresses. The palette is often punctuated by brighter accents, especially the colors associated with Rastafari and, more broadly, with Pan‑African pride and resistance. Said colors function as visual shorthand for themes central to reggae, whether it be reverence for Africa as homeland or a critique of oppressive systems. Layered and worn, these garments reflect reggae’s laid‑back but quietly defiant attitude.

A key element is headwear. Many reggae and Rastafarian adherents wear tams, crocheted or knitted caps large enough to hold dreadlocks. Dreadlocks operate as a kind of embodied fashion, rejecting Eurocentric grooming standards and embodying Biblical and Afrocentric symbolism. Accessories tend to be handmade or *look* handmade; many of these include beaded and wooden jewelry, leather wristbands, woven belts, and natural‑fiber bags. Footwear is simple and durable, from sandals and desert boots (like Clarks, which became iconic in Jamaican style) to basic sneakers. As reggae spread globally, these elements became recognizable markers of reggae culture, even as local scenes adapted them to their own climates and streetwear vocabularies.

## Music

Reggae is defined by a deep, groove-oriented foundation, set in 4/4 time. The bass guitar is the primary melodic driver, serving as the song’s lead voice, while the rhythm section, centered on the “one drop”, utilizes a kick and snare pattern on the third beat. The classic “skank” or “chop” on the offbeats provides a staccato counterpoint, maintaining an airy, non-dominant texture. Horn sections and supporting instrumentation are used sparingly for melodic riffs or counterpoints rather than saturation. Vocals are characterized by a soulful delivery; oftentimes, the lyrics revolve around social protest, spirituality, and personal struggle.

Harmonically, reggae relies on static, cyclical chord progressions that prioritize timbre and groove over rapid modulation. Modern production often features the “bubble”, a rolling, arpeggiated keyboard technique, and employs rounded tones with a deliberate use of low frequencies. A defining characteristic of the sound is the strategic application of reverb and delay, a stylistic nod to dub music that adds atmospheric depth while preserving the structural integrity of the groove.
