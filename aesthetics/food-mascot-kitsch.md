# Food Mascot Kitsch

_Also known as: Cereal Aisle Aesthetic, Brand Critter Aesthetic_

**Food Mascot Kitsch** is the main advertising aesthetic of the American [ultra-processed food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraprocessed_food "wikipedia:Ultraprocessed food") industry, characterized by anthropomorphized cartoon characters used to market packaged food and beverages, particularly to children. The aesthetic is consistently characterized by bright saturated primary colors, thick black outlines, exaggerated expressions of enthusiasm, and character designs deliberately engineered to exploit the infant-schema (*Kindchenschema*) response in young viewers. Its origins lie in the late nineteenth century with the first branded food trademarks, but the aesthetic reached its "golden age" in the 1950s and 1960s through the work of the Leo Burnett advertising agency in Chicago, whose characters (such as Tony the Tiger, the Jolly Green Giant, the Pillsbury Doughboy, Toucan Sam, and others) established a template that has became the dominant aesthetic of every supermarket's grocery aisle ever since.

The aesthetic's logic centers on suppressing any negative reality about the product, replaced by an image of uncomplicated happiness, abundance, and fun. Nutritional harm, addiction-by-design (termed "bliss point" in the industry), and predatory child-targeting are nowhere in the picture; what the picture contains instead is a cartoon tiger who is extremely enthusiastic about breakfast. The mechanism is [kitsch](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Kitsch "Kitsch") in the classical sense: the manufacture of an emotional response so warm and immediate that it forecloses any critical engagement with what is actually being sold. Brand loyalty is established before the child is old enough to evaluate the product on any other basis.
