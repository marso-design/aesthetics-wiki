# Guochao

_Also known as: Chinese fashion trend, National trend_

**Guochao** (国潮, lit. 'National Trend'), also known as "**China chic**" or "**Chinese fashion trend**", by official definitions, refers to an emerging sentiment in China that promotes Chinese ideas and cultural heritage, leading to a renewed preference for homegrown brands and products. In practice, the term *Guochao* is used to describe a design aesthetic that uses vibrant colors (especially red) and Chinese visual elements that developed during late Qing and Republican China, such as the qipao and the headdress of a *Dan*.

## History

*Needs Verification*

In February 2018, Li-Ning appeared at New York Fashion Week under the theme "Wudao" (悟道). This well-received show marked the start of the concept of Guochao. On May 10, 2018, Tmall rolled out the "China-Chic Initiative" (国潮行动), promoting joint collaborations between local brands and traditional Chinese cultural elements. 2018 was thus widely recognized as the first year of China-Chic. The term became mainstream after extensive media reports in the years that followed.

In 2020, Douyin creator YUMI优小米 open-sourced the Guochao Girl design for free commercial use. Cheap to produce, this design soon became popular among low-price takeout sellers. Gradually, internet users recognized Guochao packaging as a mark of unhygienic takeout. Around August 20, 2024, media outlets like Sichuan View News and Guangming Daily covered the related controversies extensively. "Guochao takeout" became nation-widely associated with shoddy workshop production, bad sanitation and pre-made food.

Consumers abandoned the term Guochao, and brands followed to move away from the narrow sense Guochao designs and returned to use the term Guo-feng. The media-defined broad sense Guochao market is still growing according to Xinhua News Agency.

### Terminology

Before the inventation of *Guochao*, *Guo-feng* (国风, lit. 'National Style') was the term widely used to describe aesthetics that used Chinese cultural elements. The term *Guochao* was probably invented to emphasize the modern interpretation and commercialization of these elements.

## Philosophy

The main followers of broad sense *Guochao* are millennials (born 1980-1995) and Generation Z (born 1995-2010). Unlike older generations who may have had more exposure to Western brands, these younger demographics have grown up during a period of rapid economic growth and increased national wealth in China. This background has fostered a strong sense of national identity and confidence in their traditional culture.

Guochao brands often incorporate traditional Chinese culture and style into their products and marketing. A notable example is the sportswear brand Li-Ning, which made a comeback by integrating traditional Chinese cultural elements into its designs, particularly during its 2018 New York Fashion Week appearance. The trend is not limited to new products; many long-established Chinese brands have revitalized themselves by introducing innovative marketing strategies, co-branding initiatives, and leveraging digital channels and online influencers to connect with younger consumers. Examples include the liquor maker Luzhou Laojiao launching perfume and White Rabbit candy collaborating on a lip balm.

The rise of broad sense *Guochao* is also closely linked to the popularity of Chinese social media platforms like Xiaohongshu, Douyin, and Weibo, where young people share content related to Chinese culture and domestic brands. This trend signifies a shift in consumer behavior where Chinese consumers increasingly prioritize design, quality, and cultural connotation over brand premium. Guochao represents a broader cultural movement of rediscovering and blending China's past with its present, influencing aspects of modern Chinese life.

The broad sense *Guochao* is encouraged by government policies.

## Visuals

The narrow sense *Guochao* as a design aesthetic is not exactly what its official definition suggests. It uses a very limited and fixed set of visual elements:

* Bright and vibrant contrasting colors, especially red and turquoise
* Traditional Chinese architecture, especially the Forbbiden City
* Young girls in modern and sexy qipao, tangzhuang, or Neo-Chinese fashion
* Peking opera costumes, especially the headdresses of *Dan* (旦) female roles
* Dense traditional patterns
* Traditional *xiangyun* (祥云) cloud patterns
* Loong, fenghuang, red-crowned crane
* The sunglasses that a stereotypical Chinese fortune teller wears
* *Jianghu-style* (江湖体) calligraphy art font
* Folding fans with large calligraphy
* Rebellious postures

## Subgenres

The term *Guochao* is a general term favored by offical media to emphasize the modern interpretation and commercialization of all of China's traditional elements, thus it covers many subgenres whose practitioners rarely use this term for self-reference.

### Neo-Chinese Style

➔ Main article: [Neo-Chinese Style](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Neo-Chinese_Style "Neo-Chinese Style")

In official reports and similar articles, the term *Guochao* is basically equivalent to *Neo-Chinese Style*. In practice, products tagged Neo-Chinese are usually less kitschy and more innovative. In terms of terminology, Neo-Chinese is used in architecture while Guochao isn't

### Gu-feng

*Gu-feng* (古风, lit. 'Ancient-style') is a music and fashion aesthetic inspired by Chinese period drama and historical drama. Since the invention of this term predates people's emphasis on authenticity, this aesthetic style does not emphasize historical accuracy. In fact, in many contexts, this word has little difference from *Guo-feng*.

### Han-yuansu

*Han-yuansu* (汉元素, lit. 'Han-elements') is the aesthetic that emphasizes the restoration and rediscovering of Han Chinese cultural elements before the Qing dynasty. Originated from the Hanfu movement, this aesthetic is almost entirely dominated by the fields of fashion and clothing. Unlike other subgenres of *Guochao* that are only used as commercial tags, this subgenre has a large community of practitioners from the Hanfu movement.

### Minzu-feng

*Minzu-feng* (民族风, lit. 'Ethnic-style') is used to refer to aethetics of using cultural elements from any of the 55 ethnic minorities of China. This aesthetic is highly favored by the tourism and photography industry. Naturally, this kind of aesthetics can be classified according to the specific originating ethnic group, among which the more well-known are:

* *Zang-shi* (藏式, lit. 'Tibetan-style'), an aesthetic seen in jewelry and other ornaments, inspired by Tibetan culture, especially Tibetan Buddhism.
* *Xiyu-feng* (西域风, lit. 'Western-region-style') an aesthetic inspired by ancient cultures of [Dunhuang](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dunhuang_Feng "Dunhuang Feng") and the Western Regions, cultures of Uyghurs and other ethnic minorities of Xinjiang, and stereotypes of Arab culture (brought into China by Western entertainment). Commonly seen in video games.

## Criticism

Criticism directed at Guochao usually focus on the flawed ways brands implement this popular style.

One of the most prominent problems is overhyped marketing that reduces traditional culture into hollow slogans. Countless brands simply paste cultural symbols onto their products and casually brand them as Guochao pieces, suffering from a severe lack of diverse and original design creativity. This superficial approach leaves customers feeling that the designs lack depth and genuine aesthetic value, as they clearly perceive that brands are merely capitalizing on public national sentiment to drive sales. Gradually, consumers grow tired of these stereotyped designs and develop a sense that their patriotic emotions are being exploited for commercial profits.

Another major source of public frustration is the forced association between consumption and patriotism, best represented by the problematic claim that failing to buy domestic goods means a lack of patriotism seen on the internet. Turning ordinary shopping behavior into a moral judgment sparks public resistance, for consumers only seek products that are attractive in appearance, reliable in function and reasonable in price, rather than enduring unnecessary moral tests every time they make a purchase.
