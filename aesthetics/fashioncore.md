# Fashioncore

_Also known as: Fashionxcore, FXC_

[Sensitive Content Notice](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Aesthetics_Wiki:Content_Warning_Policy "Aesthetics Wiki:Content Warning Policy") ⚠️

The following article contains and discusses content that may be distressing to some readers.  
**Reason for Warning:** This page contains mentions of suicide and self-harm.

**Fashioncore** (sometimes also written as **Fashionxcore** or **FXC**) is a fashion style emerged in the early 2000s, with its origins traceable to the late 1990s [Hardcore Punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hardcore_Punk "Hardcore Punk") and related music scenes. The term initially originated as a tongue-in-cheek or outright derogatory label used within the hardcore scene to describe bands that, alongside their music, emphasized a dark and stylized look. This look drew from influences such as [Horror Punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Horror_Punk "Horror Punk") and [Glam Rock](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Glam_Rock "Glam Rock").

As the label spread, it became attached to a recognizable youth subculture whose participants were sometimes called "Fashioncore kids." Outside of hardcore punk circles, however, mainstream media repeatedly mislabeled the style as "[emo](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Emo "Emo")," which in turn produced the terms "new emo kids" or "fake emos" as a way of distinguishing the fashion-conscious hardcore look from the emo subculture it was being lumped in with.

The distinction mattered to those inside the scene. Traditional emo carried associations of sincere sensitivity and introspection, while the "fake emo" or Fashioncore look pushed those same signifiers into exaggeration, incorporating gloomier iconography inspired by [goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Goth "Goth") culture, including references to self-harm and suicide that were themselves controversial within the goth scene.

By the mid-2000s, the style had spread well beyond its hardcore origins. As it did, a split developed: some who had adopted the look moved toward the emerging [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene") subculture, whose musical and visual priorities were diverging heavily from the early hardcore roots. Bands like Tokio Hotel and Avril Lavigne began appearing as reference points, which for many original participants marked the point where the two crowds had fully separated.

## History

### Origins (2000–2003)

The term "Fashioncore" is believed to have originated around the early 2000s within the Metalcore and Hardcore scenes of Southern California, with Orange County cited most often. It started as a joke or an insult aimed at bands like Eighteen Visions (18V), Atreyu, and Bleeding Through, who had abandoned the standard hardcore dress code of camo cargo shorts, hoodies, and baseball caps in favor of designer jeans, flat-ironed hair, and makeup.

Scene lore holds that the first "Fashioncore" t-shirt was made not by a band but by a merch guy, often identified as Floyd and variously associated with Taking Back Sunday or Eighteen Visions, as a joke at the expense of "scenesters" who would fix their hair between songs in the mosh pit. The term then escaped its original context and became a catch-all for any heavy band that was visibly preoccupied with how they looked.

### The "Boom" and Divergence (2004–2006)

By 2004 and 2005, the look had hardened into something recognizable enough to be called a style. The release of Eighteen Visions' *Vanity* is often cited as the moment it crystallized: jet-black dyed hair, flat-ironed swoops, and tight pants became the shorthand for what Fashioncore looked like.

The confusion with [Emo](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Emo "Emo") peaked during this same period. Mainstream press treated the two as interchangeable, while within the scene, the Fashioncore crowd was increasingly gravitating toward the colorful, MySpace-driven [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene") aesthetic that was taking shape alongside it. By the late 2000s, many of the style's early adopters had either moved toward mainstream rock or crossed over fully into Scene.

## Fashion

### Hair

For men, the signature feature was long straight bangs worn to the side but without covering the eye, distinguishing the look from the "lazy eye" sweep associated with [emo](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Emo "Emo"). Hair was often professionally cut, sometimes with sections bleached against a black base to produce what was known as the "skunk" look.

For women, side-swept bangs were similarly common, often pinned back with a clip, and hair was frequently teased at the back for volume. Dyeing was standard, and colored extensions featuring stripes, zebra print, or leopard print were used to add pattern as well as color.

### Clothing

Men typically wore tight black skinny jeans, often women's jeans purchased specifically for the narrower cut, paired with boots and fitted band t-shirts. Slim hoodies and belts with large buckles were common, and bandanas or scarves sometimes doubled as hair accessories.

Women initially mirrored the men's silhouette, in slim jeans and band tees, before influences from the emerging [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene") subculture introduced dresses, [punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Punk "Punk")-inflected tutus, ripped tights, and footwear split between boots and Converse or Vans.

### Accessories and Motifs

White studded belts were worn across genders and became one of the more immediately recognizable markers of the style. Other common accessories included diamond motifs, brass knuckles, stars, hearts, and skulls, alongside iconography from brands like Gloomy Bear, Skeletanimals, and Hello Kitty. Bone prints drawn from [Horror Punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Horror_Punk "Horror Punk") and striped patterns also appeared with regularity.

## Music

"Fashioncore" in itself is not a music genre, but it is frequently used to refer to the specific wave of Metalcore, Post-Hardcore, and Melodic Hardcore bands that popularized the aesthetic.

### Bands

* Eighteen Visions (the band did not create Fashioncore, but were one of the most cited examples of it)
* Atreyu
* Bleeding Through
* Avenged Sevenfold (specifically the *Waking the Fallen* and *City of Evil* eras)
* From Autumn to Ashes
* The Used (early)
* AFI (*Sing the Sorrow* era)

## Comparison to [Emo](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Emo "Emo") and [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene")

Fashioncore is frequently conflated with both [Emo](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Emo "Emo") and [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene"), though the three are distinct in origin and character. Emo was primarily a music genre since the 1980s before it ever had a look, rooted in indie rock and emotional hardcore acts like Sunny Day Real Estate and The Promise Ring, and the fashion style most people associate with it (black swoop hair, tight jeans, and band tees) developed later as the genre crossed into the mainstream in the early 2000s.

Fashioncore, by contrast, came out of a harder-edged Metalcore and Post-Hardcore scene and was never really a self-applied label at all. The confusion between the two was largely a media invention: mainstream outlets used "emo" as a catch-all for any young person in dark clothes with dyed hair, flattening a meaningful distinction that participants on both sides were conscious of.

[Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene") is the more direct successor. Where Fashioncore retained its ties to heavy music and carried a certain aggression in its presentation, Scene shed both almost entirely, orienting itself around MySpace, neon colors, and an aesthetic logic that owed more to internet culture than to any particular music scene. Many of the same people moved between the two looks across the mid-to-late 2000s, which is part of why the boundaries are blurry in retrospect, but the underlying values were different enough that those inside the scene treated the distinction as real.

## Criticism

From the start, "Fashioncore" was a term used against people rather than as a self-descriptor. Within the hardcore scene, it was a way of accusing bands of caring more about their image than the music, and some Urban Dictionary definitions circulating at the time described the label in exactly those terms, mentioning fashion items like studded belts and the haircuts as evidence of inauthenticity.

The controversy extended to the music itself. Eighteen Visions singer James Hart pushed back in interviews, saying: "It's r\*\*\*\*\*ed... We're not fans of the term at all. How does the sound of your music have anything to do with the way you look? It doesn't." Others within the scene argued that the whole criticism was a form of gatekeeping, and that deliberate fashion expression had never been incompatible with musical seriousness.
