# Goth

_Also known as: Batcaver, Positive Punk (historically), Grufti (German)_

*This article is about the Goth subculture that formed around gothic rock music. Not to be confused with the broader literary sensibility of [Gothic](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Gothic "Gothic"), medieval [Gothic architecture](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Gothic_architecture "Gothic architecture"), or [Gothic Lolita](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Gothic_Lolita "Gothic Lolita").*

**Goth** is a music-based subculture that emerged in the late-1970s to early 1980s in the United Kingdom, particularly in London and the North of England (Leeds). Gothic rock, initially nameless or referred to by the press as "positive punk," originally derived from the [post-punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Post-Punk "Post-Punk") movement of the time, which included acts such as Joy Division, Bauhaus, and Siouxsie and the Banshees. The term "Goth" was originally used by music journalists to describe bands with dark subjects, the first of which was The Doors, with their track "*The End*." Stylistically, the subculture is characterized by a style that combines various influences, such as 19th-century [Victorian](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Victorian "Victorian") mourning attire, fetish wear, and the androgynous theatricality of [Glam Rock](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Glam_Rock "Glam Rock").

Goth as a concrete music genre solidified with the release of Bauhaus' debut single "*Bela Lugosi's Dead*," aided by the opening of The Batcave, a SoHo club where many early performers originated. Since that release, many bands have formed to create and expand on the genre, intentionally or not. However, a lot of the original bands considered staples of Goth rock actually reject the label—especially musicians such as Andrew Eldritch of Sisters of Mercy.

Unlike the broader "[alternative](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Alternative "Alternative")" umbrella term, the Goth community is strictly tied to music genres such as Gothic Rock, Dark Wave, [Deathrock](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Deathrock "Deathrock"), and Cold Wave, and sometimes other offshoots such as Ethereal Wave, [Gothabilly](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Gothabilly "Gothabilly"), Neoclassical Dark Wave, and Gothic Metal. Because of this, engagement with Goth music is the primary identifier of the subculture; fashion, while visually distinctive, is considered secondary and optional. A common misconception is that Goths maintain elaborate makeup and clothing daily; in reality, many Goths prioritize musical involvement over aesthetic commitment.

In the 1980s, the Goth scene spread internationally, with distinct regional developments: America's [Deathrock](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Deathrock "Deathrock"), Germany's massive *Grufti* scene, Spain's *[Movida Madrileña](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Movida_Madrile%C3%B1a "Movida Madrileña")* (featuring bands like Alaska and Parálisis Permanente), France and Belgium's Cold Wave, Portugal's "Grey Rock," and Japan's pre-[Visual Kei](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Visual_Kei "Visual Kei") gothic rock. Each scene contributed unique musical and aesthetic elements while maintaining connections to the UK Gothic Rock lineage. Its influence has only continued to grow since then. Today, Goth thrives as an underground subculture with many organized events and festivals. The most popular is the annual Wave-Gotik-Treffen (WGT) held in Leipzig, Germany, which also provides a community for the broader *[Schwarze Szene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dark_Culture "Dark Culture")*.

## History

### Etymology and Origins

The application of the word "Gothic" to a dark aesthetic has a historical lineage spanning over 1,500 years. It originated with the Germanic [Visigoth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Visigothic_Art "Visigothic Art") tribes who sacked Rome in 410 AD, an event that permanently associated the term with the destruction of classical order. In the Renaissance, art historian Giorgio Vasari used the term pejoratively to describe medieval [Gothic architecture](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Gothic_architecture "Gothic architecture"), labelling it a "barbarous German style" in contrast to classical symmetry.

During the 18th and 19th centuries, the term was reclaimed by the [Romantic movement](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Romanticism "Romanticism") and the Gothic Revival in architecture. This period birthed the literary [Gothic](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Gothic "Gothic"), characterized by the 1816 gathering at Villa Diodati where Lord Byron, Percy Shelley, and Mary Shelley explored themes of the supernatural and the macabre, producing texts like *Frankenstein* and *The Vampyre* that laid the thematic groundwork for the modern Goth subculture.

### Musical Application

In a musical context, the term "Gothic" was first documented in October 1967. Music critic John Stickney used the phrase "Gothic rock" in the student newspaper *The Williams College Record* to describe the atmosphere of a press event for [The Doors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Doors "wikipedia:The Doors"), taking place in a gloomy, vaulted wine cellar in New York. Stickney noted that the band's music possessed a "cold and terrifying edge" akin to the national taste for violence. In the late 1970s, the term appeared sporadically in the British music press (notably by Nick Kent and Anthony H. Wilson) to describe bands like Siouxsie and the Banshees and Joy Division, referring to their "dank neo-gothic sound" or "gothic dance music." However, the musicians themselves rarely accepted the label.

### The Post-Punk Coalescence

The Goth subculture proper crystallized in the vacuum left by the dissolution of the original [punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Punk "Punk") movement. As the initial explosion of 1976 faded, a "new dark age" emerged, characterized by a shift from the aggression of punk to a more atmospheric and rhythmic sound. This period, retrospectively termed "[post-punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Post-punk "Post-punk")," saw the rise of bands like Bauhaus, whose 1979 single "*Bela Lugosi's Dead*" combined dub-influenced basslines with [vampire](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Vampire "Vampire") imagery, effectively creating a prototype for the genre.

Two specific club scenes were highly influential in establishing the social and visual elements of the movement. In Leeds, the club Le Phonographique (opened in 1979) established a darker, post-punk dancefloor sound that influenced bands like The Sisters of Mercy. Simultaneously in London, The Batcave (opened in July 1982) provided a physical hub for the scene's "vampire punks" and established the high-camp, fetish-influenced visual aesthetic. In 1983, the *NME* journalist Richard North attempted to classify this wave of bands (including Blood and Roses and Brigandage) as "Positive Punk," a term that briefly competed with "Goth" before the latter became the accepted nomenclature.

### International Popularization

#### The German Scene

By the early 1990s, Germany had displaced the UK as the central hub for the Goth subculture. Known locally as the "*Grufti*" scene, this movement was characterized by a philosophical revulsion against rationalist, production-driven modern society and the alienating effects of capitalism on the individual. Participants synthesized this critique with a nostalgia for the past, drawing specific inspiration from German history, including the mysticism of Hildegard von Bingen, the [Romanticism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Romanticism "Romanticism") of Joseph von Eichendorff, and early [Expressionism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Expressionism "Expressionism").

Unlike the club-based scenes of London, the German infrastructure developed into massive, city-wide events that institutionalized the subculture. The Wave-Gotik-Treffen (WGT) in Leipzig, established in 1992, grew from a small gathering into the world's largest festival for the Goth subculture and the broader *[Schwarze Szene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dark_Culture "Dark Culture")*. It integrates the city's high culture, utilizing venues such as opera houses, museums, and cemeteries for performances. Similarly, the M'era Luna festival in Hildesheim established a massive commercial platform for the scene.

While the majority of this movement focused on a radical critique of a "one-dimensional society," the German scene also faced internal controversy. Specific factions within the subculture moved toward militant fascist aesthetics and a musical interpretation of controversial figures like Gottfried Benn, creating a tension between the scene's romantic escapism and political extremism.

## Fashion

### Clothing

Goth fashion was described by its participants as a move away from the "drab" denim of the post-[hippie](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hippie "Hippie") era toward a "high-fashion fetishism" and "dramaticism." As fashion historian Ted Polhemus describes it, the look consists of a "profusion of black velvets, lace, fishnets and leather tinged with scarlet or purple, accessorized with tightly laced corsets, gloves, precarious stilettos and silver jewelry depicting religious or occult themes." These materials are drawn from two distinct traditions: 19th-century Victorian mourning attire, and the sexual subversion of BDSM and fetish culture, which entered the scene via the influence of punk boutiques like Vivienne Westwood and Malcolm McLaren's SEX on early figures such as Siouxsie Sioux.

Common wardrobe staples include leather jackets (often painted or studded), fishnet tights worn on legs or arms and frequently ripped, and winklepicker boots; a pointed style revived from the 1950s [Teddy Boys](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Teddy_Boys "Teddy Boys") subculture. Corsetry, recontextualized from historical undergarments to outerwear, became a staple of the look. Accessories tend to be heavy and metallic: silver skull rings, ankhs, crucifixes, and bone necklaces, drawing from the stage wear of The Cramps and Siouxsie Sioux.

### Hair and Makeup

Goth makeup draws from the pallor of silent film stars, most notably Theda Bara, the 1910s femme fatale known for her kohl-rimmed eyes, as well as horror cinema and German [Expressionism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Expressionism "Expressionism"). Faces are painted pale white or porcelain, then rebuilt with sharp black eyeliner and dark lipstick in shades of burgundy, purple, or black. Siouxsie Sioux set the template most visibly: NME critic Paul Morley, reviewing a 1980 Banshees gig, noted that roughly half the women in the audience had modeled their appearance directly after her. Patricia Morrison, Nina Hagen, and Rozz Williams were among the other early figures who contributed to the look alongside her. Hair is styled for maximum volume, achieved through backcombing, crimping, and heavy hairspray to produce the gravity-defying shapes associated with the subculture's earliest performers.

## Substyles and "Goth Types"

The concept of categorizing Goths into "types" originated as an inside joke among Usenet goths on alt.gothic in the 1990s, a tongue-in-cheek personality taxonomy that included absurdist entries like "Burritogoth" (those who have experienced tortillas) and "Htoggoth" (dyslexic goths). This was never meant as a serious style classification system; it was self-aware humor by older goths about their own subculture. However, the transition from Usenet to LiveJournal around 2001–2003 marked a critical shift: younger goths discovering these lists through social media had no context that they were jokes. As older goths gradually migrated their communities to LiveJournal, the terminology remained, but the irony was lost. By the mid-2000s, what had been elder goth banter was being taken seriously by a new generation of internet goths who listed "goth types" in their profiles as if they were legitimate subcultural categories rather than an in-joke.

This shift accelerated with the 2007 publication of "Goth Craft" by Raven Digitalis, which devoted multiple pages to categorizing goth "types" in a way that treated them as actual subcultural divisions. This formalization coincided with the rise of illustrated "goth type" guides and personality tests on DeviantArt and Tumblr in the late 2000s and 2010s, which further cemented the idea that goth fragmented into dozens of distinct aesthetic and personality categories. In reality, the subculture has never hyperfragmented in the way these lists suggest; older goths continue to emphasize individualism over rigid type-based identity.

### Named Substyles with Genuine Subcultural Roots

The most established of these substyles is [Trad Goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Trad_Goth "Trad Goth"), referring to the original 1980s look: backcombed hair, heavy black eyeliner, and a DIY [punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Punk "Punk")-influenced wardrobe of ripped fishnets and leather. This emerged organically from the first wave goth bands and UK post-punk scene. [Deathrock](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Deathrock "Deathrock") is closely related but carries a grittier West Coast [horror-punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Horror_Punk "Horror Punk") influence, often featuring the deathhawk hairstyle and tattered layered clothing; it has roots in 1980s Los Angeles punk and experienced a notable revival beginning in the late 1990s. [Romantic Goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Romantic_Goth "Romantic Goth") shifts toward the softer end of the spectrum, using flowing velvet and lace in deep jewel tones to reference 19th-century [Romantic](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Romanticism "Romanticism") imagery, a natural aesthetic evolution within the subculture. [Victorian Goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Victorian_Goth "Victorian Goth") takes a more historically specific approach, incorporating frock coats, high-collared shirts, and mourning corsetry; it frequently overlaps with the [Steampunk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Steampunk "Steampunk") community.

### Internet-Popularized Variations and Aesthetic Borrowing

Many other named variations gained traction primarily through internet illustration and social media rather than through organic scene development. [Corporate Goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Corporate_Goth "Corporate Goth") (subtle gothic accessories adapted for office wear), [Fetish Goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Fetish_Goth "Fetish Goth") (latex, vinyl, and harnesses as dark glamour), [Hippie Goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hippie_Goth "Hippie Goth") (gothic fashion combined with bohemian elements), "White Goth" (an inverted color palette of all-white or cream retaining the dramatic layering of goth fashion), [Military Goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Military_Goth "Military Goth") (accessories such as military caps), and [Gothabilly](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Gothabilly "Gothabilly") (gothic motifs merged with 1950s [Rockabilly](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rockabilly "Rockabilly") pin-up silhouettes) represent styling tendencies that existed informally but were formalized and named through online communities. The "Perky Goth" label in particular was mostly a personality-based variation popularized on social media profiles and as a pop culture cliché rather than a self-identification used by goths. On the other hand, [Nu-Goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Nu-Goth "Nu-Goth") emerged as a genuine 2010s fashion trend mixing traditional goth elements with streetwear and occult symbology, developed primarily through social media platforms like Instagram rather than localized club scenes.

### Mislabelling

Many online sources have applied the "goth" label to styles originating outside the subculture proper, reinforcing the misconception that any dark or [alternative](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Alternative "Alternative") aesthetic is a variant of Goth rather than a distinct lineage. [Emo](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Emo "Emo"), [Rivethead](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rivethead "Rivethead"), and [Metalhead](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Metalhead "Metalhead") aesthetics have roots in entirely separate musical and subcultural histories (post-hardcore, industrial music, and heavy metal respectively) yet are frequently framed as "goth types" in internet communities (e.g., "Emo Goth," "Industrial Goth," "Metalhead Goth"). [Pastel Goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Pastel_Goth "Pastel Goth"), similarly, draws inspiration from goth elements and makeup traditions to a deliberately light color palette; while widely labeled as a goth substyle online, is more of a separate goth-inspired fashion trend than as an actual subcultural identity or style rooted in goth music and community participation.

## Philosophy

The Goth subculture does not have a formalized ideology, and most participants would reject the idea that it does. What coheres the scene philosophically is better described as a set of shared values: a genuine appreciation for the macabre, a rejection of forced cheerfulness, and an emphasis on individualism expressed through creative self-presentation. These values emerged organically from the post-punk environment rather than being consciously theorized.

The subculture has historically maintained an unusual tolerance for gender nonconformity and sexual ambiguity. The early club scene, described by Robb as a space of "androgyny and angst," normalized fluid dress and behavior at a time when mainstream culture offered little room for either. This was not articulated as a political position so much as a structural feature of the scene's social spaces: clubs were safe havens where the ordinary rules of public life were suspended.

The relationship to dark subject matter, such as death, the occult, Victorian mourning culture, and horror imagery, is aesthetic rather than ideological. The subculture draws on a long literary and artistic lineage derived from [Dark Romanticism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dark_Romanticism "Dark Romanticism"), but participants engage with this tradition through music and fashion, not doctrine. As Robb observes, goth processed its morbid influences through the dancefloor rather than despair, and the emphasis on communal and physical participation has remained central to the scene.

## Misconceptions

### Goth as Depressive

Goth is often misunderstood by outsiders as a subculture centered on depression or misery. In practice, the scene emphasizes music, dance, and community. Dark and melancholic music is a creative outlet for participants rather than a symptom of mental illness. Goth clubs and events center on the dancefloor, where attendees participate in social dancing rather than isolated reflection. The aesthetic appreciation of darkness exists alongside this participatory and life-affirming social culture.

### Satanism and Violence

Goth fashion frequently incorporates occult imagery, like inverted crosses, pentagrams, and religious iconography drawn from horror cinema, Victorian spiritualism, and transgressive art movements. These symbols are employed for shock value, aesthetic effect, and as representations of rebellion against mainstream religious or social norms, rather than a literal expression of devil worship. The subculture is fundamentally non-violent; historical Goth events and clubs prioritized music, fashion, and social connection. Unlike more aggressive subcultures such as [Oi!](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Oi! "Oi!") that attracted working-class militancy and territorial violence or certain [heavy metal](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Metalhead "Metalhead") scenes, violence at Goth venues was historically uncommon.

### Distinction from Rivethead

Goth and [Rivethead](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rivethead "Rivethead") are frequently conflated, though they constitute separate musical and aesthetic lineages. Goth emerged from post-punk and glam rock (Bauhaus and The Cure); Industrial arose from mid-1970s "anti-music" experimentation (pioneered by Throbbing Gristle and Cabaret Voltaire). Though both share club spaces and audiences, their elements differ: Goth draws from [Dark Romanticism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dark_Romanticism "Dark Romanticism") and [Victorian](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Victorian "Victorian") aesthetics; [Industrial](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Industrial "Industrial") reflects dystopian and utilitarian themes. The confusion largely stems from late 1980s–90s club crossovers where both subcultures co-existed in the dancefloor. [Cybergoth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Cybergoth "Cybergoth"), despite its name, is associated with [Aggrotech](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Aggrotech "Aggrotech")/[EBM](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/EBM "EBM")/Futurepop music rather than any of the core goth genres.

### Distinction from Emo

Though often conflated, Goth and [Emo](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Emo "Emo") are different subcultures with distinct origins, aesthetics, and musical foundations. Goth emerged from post-punk in the late 1970s and early 1980s, drawing from gothic literature, Victorian fashion, and horror imagery. Emo developed separately from 1980s Washington D.C. hardcore punk; bands like Rites of Spring pioneered emotionally direct and confessional lyrics that prioritized vulnerability over cool detachment. Even though both subcultures embrace dark themes and black clothing, Emo's aesthetic is more youthful and contemporary, characterized by tight clothing and side-swept hair.

## Music

Music is the primary unifying foundation of the Goth subculture. Although the word "gothic" in music can be used very broadly and its sound is diverse, all of its main genres share a lineage traceable to late-1970s British [post-punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Post-Punk "Post-Punk"), and are generally characterized by a melancholic, atmospheric quality that remains danceable. The dancefloor has always been central to the subculture; goth clubs preceded a clearly defined "goth sound," and the music was shaped as much by what worked on a Saturday night as by any deliberate artistic program.

As the scene spread internationally through the mid-to-late 1980s, regional variants emerged with their own distinct character. In Mexico, Caifanes absorbed gothic rock into a context shaped by Spanish-language rock and indigenous imagery; their 1988 debut remains a touchstone of Latin American gothic rock. In Japan, acts like Malice Mizer developed an ornate, theatrical style that bled directly into what became [Visual Kei](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Visual_Kei "Visual Kei"). Germany's *[Schwarze Szene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dark_Culture "Dark Culture")* developed into arguably the largest and most institutionalized goth scene in the world, eventually hosting the Wave-Gotik-Treffen in Leipzig, which draws tens of thousands of attendees annually.

### Genres

* [Post-Punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Post-Punk "Post-Punk") — The genre from which goth directly emerged. Post-punk rejected the blunt aggression of first-wave [punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Punk "Punk") in favor of atmosphere, rhythmic experimentation, and a willingness to incorporate funk, dub, and electronic textures. Joy Division, Siouxsie and the Banshees' tribal percussion, and early Bauhaus recordings all fall within post-punk before the gothic rock sound had fully separated from it. Not all post-punk is goth, but all early goth is post-punk.
* Gothic Rock — The original and defining genre, generally dated from Bauhaus' 1979 single "*Bela Lugosi's Dead*." It is characterized by flanged or chorused guitar lines, prominent melodic basslines, tribal drumming, and baritone or androgynous vocals. Second-wave acts like The Sisters of Mercy and Fields of the Nephilim pushed the sound toward a harder, more stadium-scaled rock, with drum machines replacing live percussion in many cases.
* [Deathrock](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Deathrock "Deathrock") — The American parallel to UK gothic rock, emerging from the West Coast punk scene in the early 1980s. Deathrock is faster and rawer than its British counterpart, with Christian Death's *Only Theatre of Pain* (1982) serving as its foundational text. The genre shares significant overlap with [Horror Punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Horror_Punk "Horror Punk"), and its fashion, consisting of deathhawk hairstyles, tattered clothing, and heavy white face paint, developed somewhat independently of the UK scene before the two converged by the mid-1980s. Key bands include Christian Death, 45 Grave, and Alien Sex Fiend.
* Dark Wave — A broader category that incorporates electronic instrumentation, specifically synthesizers and drum machines, into the gothic rock structure. The term is sometimes used loosely to cover the entire goth-adjacent electronic spectrum, though in more precise usage it refers to acts like Clan of Xymox, Switchblade Symphony, and Lebanon Hanover, whose sound sits between post-punk and synth-pop.
* Cold Wave — Originating in the late-1970s French and Belgian post-punk and [new wave](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/New_Wave "New Wave") scenes, cold wave strips the sound down to stark, minimal synthesizer arrangements and detached vocals, frequently inspired by the electronic innovations of the German band Kraftwerk (thus giving it common ground with [Minimal Wave](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Minimal_Wave "Minimal Wave") or [EBM](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/EBM "EBM")). Early acts like KaS Product, Martin Dupont, Asylum Party, and Marquis de Sade developed the style largely in parallel to the UK scene. Cold wave has experienced significant renewed interest since the 2000s, partly through the international success of Molchat Doma, whose 2018 album *Этажи* introduced the sound to a new audience through social media.
* Ethereal Wave — A subgenre built around heavily reverbed guitars, layered female vocals, and an atmospheric density that pushes toward the abstract. The Cocteau Twins are considered the primary originators, though their own connection to the goth scene was always tangential. The 4AD label became the primary home for ethereal wave, with This Mortal Coil functioning as a kind of house project for the label's roster. The genre later fed into shoegaze and dream pop.

### Fusion & Related Genres

As the subculture matured through the 1980s and 1990s, the "gothic" sound absorbed influences from adjacent traditions, producing hybrid genres that became fixtures of goth club nights and festivals without always sharing the original post-punk lineage.

* Neoclassical Dark Wave — From the mid-1980s onward, acts like Dead Can Dance and In The Nursery departed from rock instrumentation entirely in favor of orchestral arrangements, medieval and early music influences, and processed vocals. Dead Can Dance's output for 4AD across albums like *Spleen and Ideal* (1985) and *Within the Realm of a Dying Sun* (1987) is the clearest example of the style. This genre developed a close relationship with the [Romantic Goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Romantic_Goth "Romantic Goth") and [Victorian Goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Victorian_Goth "Victorian Goth") substyles.
* Gothic Metal — Emerging in the early 1990s from the British doom metal scene, gothic metal fused the downtuned guitar weight of acts like My Dying Bride and Paradise Lost with the atmosphere and melodrama of gothic rock. Theatre of Tragedy codified the "beauty and the beast" vocal pairing of operatic female vocals against male death growls; Type O Negative brought the sound to a mainstream audience through the commercial success of *Bloody Kisses* (1993). While gothic metal sits outside the post-punk lineage, it became a primary entry point into the subculture for many listeners in the 1990s and 2000s.
* [Gothabilly](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Gothabilly "Gothabilly") — A hybrid of gothic rock and [rockabilly](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rockabilly "Rockabilly"), drawing heavily from The Cramps' B-horror movie camp and [psychobilly](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Psychobilly "Psychobilly") energy. Where gothic rock tends toward atmosphere and melancholy, gothabilly trades on kitsch, humor, and upbeat tempos. Its aesthetic is associated with [pin-up](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Pin-up "Pin-up") fashion rendered in a dark palette.
* [Minimal Wave](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Minimal_Wave "Minimal Wave")/Minimal Synth — A DIY synthesizer-based genre that developed concurrently with post-punk, new wave, and [EBM](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/EBM "EBM") in the late 1970s and early 1980s. Although not a direct outgrowth of gothic rock, minimal wave shares enough atmospheric and subcultural overlap that it circulates regularly in goth contexts. Many minimal wave recordings were originally released in tiny pressings and were largely unknown until reissue labels like Minimal Wave Records began archiving them in the 2000s. The term itself was retroactively coined by Veronica Vasicka in 2005, based on Dutch magazines from the time that utilized various fragmented terms like "minimal electronics," "cold wave," and "new wave."
* Non-goth genres with fanbase overlaps — Goth clubs and festivals have always incorporated music genres that aren't strictly goth. [Electronic Body Music](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Electronic_Body_Music "Electronic Body Music") (EBM), while rooted in [industrial](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Industrial "Industrial") and early electronic music rather than post-punk and whose followers are sometimes called "[rivetheads](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rivethead "Rivethead")" or "EBM-heads," has shared dancefloor space with goth since the late 1980s and forms part of the broader *[Schwarze Szene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dark_Culture "Dark Culture")*. Dark Italo, a subgenre of [Italo Disco](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Italo_Disco "Italo Disco") with a colder and more melancholic production style, has seen growing presence in goth club nights. 1980s synth-pop also appears regularly, particularly at events with a broader nostalgic or [alternative](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Alternative "Alternative") focus.

#### Post-Punk
