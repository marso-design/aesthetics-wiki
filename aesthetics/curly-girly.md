# Curly Girly

_Also known as: Libby Lu Kid, Tween Diva_

**Curly Girly** is an aesthetic marketed at young girls, heavily inspired by "diva" motifs, as well as 2000s celebrity and pop culture. The name is derived from the curled serif fonts and shapes used in this style, and is ornate, playful, and frilly. Curly Girly may also be referred to as Libby Lu Kid or Tween Diva.

## History

The term was coined by Jack Grimes, and foundational research was conducted by Evan Collins, both of the [Consumer Aesthetics Research Institute](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/CARI "CARI").

Curly Girly is primarily influenced by the [Groovial](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Groovival "Groovival") aesthetic, mirroring its swirls, spirals, whirly patterns, and bold colors. Additional inspiration comes from the [Whimsicraft](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Whimsicraft "Whimsicraft") aesthetic, which contributed to the 'down-to-earthiness' of Curly Girly.

This aesthetic shares elements with many others that also originated in the 90s, including the broader design style [Global Village Coffeehouse](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Global_Village_Coffeehouse "Global Village Coffeehouse"). Additionally, similar girly diva motifs are present in [Shoe Diva](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Shoe_Diva "Shoe Diva"), although more "grown up" in comparison to the child-oriented Curly Girly.

In the years following Curly Girly's inception, new aesthetics that developed also share common elements with Curly Girly. These include the aspirational fame seen in [McBling](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/McBling "McBling") and Parisian motifs seen in [Parisian Girly.](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Parisian_Girly "Parisian Girly") The [Scene Tween](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene_Tween "Scene Tween") aesthetic emerged as a counter-culture to the mainstream girly styles.

## Visuals

The visual style of Curly Girly is characterized by flat, simple design and "curly" fonts and iconography, most notably the Curlz typeface created in 1995. Pinks, blues, and purples are often used colors, and hearts, stars, and flowers are common tertiary design elements. Young, trendy drawn characters are a hallmark of the style, especially in books and websites. Common themes include fashion, phones, and other traditionally "girly" activities.

## Music

The music of the tween demographic between roughly 1996 and 2005 was primarily defined by the **"golden era" of teen pop**, a commercially driven, highly polished genre that strategically targeted young audiences with catchy melodies, upbeat tempos, and relatable lyrics about young love and school life. The Curly Girly aesthetic was used in various marketing material and music videos for several artists throughout the late 90s and early 2000s.

## Media

Movies and TV shows linked to the **Curly Girly aesthetic** are typically live-action teen or "tween" comedies and dramas from the mid-1990s and early 2000s that emphasize themes of self-discovery, high school social dynamics, fashion, and romance. They visually align with the era's vibrant "[McBling](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/McBling "McBling")" or "Bold Pink" aesthetics. The films and shows feature iconic Y2K fashion, including bold colors, pastels (especially pink), trendy accessories, and "diva" hairstyles, all of which are central to the Curly Girly aesthetic. Fashion often serves as a plot device for character transformation or expression. Similarly to musical artists, the Curly Girly aesthetic was used in marketing material as well as set design for movies and TV shows during this time.

## Retailers

Several retailers from 1996 to 2005 defined the Y2K aesthetic, catering to teens and young adults with playful, ornate, and feminine styles. Key stores included dELiA\*s with its babydoll dresses and flare jeans, Claire's and their endless accessories options and Limited Too's clothes featuring glitter and bright colors. One of the most popular retailers associated with this aesthetic is Club Libby Lu, an experience based retailer marketed towards kids and tweens that provided makeovers, hosted birthday parties, and sold kid friendly beauty products. Girls could choose from a variety of packages for their makeover, complete with hair, makeup and nails. The store encapsulates much of what the Curly Girly aesthetic is all about, and Curly Girly has been previously been referred to as Libby Lu Kid by the [Consumer Aesthetic Research Institute.](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Consumer_Aesthetics_Research_Institute "Consumer Aesthetics Research Institute") Although Club Libby Lu closed all stores in 2009, Sweet & Sassy continues to operate today with a similar retail concept.
