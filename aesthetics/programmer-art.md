# Programmer Art

The term **"Programmer Art"** refers to an aesthetic and visual style that originates from software and video-games developpement, particulary in indie games, early sharewares and personnal projects from programmers.

Althought any art made by a programmer could be classified as *"programmer art"*, the term refers specifically to the characteristic unpolished style said to be the result of the lack of artistic skills among "logical-minded" coders. It often consists of placeholder elements "threw-together", with the intent to be remplaced with a more consistent artstyle later. This manifests with characters and objects made of primitive shapes with high-constrasting colors, a disregard of style cohesion and of consistent anatomy or perspective and an extensive use of default system fonts, palettes and sounds effects. In pixel-art, the criticized "pillow" shading style is sometimes used when sprites are not simply flat-colored. Historically, when computers were more limited in their capabilities, monochrome alphanumeric symbols were commonly used to denote the elements of the video-game.

As the indie game scene grew, a form of nostalgia started to appear for the unprofessionnal sharewares of the early internet, and the style started to be intentionally simulated by artists.

## Examples

* **Early (2009-) Minecraft**, especially the sprites made by Markus Persson (Notch) using Paint.NET v3.5.10. Many textures, like the iconic creeper or the stone and grass, were made using the noise filter tool on flat colors. Most earlier sprites were simple pixel art with a minimal directional shading drew with a limited palette of colors. When the game's classic textures were changed in 2019, Mojang released a texture-pack reintroducing the original textures called *"Programmer Art"*.

* **"ASCII/DF2014" Dwarf Fortress**. The simulation game first released in 2006 originally used simple sprites consisting of primary shapes or alphanumeric symbols to illustrate its open world environment and the characters interacting in it.

* **SkiFree**. Because the 1991 game was intended to run on a 386 PC with VGA display and was entierly made by a single developper, Chris Pirih, the visuals reflect these limitations. As such, most of the screen is white, functionning as the snowy plain your playable character skies on, and the sprites are straight-forward, with green pine trees and rainbow ramps coming in your way and a chasing yeti made of two grey circles with stick figures arms and legs attached to it.
