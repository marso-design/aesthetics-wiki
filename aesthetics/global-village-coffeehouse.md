# Global Village Coffeehouse

_Also known as: GVC, Millennium Global Village, Millennium-Era Urbane-Sophistication 'World Vibes' Aesthetic_

**Global Village Coffeehouse** (GVC) is a design aesthetic that was prevalent from the late 1980s to the early 2000s. It emerged as a reaction against the sleeker, tech-oriented styles of the mid-late 1980s, such as [Memphis Lite](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Memphis_Lite "Memphis Lite") and [Laser Grid](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Laser_Grid "Laser Grid"), reflecting a shift to a more environmental and nature-oriented style. The term was coined by Evan Collins of [CARI](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/CARI "CARI"). GVC was succeeded by the [Mission School](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Mission_School "Mission School") aesthetic in the late 1990s and early 2000s.

The GVC aesthetic is defined by its use of earth tones, rough and recycled textures, and motifs that blend stylized, "handcrafted" imagery with a hodgepodge of motifs appropriated from ancient, tribal, and Indigenous cultures. This created a warm, [bohemian](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Bohemian "Bohemian"), and worldly ambiance epitomized by the interior design and branding of chains like Starbucks, Panera Bread, and Barnes & Noble during the 1990s.

The style reflected the era's optimistic yet superficial embrace of globalization, "world music", and early internet culture, while also being criticized in retrospect for its "greenwashing" and "cultural appropriation".

## History

Global Village Coffeehouse was a sort of reaction against the computer/tech boom of the 1990s. It focused on "natural" themes and artificially crafted multiculturalism. This aesthetic emerged in the late 1980s, rose in the early 1990s, and peaked in popularity in the mid-1990s.

In late 1990s and early 2000s, GVC began to decline in popularity in favor of the [Y2K Futurism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Y2K_Futurism "Y2K Futurism") and [Gen X Soft Club](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Gen_X_Soft_Club "Gen X Soft Club") aesthetics, as the pendulum swung back towards futurism. Industrial and the [Mission School](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Mission_School "Mission School") aesthetics came to be perceived as "more authentic," though traces of GVC design continued into the 2000s. Although less popular nowadays, the GVC aesthetic still inspires some interior design, such in as Panera Bread, and occasionally sees use in food packaging.

## Visuals

The visual art style of Global Village Coffeehouse is characterized by its earth tones, detailed woodcut styling, and a use stereotypically "tribal" symbols. It overlaps in some aspects with the [Memphis Design](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Memphis_Design "Memphis Design") aesthetic, but with a much more rustic and warm color scheme and geometry, rather than sharp and bold colors. GVC borrows motifs from a wide array of art styles, such as [Lowbrow](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Lowbrow "Lowbrow") (also known as Pop Surrealism), [Impressionism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Impressionism "Impressionism"), [Cubism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Cubism "Cubism"), [Art Deco](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Art_Deco "Art Deco"), and [Fauvism](https://www.britannica.com/art/Fauvism), as well as artists like Chagall, Klimt, Matisse, Picasso.

### Elements

* Earth tones
* Airbrushed, "dirty" look
* Irregular borders and lines
* Colorful gradated backgrounds
* Horror vacui (a trait common to several other aesthetics of the time)
* A general dislike of expanses of flat colors, which were usually textured in some way
* Layering backgrounds that are misaligned with their line art (also shared with various styles of the era such as [Memphis Lite](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Memphis_Lite "Memphis Lite"), [Shoe Diva](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Shoe_Diva "Shoe Diva") and [Mission School](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Mission_School "Mission School"))
* Rough, hand-drawn look
  + Line art with gaps in it, as if sketched

## Music

Global Village Coffeehouse was by and large a graphic and interior design aesthetic; however, its welcoming, multicultural feel-good philosophy was associated with a broader zeitgeist that inspired music that could be considered GVC. Album art and music videos utilized heavily exoticized or appropriated indigenous imagery and new age elements. Much of this overlaps with what could be considered [Utopian Scholastic](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Utopian_Scholastic "Utopian Scholastic") music.

## Criticism

As a result of increased environmental awareness in the late 1980s, Global Vintage Coffeehouse used imagery inspired by environmentalism. This was meant to brand GVC as down-to-earth and authentic; however, these efforts were seen as insincere, because of its use as a marketing tactic. Similarly, the 1990s brought a fascination with global cultures and new age philosophy. Capitalizing on this led to what GVC has been criticized most for: its use of imagery taken from or inspired by different cultures. This resulted in some viewing it as "cultural appropriation" that commodified Indigenous and African cultural symbols and practices, with the purpose of using as a marketing tool to make money. The successor to GVC, [Mission School](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Mission_School "Mission School") is generally seen as more "authentic" in comparison.
