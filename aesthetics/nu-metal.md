# Nu-Metal

**Nu-Metal** is a subgenre of alternative metal that originated in the mid-1990s and achieved massive mainstream popularity in the late 1990s and early 2000s. The genre is defined by its fusion of [heavy metal](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Metal "Metal") and alternative rock with a wide range of other influences, most notably [hip-hop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hip-hop "Hip-hop"), [industrial](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Industrial "Industrial"), [funk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Funk "Funk"), and [grunge](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Grunge "Grunge").

Pioneering bands like Korn and Deftones established the genre's sound, which is often characterized by down-tuned guitars, rhythmic and groove-oriented riffs, and a vocal style that can range from rapping and melodic singing to aggressive screaming.

The genre is inextricably linked to the [Mallgoth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Mallgoth "Mallgoth") subculture, which served as its primary visual aesthetic. This fashion style, popularized in the [Y2K](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Y2K "Y2K") era, is defined by baggy clothing (especially JNCO jeans), oversized band hoodies, wallet chains, spiky hair, and facial piercings. Lyrically, Nu-Metal often explores themes of angst, anger, and personal pain, which resonated with a generation of suburban youth.

## History

During the 1980s, there was a domineering rule within music culture, and that was that hip-hop and rock couldn't mix; these two genres have had a very rocky relationship, only paying minor lip service to each other, with groups like the Beastie Boys toeing the line and that one collaboration between Run DMC and Aerosmith back in 1984 with *Walk This Way.* The real shift wouldn't occur until the late 1980s into the early 1990s.

#### The Rise of Rap Metal (1989-1993)

**Faith No More** is what most people think of when recalling the history of rap metal and nu metal. Before the term “nu-metal” existed, **Faith No More** (FNM) gave bands “philosophical permission” to experiment freely without rules. Their 1989 hit “Epic” was a watershed moment—a heavy metal track with a rap verse that dominated MTV. In 1991, thrash metal band **Anthrax** collaborated with **Public Enemy** on “Bring the Noise,” blending aggressive thrash riffs with Chuck D’s militant delivery and turntable scratching—elements that became nu-metal staples. Their joint tour that year mixed metalheads and hip-hop fans in the same mosh pits, breaking down racial and social barriers between the scenes.

In the early '90s, Seattle's [Grunge](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Grunge "Grunge") scene exploded, pushing alternative rock into the mainstream and ending the era of hair metal. While grunge spoke to suburban youth, rap resonated in inner cities. Amidst rising racial tensions, the fusion of alt-metal and hip-hop felt inevitable, led by Rage Against the Machine. If Faith No More provided the genre’s "weirdness," RATM added groove and political grit. Tom Morello’s "DJ-style" guitar and Zack de la Rocha’s fierce delivery proved a band could sound like hip-hop without turntables, making their 1992 debut the definitive bridge to nu-metal.

#### Korn and the Birth of "Nu" (1994-1998)

By 1994, all these threads converged. Korn released their self-titled debut, which many credit as the **first true nu-metal album**. They took the down-tuned "groove" of Pantera, the vocal gymnastics of FNM, and the raw vulnerability of grunge to create a sound that finally broke away from its predecessors. When Korn first appeared on the scene, their aesthetic was very bizarre and unusual for an alternative metal band, a mix of rave fashion, hip hop-inspired street wear and grunge. The guitars were super down-tuned and the music ran closer to eeriness than anyone could make of what this genre was supposed to be. Similar to grunge, nu metal gave a voice to misunderstood and angsty suburban white kids. Korn and others explored themes of emotional dread, anguish, and neglect common in the genre.

Their second album debuted at No. 3 on the Billboard 200, proving the “Bakersfield sound” wasn’t a fluke. The inaugural **Ozzfest** in 1996 became a key platform for rising bands like **Coal Chamber** and **Powerman 5000**. By 1997, debuts from **Limp Bizkit** (Three Dollar Bill, Y’all$), **Sevendust**, and **Papa Roach** showed the sound spreading beyond California. **Deftones** released Around the Fur, blending nu metal heaviness with atmospheric shoegaze to broaden the genre’s appeal. On August 18, 1998, Korn released *Follow the Leader*, which hit No. 1 and sold millions. It featured the hit “*Freak on a Leash*,” whose animated video became an MTV staple and is often called the “*Smells Like Teen Spirit*” of nu metal for packing nearly every genre staple into one track and video. Also in 1998, Korn launched the **Family Values** Tour, which mixed metal, hip-hop, and industrial acts (like Ice Cube and Rammstein), solidifying nu metal as a pan cultural movement.

#### Nu Metal Takeover (1999-2003)

Nothing would undermine nu metal’s takeover more than the disaster of Woodstock ’99. During Limp Bizkit’s performance of “Break Stuff,” hordes of young fans destroyed the arena. Captured in documentaries, the festival became synonymous with the genre’s aggressive energy boiling over into riots, fires, and “Lord of the Flies” conditions. Their set is often cited as the moment the genre’s angst turned into literal destruction, cementing Limp Bizkit as the poster children for the era’s chaotic peak. Multiple nu metal acts appeared on Total Request Live, with Fred Durst bridging the mainstream pop gap with alternative culture, especially among young teens—similar to what Eminem did for hip hop and Blink-182 for pop punk. By 1999 and 2000, the genre reached its cultural peak with major albums: Hybrid Theory (2000), Infest (2000), Chocolate Starfish and the Hot Dog Flavoured Water (2000), The Gift of Game (1999), Slipknot’s self-titled (1999), and White Pony (2000).

As it was starting to dominate the alternative space, nu metal was also featured in many Hollywood soundtracks. This is more pronounced during Hollywood's more action/sci-fi-oriented craze, also known as [90s Cool](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/90s_Cool "90s Cool"). Many artists from the nu metal scene were featured in many films from that era. This was due to the nature of the films, having a more cyberpunk/industrial tint, which also fit nu metal's vibe. The genre defined the "leather-trenchcoat" era of cinema. Key soundtracks included ***The Matrix*** (Deftones, Rammstein), ***Mission: Impossible 2*** (Limp Bizkit’s "Take a Look Around"), and ***Queen of the Damned*** (written entirely by Jonathan Davis). By 2003, with the release of **Linkin Park’s *Meteora*** and **Evanescence’s *Fallen***, the genre had reached its absolute commercial ceiling, but soon that'll all come to a close in the coming years.

#### Decline (2003-2004)

Nu-metal's decline was driven by many factors, but critics saw it as inevitable. Woodstock '99 served as a turning point, with its aggression giving critics ammunition to lambast the genre. Often dismissed as pretentious, the sound and aesthetic led many to lump nu-metal with post-grunge as "sellout" hardcore. While 2003 saw some success, it was the genre's last big year, as acts like **System of a Down** and **Deftones** distanced themselves by adopting the "alternative metal" label. Meanwhile, publications like *Rolling Stone* and *NME* championed "intellectual" rock from bands like **The Strokes**, **The Hives** and **The White Stripes,** making nu-metal's baggy-pants aesthetic appear primitive and uncool. [Third Wave Emo](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Emo "Emo") Bands like **My Chemical Romance** and **The Used** captured the "outcast" emotional energy of Nu Metal but traded the aggression for theatricality and "mall-goth" aesthetics. The radio-friendly side of Nu Metal was absorbed into the "butt-rock" era—bands or Post-Grunge like **Nickelback** and **3 Doors Down**—which took the distorted guitars but removed the hip-hop elements. It's safe to say this was a curated effort to "kick" nu metal out of the zeitgeist and contain it in the [Y2K](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Y2K "Y2K") era.

#### Nu-Renaissance (Late 2010s-Present)

The 2020s saw a slight revival and retrospective appreciation for the genre as part of all things [Y2K](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Y2K "Y2K"). People were quick to defend the genre and look to it as a major inspiration for modern rock and rap artists, especially those within [Emo Rap](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Emo_Rap "Emo Rap") of the late 2010s and early 2020s. Modern hyperpop also borrows heavily from the style and aesthetic of nu-metal. Artists like **Rina Sawayama**, **Grimes**, and **Poppy** have openly used Korn-style riffs and industrial textures in their pop tracks. **JNCOs** are back in a big way. The ultra-wide-leg silhouette is being paired with "baby tees" or oversized hoodies to create a dystopian, late-90s look. New York Yankees fitted caps, Adidas Campus shoes, and Cargo pants have made a huge fashion revival, especially with singers like Billie Eilish popularising the look. Digital creators are using **VHS filters**, **datamoshing**, and high-contrast "dirty" edits to evoke a sense of late-90s anxiety. It’s no longer seen as "bad quality"; it’s seen as a "vibe" that feels more authentic than the hyper-polished CGI of the 2010s.

## Visuals

The visual identity of Nu-Metal was as aggressive and eclectic as its sound, closely tied to the continued popularity of grunge. But it also mixes grunge, horror grunge, and corporate grunge elements to create a genuinely gritty, decaying, and unfiltered quality. Nu Metal visuals also included a ton of industrial reverie. It focuses on "impermanence" through visual motifs like rust spots, peeling lead paint, and eroded metal surfaces. In Nu-Metal media, this is the "dirty" high-contrast background that makes the neon or chrome elements pop. Modern interpretations of this style often describe digital art as a "noise field" where random pixels are modulated to look like rusted abstract landscapes. This mirrors the way Nu-Metal producers used "anti-musical" harsh mechanical sounds as a foundation for their visuals. **Atmospheric Sludge** is already a classic staple of traditional heavy metal, and this would often get mixed in with the horror grunge elements of nu-metal. Visuals are often "moodily lit" and monochrome, prioritising the raw character of exposed brick, concrete floors, and visible utilitarian piping. This was the standard backdrop for late 1990s - early 2000s "industrial gothic" and "[Factory Pomo](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Factory_Pomo "Factory Pomo")" aesthetics found in warehouse-set music videos.

### Album Art

Nu-Metal album art serves as a visual bridge between the **raw entropy of the industrial past** and the **sterile, high-tech anxiety** of the [Y2K Futurism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Y2K_Futurism "Y2K Futurism"). It often uses "Industrial Reverie" to create a setting of mechanical melancholy, where human angst is reflected in rusted machines and urban decay. Horror Grunge is often the main go-to for nu-metal album art, as it's meant to emphasis it's raw visuals through its music and marketing. While standard grunge was "messy," horror grunge (and by extension, Nu-Metal) leaned into **grotesque decay** and urban rot. This is shown with albums like *Kilgore - A Search for Reason (1998), Landmine Spring - Elephantine (2001)* and *Dry Kill Logic - The Darker Side of Nonsense (2001),* just to name a few. Since nu-metal had its heyday around the turn of the millennium, between 97-03, it would have major crossover with [Y2K Futurism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Y2K_Futurism "Y2K Futurism"). This style is a "polished dystopia." It uses clean, often monospaced or industrial fonts, barcodes, and "future-noir" elements that feel mass-produced yet cold. Deftones' White Pony is a prime example of the "clean" side of this aesthetic, using a stark, elegant, and minimal design to stand out from the typical "aggro" art of the era. The same goes for Evanescence's album Fallen, as it resembles a low-quality digital camera with various lomo effects thrown in as well.

### Music Video Visuals

Nu-metal is famous for its distinct music video styles, which evolved across two main eras. Starting in the mid-90s, the aesthetic mirrored **grunge** and **skate punk**, characterised by **fish-eye lenses**, low-contrast **VHS filtering**, and "camera shakes" synced to heavy riffs. These videos relied on a mix of **slow-motion** dramatics and hyper-fast "**twitch**" editing, perfectly captured in Deftones' video for "**My Own Summer (Shove It)**," which prominently features lomo effects and distorted framing. Nu Metal uses a lot more urban/industrial decay (abandoned warehouses, factories, and construction sites) mixed with cyberpunk, especially during the [Y2K](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Y2K "Y2K") era. From the late '90s into the new millennium, nu-metal videos used heavy colour grading—specifically high-contrast greens, greys, and "piss-yellow" tints—to create a grimy, urban feel. Early CGI from bands like Linkin Park and Mudvayne featured distorted faces and digital creatures, while stroboscopic lighting during breakdowns created a jittery, "stop-motion" effect on band movements. These intense white or yellow flashes were often synced to snare drums or turntables, frequently paired with "dying tape" effects like color bleeding and horizontal lines. ### Classical Nu-Metal Pose

The **Classical Nu-Metal Pose** (specifically the "Fisheye Reach") is the definitive visual signature of the nu-metal era, characterized by an aggressive, "in-your-face" energy directed at the viewer. Popularized by icons like **Fred Durst**, this pose utilizes extreme foreshortening to create a sense of kinetic tension. It is designed to make the artist appear as if they are physically breaking through the frame to grab the audience.

#### Key Characteristics

* **The Splayed Reach:** Both hands are thrust forward toward the lens with fingers spread wide and tense.
* **Fisheye Distortion:** The pose relies on a wide-angle lens to make the hands appear disproportionately large compared to the body.
* **The Aggro-Crouch:** A low, hunched-forward stance that centers the face between the reaching arms.
* **The Brow-Gaze:** The head is tilted down while the eyes look upward, creating an intense or "maddened" expression.

## Fashion

Nu-metal fashion is a high-contrast blend of late-'90s subcultures, merging the aggressive edge of metal with the functional aesthetics of streetwear. This "unholy collision" of styles prioritized comfort, volume, and an anti-establishment attitude. A key difference between the Nu-Metal and Metalhead fashions is Nu-Metal fashion borrowed more from [Hip-Hop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hip-Hop "Hip-Hop"), [Raver](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Raver "Raver"), [Skater](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Skater "Skater"), and [Grunge](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Grunge "Grunge") fashions of the time and often times crossed over into those other subcultures.

#### Raver

Raver culture had a major choke-hold on 90s youth, but primarily within fashion, the subcultures of alt rock and dance music would often pay lip service to each other, the same way it would for rap during the early parts of the 90s, with the UK scene often dressing in low-middle-class street entire this would then spill into the American suburbs. This provided nu-metal with its signature extreme silhouettes and technical elements. Brands like JNCO and Kikwear brought ultra-wide-leg "phat" pants to the scene, while the female side often adopted Buffalo platform shoes. Elements like fishnet shirts and neon or vibrant hair dyes were also direct carryovers from the rave scene.

#### Hip-Hop

Hip-hop began gaining mainstream success in the 90s, and it was the driving force behind youth-driven street fashion. Oversized street clothing started being marketed to more under-18s as the dominant way of looking "cool" or "street". Many brands flocked to marketing primarily to a hip-hop-listening audience, as most rappers would promote popular 90s streetwear. This would eventually blend into grunge, rap metal and ska punk, respectively. The heavy use of oversized athletic wear, including Kappa tracksuits, Adidas tracksuits and trainers, sports jerseys (MLB/NFL/NBA), and fitted baseball caps, stems directly from hip-hop. This influence is most famously associated with bands like Korn and Limp Bizkit, who integrated sneakers, baggy denim, and wallet chains into a new kind of "urban metal" look.

#### Skate Punk

Another subculture, nu-metal, overlaps with skaters in terms of style, as this was another style that had a major uptick in mainstream culture of the 90s into the 00s, with the growing success of Tony Hawk and the X Games, many 90s brands and mega corporations infused "extreme" and "edgy" as the new way of rebellion against the system, skaters were a major part of this especially when it came to how they dressed. Skater culture contributed functional footwear and relaxed fits. Chunky skate shoes from brands like Osiris (specifically the D3 model), DC, Etnies, and Vans became the standard footwear for the genre. Skater staples like Dickies pants and cargo shorts were also popularised by figures like Fred Durst.

#### Grunge

Nu-metal inherited the "messy" and angst-driven visual style of grunge, including flannels, beanies, and a general layer-heavy approach. This influence is often seen in more stripped-down, gritty visuals and the DIY, non-conformist ethos. Grunge visualises raw authenticity through muted colours and "dirty" cardigans. Nu-metal evolved this into "Industrial Reverie."

#### The "Mall Goth" Connection

Hot Topic acted as the primary bridge, selling both nu-metal band merchandise and gothic accessories in one location. This was the central hub of the aesthetic, as many who were part of the goth subculture (or in this case, mall goth) listened to heavy music. The band Kittie often incorporated Mall Goth fashion and aesthetics heavily within their music, with other bands who were part of nu-metal dressing more closely to Mall Goth than the standard nu metal get-up. Nu-metal fans often adopted dark makeup, including black eyeliner (guyliner), dark nail polish, and fingerless leather gloves—trends shared with the burgeoning mall goth scene.

## Music

Nu-Metal music tends to take the metal sound and strip it down, taking inspiration from Grunge before it (which has lead to a lot of bands in the Nu-Metal genre getting the label "Post-Grunge"), and some bands incorporating elements of Hard Rock, Funk, Hip-Hop, and Industrial sounds, the songs often playing on pure [shock factor](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Shock_factor "Shock factor") or raw emotion with songs about drug use, self-harm, depression, sex, death, and other dark, macabre, or otherwise controversial subjects. While nu-metal had a large following of fans and listeners, some critics and metal purists of the time disdained the genre for its strong departure from many traditions of metal music and its association with the Jock/Frat stereotype. Some purists derided Nu-Metal with terms like "Mallcore" or "Aggro Metal". Nu-Metal has various styles clumped together that would shape the sub-genre. I'll list them below.

#### The Rap Style

This style is derived from the rap metal genre, most notably from the early 90s. It's inspired by groups like Clawfinger, Faith No More and Helmet. This element features rhythmic, spoken-word, or rapped verses delivered over heavy, distorted riffs. DJs are frequently integrated to add turntable scratching and digital sampling.

#### Industrial-Metal Hybrid

This side incorporates mechanical textures, synthesisers, and electronic loops to create a cold, dystopian atmosphere known as "Industrial Reverie". It often highlights themes of urban decay and man-versus-machine angst. This runs closer to [Y2K](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Y2K "Y2K") due to its themes on technology and heavy severance.

#### Post-Grunge Adjacent

A lot of post-grunge and nu metal were often lumped together, considering both genres were targeted during their cultural peak. Acting as a "missing link," this style prioritises radio-friendly melodies and personal vulnerability over rap or electronics. These bands often used standard rock structures but maintained nu-metal's heavy, down-tuned chug and aggressive screaming in the choruses.

#### Hardcore Elements

Their nu-metal bands that often toed the line between hardcore and nu-metal, especially bands like Slipknot and Soulfly. These elements would often include '90s hardcore punk and groove metal; this side focuses on pure aggression and high-energy breakdowns. It often features more intense screaming or growling and more aggressive, less melodic delivery.

A key aspect of nu-metal music videos was their addition to Total Request Live, a network chain of MTV that was mostly known for hosting post-teen pop era music videos with the occasional rap. Nu metal acts were the only heavy artists consistently competing with pop juggernauts like Britney Spears and NSYNC. Fans would call in relentlessly to ensure bands like Limp Bizkit or Korn stayed in the top 10, often reaching the coveted #1 spot. As the frontman of Limp Bizkit, Fred Durst became the face of this crossover. He was a frequent guest in Times Square, famously hanging out with Britney Spears and eventually being appointed Senior Vice President of A&R at Interscope Records.
