**Jejemon** is a youth subculture and internet aesthetic originating from the Philippines that emerged during the late 2000s and reached its peak in the early 2010s. It is characterized by its distinctive writing style, known as *Jejenese*, as well as a fashion style combining elements of [scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene"), [swag](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Swag "Swag"), [hip-hop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hip-hop "Hip-hop"), and streetwear. The subculture was primarily associated with mobile texting, Friendster, Facebook, and Yahoo! Messenger, becoming one of the most recognizable internet phenomena in the Philippines during the period.

According to Urban Dictionary, a Jejemon is a person "who has managed to subvert the English language to the point of incomprehensibility." The *Philippine Daily Inquirer* described Jejemons as a "new breed of hipster who have developed not only their own language and written text but also their own subculture and fashion."

## History

### Late 2000s–Early 2010s

Jejemon culture emerged in the Philippines during the late 2000s alongside the widespread use of affordable mobile phones, SMS texting, and social networking websites such as Friendster, Facebook, and Yahoo! Messenger. The subculture initially became known for its unconventional writing style, which incorporated leetspeak, alternating capitalization, and decorative punctuation.

As the term gained popularity, "Jejemon" evolved beyond its linguistic origins into a broader youth subculture. Participants adopted distinctive fashion inspired by scene, hip-hop, emo, and streetwear trends while embracing heavily edited profile pictures, personalized social media pages, and remix-oriented music. By the early 2010s, the phenomenon had become a widespread subject of internet memes, television programs, and mainstream media coverage.

Although its popularity declined throughout the mid-2010s, Jejemon remains a reminiscent of early Filipino internet culture and is frequently revisited through nostalgia.

## Etymology

The word Jejemon supposedly originated from online users' penchant to type in "hehehe" as "jejeje", either because "jeje" is derived from Spanish usage of "j", whose speakers denote the interjection as laughter, or because the letters "h" and "j" are beside each other, and that it is appended by "-mon" that came from the Japanese anime Pokémon, with "-mon" meant as "monster," hence "jeje monsters."

## Fashion

Jejemon fashion mostly drew inspiration from several youth fashion trends popular during the late 2000s and early 2010s, particularly [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene"), [hip-hop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hip-Hop "Hip-Hop"), streetwear, [Y2K](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Y2K "Y2K") fashion, and contemporary Filipino celebrity styles.

Male Jejemons commonly wore fitted or backward flat-brim caps, basketball jerseys, oversized graphic T-shirts, flannel shirts, skinny jeans, cargo shorts, colorful sneakers, ripped jeans, bandanas, and studded belts. Hairstyles frequently featured bedheads, sideburn fade, side-swept bangs styled with hair wax or gel or spiky hairs, often inspired by scene and [emo](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Emo "Emo") hairstyles while incorporating elements of hip-hop fashion.

Female Jejemons typically adopted styles combining elements of [McBling](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/McBling "McBling"), Scene, and Y2K fashion, with influences from Filipino teen celebrities such as Kathryn Bernardo, Liza Soberano, Julia Montes, and Kim Chiu during the period aswell. Common hairstyles included long layered hair with side-swept bangs, teased volume, hair bows, headbands, and brightly colored hair accessories. Clothing often consisted of fitted graphic T-shirts, plaid shirts, skinny jeans, leggings, shorts, skirts, colorful sneakers, ballet flats, oversized handbags, charm bracelets, and large sunglasses. Bright colors, rhinestone embellishments, cartoon prints, and heart motifs were also common.

Both male and female Jejemons frequently accessorized with friendship bracelets, silicone wristbands, oversized watches, rings, necklaces, and personalized items. Their appearance was often complemented by heavily edited photographs featuring glow effects, sparkles, hearts, lens flares, decorative borders, and collage-style layouts, reflecting the visual style of early Philippine social media.

## Lingo

The sociolect associated with Jejemons is known as *Jejenese*. It is primarily derived from Taglish, though it may also incorporate Bisaya and other Philippine languages depending on the region. Jejenese is characterized by the use of leetspeak, camel case, and the frequent addition of the letters H, X, and Z. Its orthography often features superfluous or silent letters, along with intentionally unconventional spelling. The writing style is distinguished by alternating capitalization, substitutions of letters with numbers, repeated characters, and decorative punctuation. Examples include "JeJeM0N pOh!!", "mUsT4H nAh,?", or "iM1SsYuUh". The writing system is informally referred to as the *[Jejebet](http://jejemonjejemon.blogspot.com/p/jejebet-jejemon-alphabet.html)*. Its vocabulary and expressions also share similarities with *[Swardspeak](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swardspeak)* (also known as *Bekimon*). A text conversion tool known as the [Jejenator (Jejenese Generator)](https://jejenator.netlify.app/) can be used to convert standard text into the Jejenese writing style.

## Online culture

Jejemon culture was closely associated with early Philippine social media and mobile communication. Facebook profile customization, Friendster testimonials, Yahoo! Messenger display names, chain messages, and heavily edited profile pictures were common aspects of the aesthetic. Decorative text, glitter graphics, colorful image effects, and collage-style photo editing were frequently used to personalize online identities.

## Music

Jejemon music generally consisted of mainstream Filipino pop, hip-hop, rap, novelty songs, dance remixes, and electronic dance music. Budots became particularly associated with the subculture due to its popularity among Filipino youth during the early 2010s. Love songs, rap ballads, and remix culture were also prominent.
