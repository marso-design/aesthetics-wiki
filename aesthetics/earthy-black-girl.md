# Earthy Black Girl

_Also known as: Black Girl Boho_

**Earthy Black Girl** is an Afrocentric, Neo Soul revival aesthetic that emerged on TikTok in 2023. While this aesthetic is primarily *inspired* by the African-American reclamation movement of West African spirituality, it is deeply tied to new age spirituality, connection to the Earth, and Neo Soul music and culture.

Unlike the [Hippie](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hippie "Hippie") and [Boho-Chic](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Boho-Chic "Boho-Chic") aesthetics, for which it is often confused, Earthy Black Girl does not appropriate West African cultural traditions; instead, it uses them with curiosity and respect to reconnect Black Americans with cultures institutionally removed from them through Slavery. Hippie and [Bohemian](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Bohemian "Bohemian") styles also more heavily take inspiration from the Roma people of Europe, another oppressed minority, though modern Boho-Chic specifically does take inspiration from African fashion as well.

It is related to [Ethnochic](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Ethno-Chic "Ethno-Chic"), new age spirituality and mysticism, and more recently, to the Black Nouveau interior design movement.

The closest aesthetic that is not tied to the Black identity is [Boho-Chic](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Boho-Chic "Boho-Chic"). Many south Asian women, particularly Indian women, resonate with this aesthetic due to similar looks and shared spiritual beliefs.

## History

Neo Soul is the musical movement from the early 1990s that Earthy Black Girl gets its fashion, music, and mystical beliefs from. An alternative to the R&B music that was popular at the time, Neo Soul evolved from both R&B and soul, combining elements of funk, hip-hop, rock and jazz with "conscious" lyrics to create something wholly new. Erykah Badu was a pioneer of this genre, and popularized the mystic and spiritual aspects to it in 1997 with her album *Baduizm.* Donning her now-iconic headwraps, African-inspired jewelry and colors, and natural hair, Badu completed the Neo Soul transformation from music genre to movement, which (alongside progressive hip-hop) continues to inspire Earthy Black Girl and similar Black aesthetics to this day.

Both Earthy Black Girl and Neo Soul have their roots in Black progressive movements, and this is evident through the fashion. In the 1700s, headwraps were a symbol of high status in Sub-Saharan Africa, and could communicate a woman's culture and family practices through design alone. Headwraps had the bonus of being added protection against the sun. When Slavery began in the mid-1700s, enslaved women were forced to wear headwraps as a symbol of their enslavement. They would use headwraps to protect their scalps and hair from the burning sun as they worked outside for up to 14 hours a day. The headwrap became a signifier of slavery this way, but also the beginnings of African-American cultural identity, which was only just emerging. In the late 1700s, a law was passed in New Orleans that required black women to wear their hair covered with headwraps. Once Slavery was abolished, headwraps became a sign of mockery and caricature. For all of these reasons, Black Americans began to abandon the headwrap.

In the 1960s and 70s, the Black Power movement encouraged Black Americans to embrace their racial and cultural identity and fight against the reigning power that tried to diminish and devalue them. Natural hair, primarily the afro, became a sign of political resistance. In the 1990s, Erykah Badu, Lauryn Hill and other Neo Soul artists began to use the headwrap to connect to their roots and reclaim the symbol for the culture, refusing to let its history control the entire Black narrative.

Another fashion aspect that connects Earthy Black Girl to Black American progressiveness is yellow gold jewelry. Historically, gold is passed down through generations in families of African cultures. Gold connects directly to Egypt, a land rich in the mineral, and to its Pharaohs as a status symbol, and Mansa Musa, the richest man to ever live, was an African man with plenty of it. Yellow gold specifically has always been tied to the Black identity -- men wear gold chains and grills, women wear gold bangles and hoop earrings, and African art is often adorned with gold, both the material and the color. Yellow gold was deemed as "ghetto" due to this association with Blackness, and white gold was considered the standard of high class luxury. The Earthy Black Girl aesthetic reclaims this too, returning yellow gold jewelry back to its historically rich roots, bringing it back into the cultural consciousness as a status symbol and as a way to connect to their African history.

This aesthetic also incorporates new age spirituality and esotericism. Tarot cards, astrology, crystals and manifestation are used alongside African and Caribbean religious practices, such as Hoodoo, Voodoo, Kemetism, Ifá and more. This is part of a larger Black movement, one that attempts to decolonize their spiritual beliefs away from Christianity by mixing ancient religious traditions with modern ones.

When Earthy Black Girl emerged in the early 2020s, it was a way for Black women to fight against the cultural assimilation white America had forced upon them, and to reclaim their own identity, culture, and history. Waist beads, Egyptian symbolism, and Black hairstyles are all ways to reach back to their African ancestors, despite the physical and temporal distance caused by Slavery. Earthy Black Girl is just another aspect of Black American progression, fighting to maintain their truth and identity in a country that has only ever wanted to erase those things.

## Visuals

The visuals of Earthy Black Girl are steeped in Black history and significance. The color palette is earthy -- **browns, greens, oranges, rusty reds and terracotta** -- but also incorporates the **colors of Pan-African flags: yellow and gold, white and black, and sometimes blue**. **Traditional African patterns** are seen within this aesthetic and vary from all over the continent. From the **Kente design** of Ghana and the **Ankara print** of the greater West African region, to the **Bogolan pattern** of Mali -- though they tend to be from West Africa, as that is what is the most popularized overseas, prints and fabrics from the entire continent are prevalent here. **Tie-dye, paisley and stripes** are also common prints. You will often see **beads and shells** used in fashion here, and even as decoration for some hairstyles. **Gold** is a common finish for everything, be it jewelry or cloth.

**Adinkra and Kemetic/Egyptian symbols** are used in fabric prints and jewelry. Common ones are the **Gye Nyame, the Sankofa bird and heart, the Ankh, the feather of Ma'at and the eye of Horus**. You will also see New Age Spirituality represented in the same way, usually through **chakras, moon phases, sacred geometry (especially the lotus flower), the Nazar, and Tarot references**.

## Fashion

As this is an aesthetic made by and for women, the fashion for it is hyperfeminine. Since it is **Afrocentric**, it relies heavily on both Black American and African popular cultures. For example, you will see lots of beading and shells, but rarely any bows or ribbons despite being hyperfeminine -- this is due to the cultural differences between Black and white Americans. Africa is hot and humid (Central West Africa) or hot and dry (North/South Africa), so the garments here are **light and airy**, and **show a decent amount of skin** -- African cultures do not believe that showing your arms, midriff, back or legs are inappropriate like many other cultures do, and so the clothing is **less conservative** than some. **Natural, breathable fabrics** are important to this aesthetic -- **hemp, linen, cotton and jute** are common, as are **crochet and knits**, and sometimes even **lace**. **Rayon and silk** are used for headwraps, as it reduces friction against the hair and thus, prevents breakage. Silk also preserves natural oils and aids in hair moisture and hydration. **Jewelry is typically layered**, be it necklaces, bracelets, or earrings, and usually incorporate **beads, small shells**, **gold** or organic gemstones like **turquoise, jade, jasper,** and **amber.**

### Tops

* Tank tops, camisoles, and bandeau tops
* Loose, knit or crochet cardigans, often cropped
* Crop tops
* Tie-front tops and blouses
* Off-the-shoulder tops
* Flared sleeves, bell sleeves, angel sleeves

### Bottoms

* Long, flowing midi and maxi skirts
* Patchwork and tiered skirts
* Cargo mini shorts
* Cargo pants
* Flared denim jeans

### Dresses

* Rompers, jumpsuits and playsuits, particularly loose-fitted and non-restrictive ones
* Sundresses (Black Americans refer to bodycon maxi dresses as sundresses; the term applies to a different garment for white Americans)

### Shoes

* Sandals, flip-flops, and slides
* Clogs
* Go-go boots
* Barefoot

### Accessories

* Headwraps, head scarves, bandanas and headbands
* Hoop and dangly gold and jade earrings
* Large golden bangles
* Beaded bracelets, sometimes with cowrie, puka, or other types of shells
* Chain belts, shield belts
* Waist beads
* Arm cuffs
* Hair/braid cuffs, braid charms, coils and hair rings
* Tattoos and henna

### Hair, Makeup and Beauty

* Dark, earthy, and warm toned makeup
* Hair worn in any textured hairstyle (natural coils and curls, afros, puffs, pineapple updo), braids (box braids, bubble braids, boho braids, micro braids, Fulani braids), locs (goddess locs, bohemian locs, micro locs, sister locs, butterfly locs, dreads) and twists (passion twists, Marley twists, two-strand twists, Senegalese twists). Generally any Black hairstyle without using heat or a relaxer to alter the texture
* Shea butter, cocoa butter (lotions, balms, oils)
* Scents like vanilla, Egyptian musk, patchouli, brown sugar, and cocoa butter
