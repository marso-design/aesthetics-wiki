**Tropicália** or **Tropicalism** was a artistic, political, cultural and social movement that originated in Brazil and became popular in 1970s and 1980s during the Brazilian Military Dictatorship era. The goal of Tropicalism was to oppose traditionalism disseminated by the military and the elite, uniting Brazilian cultural elements with foreign genres.

## History

### Origins (1920s)

Tropicalist aesthetics are strongly influenced by Brazilian modernism from the early 20th century. Many Brazilian artists and intellectuals lived in Europe after the First World War, absorbing avant-garde ideas and aesthetics such as [Cubism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Cubism "Cubism"), [Dadaism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dadaism "Dadaism"), [Futurism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Futurism "Futurism"), [Expressionism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Expressionism "Expressionism"), and [Surrealism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Surrealism "Surrealism"). Through these intellectuals, the effervescence of innovative aesthetics and European social ideologies arrived in Brazil with the intention of breaking with traditionalism and classicism and renewing Brazilian aesthetics.

The artistic and literary works of the Brazilian modernist period are directly influenced by the European avant-garde, but also adopt a regional and national identity specific to Brazil and Latin America. In 1922, the first major modernist artistic event was organized. The Modern Art Week was organized by leading figures of local modernism in order to exhibit avant-garde art that represented Brazilian society in the arts. Among the artists presented were the works of Tarsila do Amaral representing Brazilian daily life through Cubism and Surrealism, the regionalist poetry of Oswald de Andrade and Mario de Andrade, and musical concertos by Heitor Villa-Lobos.

Heitor's music was strongly influenced by Bach's style, but the Brazilian musician decided to incorporate instruments of local indigenous origin into the orchestra. The event was marked by its accessibility to the poorest and its representation in the arts. When Heitor Villa-Lobos entered the stage to conduct the Orchestra, he wore a sandal on one foot and a dress shoe on the other, aiming to represent the modernist characteristic of representing all of Brazilian society through arts considered erudite and elitist, a fact that caused strangeness among members of the elite who visited the art exhibition. Representation, innovation, and provocation of the elite through art, literature, and music are elements that would most directly influence the Tropicália movement.
