# Necrotrap

[Sensitive Content Notice](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Aesthetics_Wiki:Content_Warning_Policy "Aesthetics Wiki:Content Warning Policy") ⚠️

The following article contains and discusses content that may be distressing to some readers.  
**Reason for Warning:** This article discusses an aesthetic centered on stylized themes of warfare, geopolitical conflict, and fictional terrorism. While the imagery draws from real-world locations (e.g., Iraq and Afghanistan) and paramilitary motifs that may be sensitive to some readers, it is presented in a fictionalized, archival context intended for world-building and shock value. Reader discretion is advised.

**Necrotrap** is an internet-based music genre and aggressive derivative of [Trap Metal](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Trap_Metal "Trap Metal") that emerged from the [SoundCloud Rap](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/SoundCloud_Rap "SoundCloud Rap") underground in the early 2020s. The genre is built around heavily distorted 808 basslines with aggressive vocal delivery influenced by metal, incorporating Drum and Bass and Dubstep elements primarily in kick patterns and mixing approach.

The genre's production centers on 808 basslines pushed through heavy saturation and distortion to achieve maximum loudness while maintaining controlled mixing standards. Visually, the movement uses degraded low-fidelity imagery and cyberwarfare motifs to simulate an immersive and combat-focused atmosphere. While Necrotrap originated with the collective NECROSOCIETY, the aesthetic has since evolved into a decentralized online music movement focused on the collision of modern warfare, horror/gore, and hacker culture.

## History

Necrotrap originated in the early 2020s as a direct reaction to the commercialization and perceived stagnation of the mainstream [Trap Metal](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Trap_Metal "Trap Metal") scene. Artists like Scarlxrd and collectives such as Spider Gang had popularized the fusion of heavy metal and rap, but early proponents of Necrotrap felt the sound had become formulaic and repetitive.

Contrary to early misconceptions attributing the term solely to Gorejit, the Necrotrap sound crystallized in 2021 through the formation of the NECROSOCIETY collective. Co-founded by HXLX, Gorejit, and Tsaruicide, the group did not initially set out to "invent" a named genre; rather, the style emerged organically as they experimented with fusing the aggression of metal, heavy trap basslines, and electronic production techniques that deviated from the standard Trap Metal formula of the time.

The three co-founders—HXLX, Gorejit, and Tsaruicide—each contributed to shaping the genre's early sound and direction, with HXLX and Tsaruicide continuing to manage the collective to this day. As the roster grew to include new members, the "Necrotrap" descriptor was adopted to categorize their output, a sound born from collaborative experimentation rather than a singular coinage event.

While the most prominent artists remain associated with the NECROSOCIETY and IRAQ-A-HOLICS collectives, the genre has transitioned into a decentralized movement. Numerous unaffiliated artists on SoundCloud (e.g., Dollscar, Torturechainn, M44A1, HYPNXSIS, just to cite a few) have adopted the "Necrotrap" label and its associated visual aesthetic, signaling the style's evolution from a collective-specific sound into a broader internet music movement. This is also evidenced by numerous fanmade Necrotrap production tutorials and "type beats" avaiable on YouTube.

## Visuals

Necrotrap's visual aesthetic is built around deliberately degraded image processing. Unlike the high-contrast or anime-centric visuals common in mainstream [Trap Metal](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Trap_Metal "Trap Metal"), the genre uses grain, noise, sharpening, and stretching to create an intentionally low quality look. The style draws from three main visual territories: military and tactical imagery, including guns, vests, and combat settings; [horror](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Horror "Horror") and slasher film elements including gore, body horror, and occult imagery; and hacking and cyberwarfare visuals. The overall look is meant to feel raw and aggressive, not clean or polished.

Necrotrap's reliance on tactical equipment, weapon schematics, and "Kill-Cam" visual effects is heavily influenced by first-person shooter (FPS) gaming culture and military simulation media. Many of the transgressive themes and titles are rooted in the 'edgy' humor and roleplay prevalent in 2010s-era gaming lobbies (such as *Call of Duty* or *Counter-Strike*), where militaristic jargon is recontextualized as a fictional persona rather than an actual political statement.

During the genre's early days (circa 2020-2022), the aesthetic also incorporated clay figures created by artist bezbique depicting mutilated or armed avatars. While this remains a legacy motif, the contemporary style has evolved toward darker and more grounded imagery across all three visual themes.

## Music

### Characteristics

#### Vocals

Vocal delivery in Necrotrap is aggressive and raw, drawing its style primarily from metal influence. The core techniques include yelling, screaming, vocal frying, whispered delivery, and heavily distorted layering. Vocals are not clean or overproduced — the raw quality is intentional and is one of the biggest defining elements of the genre alongside the 808s and percussion patterns.

Unlike traditional rap flows, vocals serve as a primary element of every track. While heavily processed and sometimes layered, they remain a central focus rather than background texture.

#### Production

The genre's production is described as controlled distortion. Producers push 808 basslines to their limit using saturation plugins to achieve maximum loudness and texture without the unwanted artifacts of accidental clipping. Percussion patterns frequently deviate from standard trap arrangements, incorporating elements from Dubstep and Drum and Bass (DnB), specifically in kick patterns and mixing approach. Reese bass textures and industrial snares appear occasionally but are not a defining element of the sound.

Atmospheric elements contribute to the genre's "warfare" aesthetic. Tracks often feature samples from military shooters (e.g., *Call of Duty*), action films, and combat documentaries used as intros, outros, or ambient texture. While some producers utilize Memphis rap samples (occasionally referred to as "Necrophonk"), the overall sound is meant to hit like a soundtrack from a slasher film or a violent video game.

## Criticism

Necrotrap has been criticized for its reliance on paramilitary imagery and violence associated with real-world conflicts. The genre's aesthetic is built on high-intensity [shock value](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Shock_value "Shock value") imagery, which critics argue can trivialize geopolitical trauma by presenting it through an "edgy" or gamified lens.

A common misconception and stereotype is that the genre is a product of "white suburban American teenagers." However, the demographic of major collectives like NECROSOCIETY includes predominantly European adults. This has not alleviated concerns regarding cultural appropriation, as critics argue that the use of warzone footage and specific religious terminology by artists disconnected from those conflicts reduces real-world suffering to a "tactical aesthetic."

### Artist Response

In response to allegations of trivializing violence, members of the NECROSOCIETY collective have described the aesthetic as a deliberate reflection of "generational desensitization" to digital warfare.

According to the collective, the use of militaristic and combat imagery is not intended as a political endorsement but rather as an extension of the [horror](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Horror "Horror") genre. They argue that just as audiences consume slasher films for the "thrill" of fictionalized death, Necrotrap listeners process the horror of modern warfare through a similar lens of detachment and adrenaline. The collective posits that the imagery is "animalistic and sketchy on purpose" to evoke a specific visceral reaction, functioning as an immersive world-building exercise rather than a commentary on specific geopolitical events.
