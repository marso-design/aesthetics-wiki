# Russian 2K17

_Also known as: Give me back my 2k17_

**Russian 2K17** (also known as **Vernite Moy 2017** / **Bring Back My 2017**) is a Russian hybrid digital-everyday aesthetic from 2016–2018, peaking in 2017, combining new school music, comfortable street style, post-irony, and the everyday realities of post-Soviet suburbs, such as nighttime strolls, apartment building entrances, panel blocks, house parties, school hallways, abandoned buildings, and neon signs. The aesthetic is primarily associated with Russian Gen Z and is centered on VKontakte (VK).

## History

Russian 2K17 emerged around 2016 alongside a wave of new musical styles (Russian deep house, cloud rap, and a loosely defined "new wave" scene) circulating primarily through VK. Although partially inspired by Tumblr-era melancholy, the aesthetic wasn't a direct adaptation: it localized what it borrowed. Tumblr's introspective sadness became the culture of "sad saves"; [Vaporwave](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Vaporwave "Vaporwave")'s urban detachment was refracted through the specific texture of prefabricated Soviet panel housing. Artists like Face and Элджей gave the [Sadboi](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Sadboi "Sadboi") adjacent mood an uniquely Russian personality.

The aesthetic's retrospective name, "*Vernite Moy 2017*" ("Bring Back My 2017"), reflects how quickly it was recognized as a distinct cultural moment, one tied not just to a musical era but to a specific way of inhabiting and photographing everyday life.

### VK and Sohranënki Culture

To understand Russian 2K17, VK (VKontakte) is inseparable from it. For Eastern European youth, VK was a combined social network and music platform, allowing users to upload and stream audio freely without copyright restrictions. This made it the primary site for music discovery, rare leaks, and underground releases, and the absolute center of teenage cultural life.

The most characteristic expression of this was the *Sohranënki* (Сохранёнки) — the public "Saved Photos" album on a user's VK profile. Unlike Instagram, which rewarded polished self-presentation, the Sohranënki was a raw, accumulative visual diary: low-resolution urban photos, depressive quotes, screenshots of song lyrics, and post-ironic memes coexisted without hierarchy. Teenagers browsed each other's saved folders as a way of gauging compatibility, forming friendships based on shared visual and emotional sensibilities.

## Visuals

Russian 2K17 is characterized by the texture of post-Soviet urban life photographed at night or in low light. A typical image is grainy and high-contrast, often taken with a flash on a cheap phone camera: the entrance hall of a panel apartment block, a lit window in a residential tower, a neon sign reflected in wet pavement. Yellow streetlight and cold blue shadow are the dominant ambient tones, occasionally broken by pale pink or raspberry; colors associated with the era's music aesthetic as much as its imagery.

Interiors are equally important. Teenage bedrooms appear frequently: Soviet-era carpets on walls, string lights or garlands, a laptop open on a bed. Branded clothing from Thrasher, Adidas, and Vans appears not as aspiration but as casual background detail. Text is woven into the aesthetic too, in the form of short captions and song lyric screenshots overlaid on images, written in a way that is simultaneously sincere and self-aware.

## Philosophy

The aesthetic is characterized by both performed loneliness and collective identity, as well as the merging of local and global. The romanticization of uncertainty (like cigarettes, alcohol, late nights, and unresolved emotion) carried the same post-ironic distance that characterized the music scene it grew alongside. Sincerity was present but always slightly undercut, a generational reflex. From a broader cultural standpoint, the aesthetic reflects the particular condition of growing up in post-Soviet Russia: absorbing global internet trends while remaining rooted in a physical and social environment that those trends hadn't accounted for.
