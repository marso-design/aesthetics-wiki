# Huang-mao

_Also known as: Huang-mao_

**Yellow Hair** or **Huang-mao** (黄毛) is a pejorative term used to describe young street layabouts who dropped out of school at an early age, shun honest work, and dress ostentatiously. The stereotype gets its name from the typical look of cheaply bleached blonde hair.

The image of the yellow hair is a common look for low-ranking gang members in Hong Kong triad films, while in Mainland China it has became a commonly used trope for shaping rebellious and marginalized young characters.

Note that influenced by Japanese anime, people often teases that while these people have no steady jobs, they are adept at delivering emotional value and possess strong social skills, which makes them popular with young girls who are naive and inexperienced in life. This is rarely depicted in the domestic film industry.

## History

*Needs Work & Verification*

### In Hong Kong

Origin is unclear, but in classic 1980s Hong Kong triad films such as *A Better Tomorrow* (1986) and *Prison on Fire* (1987), yellow hairstyles were already a common look for low-ranking gang members. The *Young and Dangerous* Film Series (From 1996 onwards) made the combination of yellow hair, loud patterned shirts and street loitering a widely recognized stereotype of hooligans across all Chinese-speaking regions.

Young [Ah Beng](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Ah_Beng "Ah Beng") in real triad societies deliberately copied the yellow hairstyles in these movies for signaling group identity and intimidated outsiders.

### In Mainland China

While triad societies had little existence in mainland China, from the 1990s to the early 2000s, many marginalized young people became obsessed with the underworld culture of Hong Kong triad films. They deliberately copied the looks and deeds of movie characters, including dyeing their hair yellow, wandering in groups, smoking and picking fights together.

Meanwhile, mainland Chinese TV dramas with rural, campus and urban folk themes also adopted this set of styling when creating roles of petty street hooligans, constantly reinforcing this stereotypical label. Most blonde-haired youngsters that ordinary passers-by could come across on the streets were exactly those who dropped out of regular study and work. In this way, a clear link was established between the visual symbol and real-life conduct.

In more recent years, some realistic works in Mainland China started depicting the real lives of Huang-mao from underprivileged groups and marginalized workers, one of the most prominent being the 2018 movie *Dying to Survive*.
