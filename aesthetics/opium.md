# Opium

_Also known as: Vamp, 00, Opiumcore_

[Sensitive Content Notice](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Aesthetics_Wiki:Content_Warning_Policy "Aesthetics Wiki:Content Warning Policy") ⚠️

The following article contains and discusses content that may be distressing to some readers.  
**Reason for Warning:** This page documents an aesthetic that frequently references drug use, Satanic imagery, and promiscuity. The content is presented for encyclopedic purposes and does not represent an endorsement of these themes.

**Opium**, also known as **Vamp**, is a fashion and musical aesthetic that originated in the early 2020s, stemming from the artistic direction of Playboi Carti and his record label of the same name, Opium.

Visually, the aesthetic is defined by a fusion of avant-garde high fashion (specifically the "darkwear" of Rick Owens and Balenciaga) with the rebellious iconography of 1970s [Punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Punk "Punk") and [Black Metal](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Black_Metal "Black Metal"). It represents a shift in hip-hop fashion away from the colorful designer streetwear of the 2010s (e.g., [Hypebeast](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hypebeast "Hypebeast") culture) toward a monochromatic, gothic, and silhouette-focused look.

Musically, it is associated with "Rage" beats; heavy, distorted synthesizers and erratic vocal performances that mimic the energy of a punk rock mosh pit.

## History

### Origins (Pre-WLR)

While dark themes in Trap music existed previously through artists like SpaceGhostPurrp (Raider Klan) and Lil Tracy (GothBoiClique), these iterations were largely lo-fi and grounded in underground internet culture. The specific "Opium" look began to coalesce around 2019 as Playboi Carti began working with designer Matthew Williams (Givenchy/Alyx) and adopting a "Vampire" persona.

### The "Whole Lotta Red" Shift (2020)

The aesthetic was formally established with the release of Carti's album *Whole Lotta Red* in December 2020. The album's creative direction abandoned traditional hip-hop luxury for a jagged, aggressive punk aesthetic. This era introduced the "Vamp" archetype: leather pants, mesh tops, erratic behavior, and Satanic imagery.

### Mainstream Explosion (2022–Present)

The aesthetic exploded in popularity with the rise of Opium signees Destroy Lonely and Ken Carson. Destroy Lonely's 2022 viral hit "*No Stylist*" and the subsequent leak of "*If Looks Could Kill*" popularized the "Fashion Demon" look on TikTok, transforming niche avant-garde fashion (Rick Owens boots, Vetements hoodies) into a widely replicated internet aesthetic. By 2023, the aesthetic had fractured into sub-styles, such as Ken Carson's [Goreweb](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Goreweb "Goreweb") (Chaoscore) visual identity.

## Fashion

Opium fashion is distinct from standard "[Goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Goth "Goth")" or "[Emo](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Emo "Emo")" styles due to its reliance on luxury avant-garde brands and specific silhouette play. The look is almost exclusively monochromatic black, rejecting the colorful palettes of the previous "[hypebeast](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hypebeast "Hypebeast")" era.

The "Opium uniform" typically plays with extreme proportions to create a non-human silhouette. Tops are often either extremely cropped and tight (such as compression shirts or mesh tanks) or massively oversized and draped, seen in items like Vetements bombers or Balenciaga raver hoodies. Bottoms frequently consist of waxed denim, leather trousers, or wide-leg cargo pants, exemplified by the popular Rick Owens Bolan Banana cuts.

Footwear is a highly important component of the look, with heavy, brutalist boots serving as a mandatory anchor for the outfit. Iconic models include the Rick Owens Kiss Heels, Bozo Tractor Boots, and the Balenciaga Crocs Boots or Trooper Boots. Accessories further define the style, heavily favoring silver chains and iconography from Chrome Hearts or Parts of Four, alongside futuristic, wrap-around sunglasses that obscure the face.

## Visuals

The Opium aesthetic draws heavily from German [Expressionism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Expressionism "Expressionism") (e.g., *Nosferatu*), occultism, and anarchist imagery. Photography often utilizes "deep-fried" editing, thermal/infrared cameras, or high-flash night photography to create a disorienting, drug-induced atmosphere. Graphic design within the aesthetic references [Black Metal](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Black_Metal "Black Metal") logos with illegible, spiky typography, anarchy symbols, and the pervasive "00" numeric branding. Through artists like Ken Carson, the aesthetic often incorporates shock-site imagery and digital decay, documented specifically as [Goreweb](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Goreweb "Goreweb").

## Music

### Characteristics

The Opium sound is a subgenre of Trap often called Rage. Production is characterized by "sawtooth" synthesizers, distorted 808 bass, and a lack of melodic progression, creating a hypnotic, aggressive wall of sound. Producers like F1lthy (Working on Dying) defined this sound. Vocally, artists utilize "baby voices," deep guttural frying, or erratic screaming to treat the voice as an instrument rather than a vehicle for lyrics.

## Criticism

The Opium aesthetic is frequently criticized for its cost-prohibitive nature and conformity. Critics argue that despite the aesthetic's roots in "punk" rebellion, it has become a rigid uniform where fans simply purchase the same expensive designer items to fit in, removing the [DIY ethos](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/DIY_ethos "DIY ethos") of actual [punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Punk "Punk"). Additionally, the heavy use of inverted crosses and pentagrams has drawn ire from religious conservatives, though fans argue this is purely aesthetic shock value borrowed from 80s Metal culture.
