# 2020 Alt

_Also known as: Alt Kid, E-Kid, Bunny Hat Kid, Alt TikTok (broader term)_

"Alt Kid" redirects here. Not to be confused with [alternative](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Alternative "Alternative") fashion and subcultures.

**2020 Alt** (variously called **E-Kid**, **Alt Kid**, or **Bunny Hat Kid**) is an internet-based youth subculture and fashion style that gained prominence during the COVID-19 pandemic in the early 2020s. Popularized on TikTok (specifically the sub-community known as "Alt TikTok"), the aesthetic aggregates pre-existing [alternative](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Alternative "Alternative") styles, functioning as a time capsule of quarantine-era online culture.

The style utilizes a collage-like approach to fashion, drawing heavily from [Mallgoth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Mallgoth "Mallgoth"), [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene"), and [E-Girl](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/E-Girl "E-Girl")/[E-Boy](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/E-Boy "E-Boy") aesthetics. It maintains thematic links to anime, video games, and the [hyperpop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hyperpop "Hyperpop") music scene. The community adopted the term "Alt Kid" to replace the gendered "E-Girl" and "E-Boy" labels, aiming for inclusivity while distancing the subculture from negative connotations associated with those earlier terms. Following its initial decline, the aesthetic experienced a brief revival attempt in 2025 under the slogan "2025 is the new 2020."

## History

### The "Straight vs. Alt" Dichotomy

Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, alternative fashion on TikTok was largely restricted to the polarized [E-Girl](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/E-Girl "E-Girl") and [VSCO Girl](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/VSCO_Girl "VSCO Girl") subcultures. The 2020 Alt identity coalesced from these early styles, rapidly mainstreaming previously marginalized queer and neurodivergent youth expressions. This new aesthetic formed in direct opposition to "Straight TikTok," a colloquialism for the platform's default algorithm. Straight TikTok relied on viral dance choreographies and conventionally attractive influencers. In contrast, the "Alt TikTok" community hosted unconventional, experimental content. Users actively policed the boundaries between these two algorithms, frequently utilizing the catchphrase "bestie, we almost lost you to Straight TikTok" to signal in-group solidarity when mainstream content appeared on their feeds. Media observers noted this side of the platform operated as a conscious counterculture, utilizing dramatic makeup and absurdist humor. Sociological analyses compared this dynamic to classic models of social deviance, where Alt users actively rejected mainstream ideals to foster an exclusive identity.

### DIY Ethos and Micro-Trend Controversy

Because the demographic largely consisted of young teenagers confined to their homes without disposable income, the subculture developed a strong DIY component. Participants crafted jewelry from safety pins, stacked handmade kandi bracelets, and repurposed Monster Energy can tabs as faux septum piercings. However, this generated significant internal controversy regarding ethical consumption. Traditional alternative communities heavily criticized 2020 Alt as a fast-fashion micro-trend, arguing that despite the DIY elements, the aesthetic heavily relied on mass-purchasing cheap garments from Amazon, most notably the plush bunny hats, which were quickly discarded into landfills once the trend passed. Despite this friction, the community maintained a highly protective structure, frequently accusing mainstream creators of co-opting a culture meant for marginalized youth when Alt audio tracks went viral.

### Decline and the 2025 Revival Attempt

Music industry analysts initially projected the movement would achieve mainstream crossover success comparable to the mid-2000s third-wave [emo](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Emo "Emo") boom. The peak of the 2020 Alt aesthetic, however, remained brief. By 2023, the style had largely faded as subsequent internet trends, such as the Y2K revival, gained dominance in youth fashion. In January 2025, former participants attempted a "2020 Revival," creating nostalgic content featuring the era's original fashion and audio tracks. This resurgence collapsed almost immediately due to the political turmoil and algorithmic suppression surrounding the U.S. government's legislative efforts to ban TikTok that same month, stifling the movement's momentum.

## Visuals

The 2020 Alt aesthetic relies on specific cosmetic applications and digital post-processing techniques native to early quarantine-era TikTok. Makeup application centers on heavy, unblended pink blush applied directly across the tip of the nose, paired with exaggerated black winged eyeliner. Users frequently draw additional cosmetic details using liquid eyeliner, such as under-eye dots, faux stitches across the nose bridge, or drawn-on lower eyelashes. A prominent subset of the community adopts extreme, theatrical makeup featuring stark white foundation and black lipstick, directly imitating the aesthetic of musical artist Jazmin Bean.

Photography and video content typically take place in domestic bedroom settings illuminated by colored LED strip lights. Users edit these images with high-saturation filters and overlay them with dense clusters of digital stickers. This digital curation heavily features Sanrio characters like Kuromi, alongside Care Bears, glowing hearts, and pentagrams.

## Fashion

2020 Alt fashion is a pastiche of older alternative subcultures, combining [Mallgoth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Mallgoth "Mallgoth"), [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene"), and [E-Girl](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/E-Girl "E-Girl") elements into a layered maximalist silhouette. Upper-body clothing frequently features oversized graphic T-shirts layered over long-sleeved striped shirts, or heavily distressed, oversized striped knit sweaters. Lower-body garments include pleated mini-skirts, dark cargo pants, or lounge pants featuring pop-culture motifs like *The Nightmare Before Christmas*. Participants typically layer these over ripped fishnet tights, paired with asymmetrical or striped thigh-high socks. Footwear consists of heavy, high-platform boots with multiple metal heart or strap buckles, heavily utilizing brands like Demonia.

Accessories are layered to create a cluttered appearance, incorporating studded belts, thick silver chains, spiked chokers, and fluffy arm warmers. The plush bunny hat with long, dangling ears is the primary accessory of the era, occasionally customized with devil horns or safety pins. Hair is frequently dyed in bright unnatural colors, including split-dye techniques, and styled with high volume, often brushed forward to obscure the eyes.

## Sub-Trends

### Glitter Anime Profile Pictures

An ubiquitous micro-aesthetic on Alt TikTok in 2020. Users would set their profile pictures to screenshots of characters from popular anime (e.g., *Hunter x Hunter*, *Toilet-Bound Hanako-kun*, *My Hero Academia*) or games like *Danganronpa*. These images were edited with "sparkle" filters, pastel overlays, and soft grain. It signaled membership within the "Alt" community and often indicated that the user was a minor or part of the "anitok" (Anime TikTok) circle.

### Maximalist Cosplay

During the 2020 COVID-19 lockdowns, a specific approach to cosplay emerged within the 2020 Alt community, heavily intersecting with the *Danganronpa* video game fandom. Participants moved away from traditional "canon accuracy" and instead layered character base costumes with a high density of subcultural accessories. This practice drew direct inspiration from Japanese [Decora](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Decora "Decora") fashion, resulting in outfits crowded with handmade [kandi](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Kandi "Kandi") bracelets (historically associated with [rave](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rave "Rave") culture), spiked chokers, rainbow butterfly clips, and safety pins.

The style integrated the cosplayer's personal alternative wardrobe, such as striped fingerless gloves and novelty animal hats, with the fictional character's recognizable traits, leading to the colloquial label "Danganronpacore."

The approach generated distinct internal debates within the cosplay community. Traditionalists argued the excessive layering obscured the original character designs, while proponents viewed the practice as a valid avenue for highly personalized self-expression. A separate, severe controversy occurred between 2020 and 2021 when a small subset of participants allegedly began attaching used sanitary products, such as tampons and menstrual pads, to their costumes as shock-value accessories. The broader internet community widely condemned this practice as unsanitary and socially inappropriate, generating significant backlash against the 2020 Alt cosplay scene on platforms like Reddit and TikTok.

### "Battery Acid" Drinks

"Battery Acid" was a viral drink recipe popularized by the 2020 Alt community on TikTok. The beverage consists of a base of Monster Energy (typically the white or pink zero-sugar varieties) mixed with an assortment of highly processed snacks like gummy candies, such as Sour Patch Kids or Haribo cherries. The candies are dropped directly into the energy drink and left to soak. The final ingredient is a packet of Pop Rocks, which is poured into the liquid to create a loud crackling sound and a fizzing reaction. Users frequently filmed themselves mixing and taste-testing the concoction, turning the intentionally unhealthy drink into a recognizable staple of quarantine-era internet culture.

### The "Discourse Community"

The 2020 Alt subculture co-existed with the highly politicized social media of the COVID-19 pandemic, frequently aligning with progressive movements such as Black Lives Matter (BLM), LGBTQ+ rights, and adopting the rejection of "[cringe culture](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Cringe_culture "Cringe culture")" as a main belief. This alignment generated a specific sub-trend known as the "discourse community." Users created image edits that deliberately juxtaposed radical political slogans with cute, visually unrelated media properties. Common formats paired Sanrio characters, anime screenshots, or *Danganronpa* character sprites with anti-capitalist quotes or anti-police (ACAB) acronyms. The practice relied heavily on the [aestheticization of politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/aestheticization_of_politics "wikipedia:aestheticization of politics"), transforming complex sociopolitical debates into highly shareable meme formats. Critics frequently categorized this trend as performative activism, arguing that the community reduced serious geopolitical issues to superficial virtue signaling.

### Deep TikTok

As the Alt TikTok community expanded and intersected with the platform's mainstream userbase ("Straight TikTok"), a faction of creators seeking to maintain a strict countercultural edge developed "Deep TikTok." DeepTok relied heavily on memes featuring absurdist humor and intentionally "cursed" media designed to disorient the viewer. Visually, the trend utilized extreme facial distortion filters, deep-fried color saturation, and glitch overlays.

Creators frequently repurposed children's entertainment (such as *Peppa Pig* or *Thomas the Tank Engine*) and ElsaGate-style content, pairing the visuals with heavily distorted, repetitive audio tracks or corrupted nursery rhymes. The subculture also popularized specific text-based slang, most notably the frequent deployment of the "👁👄👁" emoji combination and the use of randomized foreign language scripts intended to evoke a sense of [digital horror](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Digital_Horror "Digital Horror").

## Music

Unlike older subcultures that formed around specific genres (like [Goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Goth "Goth"), [Punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Punk "Punk"), [Metalheads](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Metalhead "Metalhead"), etc.), the music associated with the 2020 Alt subculture was highly disparate, being an aggregate of multiple distinct genres rather than a singular genre. This sonic variety was driven by the algorithmic nature of TikTok where specific audio snippets dictated visual trends. The most commonly associated genre was [hyperpop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hyperpop "Hyperpop"). Acts like 100 gecs and CMTEN's "*NEVER MET!*" popularized a chaotic and heavily processed sound that directly influenced the subculture's editing styles. Mainstream music outlets sometimes termed this style "[Glitchcore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Glitchcore "Glitchcore")," though on TikTok it is primarily understood as a visual style rather than a music genre. This electronic foundation frequently overlapped with digital hardcore and [glitchbreak](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Glitchbreak "Glitchbreak") (a specific subgenre of atmospheric drum and bass that is often mistakenly called "breakcore"), resulting in artists like Machine Girl and older electro-punk acts such as Crystal Castles receiving renewed attention within the "Alt" community.

A second common category consisted of theatrical internet-based artists whose music featured highly specific cynical or absurd lyricism. Musicians including Penelope Scott, Sodikken, Jack Stauber, and Jazmin Bean provided tracks that participants heavily utilized for cosplay transitions and makeup tutorials. This theatricality sparked a massive revival of 2000s-era [Dark Cabaret](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dark_Cabaret "Dark Cabaret") and "[Nerdcore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Nerdcore "Nerdcore")" bands. Groups such as Tally Hall, Lemon Demon, and Will Wood and the Tapeworms experienced significant resurgences, as their eccentric sounds aligned with the community's maximalist presentation.

However, the community also elevated bedroom pop and indie rock. Artists such as Mitski, TV Girl, Cavetown, and Roar were the softer soundtracks for 2020 Alt videos. Alternative rock and post-punk bands, most notably Mother Mother and The Garden, became audio staples for outfit showcases. Additionally, the subculture maintained strong ties to earlier niche internet music traditions, frequently circulating tracks from Vocaloid producers like Kikuo and the [Y2K](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/McBling "McBling")-inspired pop of Ayesha Erotica.
