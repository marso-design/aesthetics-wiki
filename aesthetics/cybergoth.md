# Cybergoth

[Sensitive Content Notice](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Aesthetics_Wiki:Content_Warning_Policy "Aesthetics Wiki:Content Warning Policy") ⚠️

The following article contains and discusses content that may be distressing to some readers.  
**Reason for Warning:** This page documents a subculture characterized by transgressive themes. The associated music genres explicitly feature motifs of dystopian conflict, violence, and aggression. The fashion incorporates fetish-inspired elements (e.g., gas masks, medical motifs, PVC/vinyl) and references explicit themes in its lyrical content (e.g., violence, sadomasochism). Viewer discretion is advised.

**Cybergoth** is a subculture that originated in the late 1990s, drawing influence from [Raver](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Raver "Raver"), [Rivethead](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rivethead "Rivethead"), and [Cyberpunk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Cyberpunk "Cyberpunk") aesthetics. The style is characterized by its futuristic and [post-apocalyptic](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Post-apocalyptic "Post-apocalyptic") fashion, which combines a base of black, industrial-style clothing with UV-reactive neon colors. The most iconic elements of the look are synthetic dreadfalls known as "cyberlox," large goggles (often worn on the forehead), and accessories like gas masks, biohazard symbols, and circuit board patterns. The fashion is completed with massive platform boots and is designed for the high-energy "industrial dance" style performed in clubs to electronic music genres like [EBM](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/EBM "EBM"), [Aggrotech](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Aggrotech "Aggrotech"), and Futurepop. Despite its name, Cybergoth is a subculture distinct from [Goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Goth "Goth"), as its musical foundations are in electronic and [industrial](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Industrial "Industrial") music scenes, rather than gothic rock or [post-punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Post-punk "Post-punk").

## History

The term "cybergoth" was first coined by the roleplaying game company Games Workshop in 1988 for their game *Dark Future*. However, this use of the term is unrelated to the subculture that would later adopt the name. The Cybergoth subculture and fashion style emerged independently in the late 1990s out of a fusion of the German and Austrian rave scenes and the [industrial](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Industrial "Industrial") music scene. The precursors to cybergoths were sometimes referred to as "**Gravers**" (a portmanteau of "Gothic Ravers"), who were individuals from the [rave](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rave "Rave") scene who began frequenting [goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Goth "Goth") and industrial clubs.

The look began to solidify in the late 1990s and early 2000s, with brands like the London-based Cyberdog, founded in 1994, playing a key role in developing and popularizing the aesthetic. Cyberdog's fusion of neon, UV-reactive materials with trippy, futuristic designs provided a commercial foundation for the emerging style. The subculture gained visibility online through communities like the Usenet group alt.gothic.cybergoth and rose to prominence in club scenes in cities like London, particularly at venues like Slimelight. The look became internationally recognized, with strong scenes developing in the UK, Germany, and the United States.

By the late 2000s, the subculture's mainstream visibility began to decline, in line with a general downturn in the nightclub industry and the fading of many distinct youth subcultures. While the scene is smaller today, it continues to exist in dedicated club nights and at large industrial and goth music festivals, such as Wave-Gotik-Treffen in Germany.

## Fashion

Cybergoth fashion is defined by its futuristic, artificial aesthetic, which combines a dark, monochrome base with a single, starkly contrasting neon or UV-reactive color, such as green, pink, blue, or orange. The silhouette is often androgynous.

Clothing is typically made from materials that create an artificial and futuristic look. This includes shiny black PVC or vinyl, matte rubber, and reflective materials. Common garments include tight pants, vests, and tops made of solid, ripped, or fishnet fabrics. Faux fur leg warmers, known as "fluffies," are also a popular accessory.

The most defining feature of the Cybergoth look is the hair. Adherents often wear elaborate synthetic dreadfalls known as cyberlox, which are made from a variety of materials including yarn, foam strips, and tubular crinoline. These falls are typically multicolored, incorporating the chosen accent color.

Accessories are essential to the style and emphasize its technological and post-apocalyptic themes. Large goggles, often in an aviator style, are almost always worn, typically on the forehead rather than over the eyes. Gas masks or shiny PVC medical-style masks are also common. Other motifs include biohazard symbols, circuit board patterns, and futuristic or medical fetish-inspired elements. Footwear consists almost exclusively of large, heavy platform boots from brands like Demonia.

## Dance

A central element of the Cybergoth subculture is "industrial dance," a high-energy and expressive style performed in clubs. Industrial dance is characterized by rhythmic, stomping movements of the feet, combined with flailing or punching arm motions, often incorporating glow sticks to create light trails in the dark club environment. This dance style is a key part of the subculture's identity and is a major focus of Cybergoth gatherings.

## Music

The Cybergoth subculture is centered around specific genres of electronic and [industrial](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Industrial "Industrial") music, and is not associated with the gothic rock or [post-punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Post-punk "Post-punk") of the traditional [goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Goth "Goth") scene. The music is characterized by its hard, electronic beats and is designed for the high-energy "industrial dance" style.

## Terminology and Relationship to Goth

While "Cybergoth" is the de facto name for the subculture, it is frequently cited as a technical misnomer within the alternative community. The suffix "-goth" implies a direct lineage from [Gothic Rock](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Goth "Goth") and [Post-Punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Post-Punk "Post-Punk"), but Cybergoth shares almost no musical DNA with these genres. Its roots lie firmly in the [Industrial](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Industrial "Industrial") (specifically [EBM](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/EBM "EBM"), [Futurepop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Futurepop "Futurepop"), and [Aggrotech](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Aggrotech "Aggrotech")) and [Rave](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rave "Rave") scenes.

This distinction has historically led to friction, where traditional Goths may view Cybergoth as a branch of [Rivethead](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rivethead "Rivethead") or Raver culture rather than "true" Goth. However, the term has persisted because the subcultures share a "dark" visual aesthetic and frequent the same venues and festivals (such as the "[Dark Culture](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dark_Culture "Dark Culture")" or *Schwarze Szene* in Germany). As a result, Cybergoth is widely accepted as a "cousin" to Goth, linked by fashion and club spaces rather than music.
