# Yé-yé

**Yé-yé** is a genre of pop music and youth phenomenon that originated in the early 1960s across Southwestern Europe, initially in France, but also had a significant presence in Spain, Italy, and Portugal. While the term derives from the English "yeah! yeah!" popularized by British beat groups, the movement was a distinct European phenomenon that recontextualized American rock and roll and British [Mod](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Mod "Mod") culture into a local pop-culture consciousness. It represented a cultural shift during a period of post-war economic growth, reflecting a modern cosmopolitan identity among a new generation of youth who were increasingly connected through media like the magazine *Salut les copains*.

The musical style was characterized by a combination of Anglo-American influences with traditional European pop forms, such as French *chanson*, often featuring catchy, upbeat melodies and simple danceable rhythms. While the genre frequently focused on themes of teenage life, innocent romance, and mild rebellion, some artists like Françoise Hardy incorporated more melancholic and sophisticated arrangements. The performers were predominantly young female singers known as "**yé-yé girls**" ("*chicas yé-yé*" in Spain), who became popular idols and defined the movement's fashion aesthetic. Key figures included France Gall, Françoise Hardy, Brigitte Bardot, and Sylvie Vartan in France, and Concha Velasco and Karina in Spain.

Rather than solely being a music genre, its singers ("yé-yé girls") were significant fashion icons whose style was heavily influenced by the London [Mod](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Mod "Mod") scene. This aesthetic featured modern, youthful garments such as miniskirts, A-line dresses, and go-go boots, often adorned with bold geometric patterns. The associated beauty standard was characterized by heavy "cat-eye" eyeliner and architectural hairstyles like the bob cut with a fringe, making the movement as much a phenomenon of style and modern femininity as it was of sound. In countries like Spain, the movement served as a non-confrontational but clear cultural alternative to the rigid traditionalist values of the era.

## History

### Origins

Yé-yé originated in France during the early 1960s, quickly gaining popularity throughout Western Europe. Its origins are closely linked to the French radio program *Salut les copains* ("Hello pals"), which first aired in December 1959. A key feature of this program was the segment "*Le chouchou de la semana*" ("The Sweetheart of the Week"), which focused on the latest Yé-yé music and fashion. The show played a central role in popularizing the movement into the mainstream, leading to the launch of a magazine of the same name in 1962 with editions in France, Spain, Italy, and Germany.

Hosted by Daniel Filipacchi and Frank Ténot, the show was aimed specifically at teenagers and featured iconic artists such as Sylvie Vartan, Françoise Hardy, Serge Gainsbourg, Sheila, Johnny Hallyday, Mireille Mathieu, and Salvatore Adamo. This shift coincided with the aftermath of World War II, a period when global life expectancy rose and the boundaries between "teenager" and "adult" began to be redefined. Previously, young people often shared the clothing and media tastes of their parents. The economic empowerment of youth led to the emergence of adolescence as a distinct age category, with mass media providing new idols, many of whom were influenced by the "British Invasion". While cinema first introduced adolescent heroes like James Dean, Rock 'n' Roll soon took over this influential role, with stars like Elvis Presley and French icons such as Johnny Hallyday transitioning between music and film.

### France

In France, Yé-yé represented a significant cultural shift, being one of the first musical scenes to be dominated by young women. These singers were teenagers who addressed subjects relevant to their own age group, and the movement’s influence eventually extended as far as Quebec and Japan. Yé-yé girls often projected an image of youthful innocence; for example, France Gall was 14 when she recorded her first album and won the 1965 Eurovision Song Contest at age 17. While songs like Françoise Hardy's "*Tous les garçons et les filles*" explored first love, others began to subtly touch on themes of sexuality.

Despite its DIY roots in Rock 'n' Roll, French Yé-yé was also shaped by commercial interests. Producers often encouraged a polished sound and upbeat lyrics to appeal to parents and advertisers, marketing singers as wholesome idols for brand endorsements. While the movement faded in the late 1960s, it inspired women to embrace more independent lifestyles and experienced a resurgence in popularity during the late 1970s.

### Spain

The movement persisted in Spain between 1963 and 1968, reflecting changing attitudes toward women and hinting at a future shift toward democracy. Pilar García de la Mata (Mimo) is cited as a pioneer of the scene. In 1965, the film *Historias de la Televisión* featured Concha Velasco performing "*Chica Yeyé*" which remained on the charts for 27 weeks and became a definitive national hit.

In 1968, Massiel won Eurovision with "*La, la, la*" a song originally intended for Joan Manuel Serrat. However, the Francoist regime suppressed autochthonous languages, leading to the disqualification of the original version because it was in Catalan. Spanish Yé-yé also had a strong connection to Italian pop; the San Remo Music Festival served as a major influence on Spanish events like the Benidorm International Song Festival. Regional festivals in Catalonia and Valencia fostered cultural interchange, leading to the success of artists like Salomé and Raimon, though the use of regional languages often resulted in bans from future performances by the regime. Other notable Spanish Yé-yé girls include Marisol, Rosalía, Karina, and Rocío Dúrcal.

### Portugal

Portugal's movement began in 1956 in Coimbra, inspired by international Rock 'n' Roll and the mid-1960s arrival of The Beatles. Bands like Os Babies, led by José Cid, and groups such as Os Sheiks and Os Ekos contributed to the Portuguese scene. Despite the *Estado Novo* regime's censorship, Yé-yé music appeared in nightclubs and films. The regime inadvertently promoted the movement through the 1965 "*Concurso Yé-Yé*" in Lisbon, which saw over 300 bands compete.

Unlike other countries where female singers were prominent, the Portuguese movement was notably dominated by male artists. During this period, women's rights in Portugal were significantly restricted under the regime's conservative policies.

### Influence and Legacy

The Yé-yé movement’s influence resurfaced in the 1990s through the Japanese [Shibuya-Kei](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Shibuya-Kei "Shibuya-Kei") genre. Artists like Pizzicato Five and Cornelius combined Yé-yé elements with Bossa Nova and [Disco](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Disco "Disco"). Today, the icons of the movement are remembered as national cultural symbols whose style continues to influence modern pop culture, such as informing the style of [Coquette](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Coquette "Coquette") icons like Lana del Rey.

## Fashion

### Yé-yé Girls

"**Yé-yé Girls**" (also known as *Chicas Yeyé* in Spanish, *Meninas Yé-yé* in Portuguese, *Ragazze Yé-yé* in Italian, or *Noies Ié-ié* in Catalan) is the term that came to collectively describe the young female singers associated with this movement. They were more than just pop singers; they were fashion icons, cultural trendsetters, and symbols of youthful rebellion and female empowerment during the 1960s. Their fashion style also contributed to the changing social attitudes of the time, as Western societies became less restrictive of women's fashion.

Miniskirts and tight dresses were classic elements of Yé-yé fashion as they became progressively more socially acceptable, with clear influences from [Mod](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Mod "Mod"). Hairstyles were equally important, some of the most popular ones being long bangs, straight hair, ponytails, bob cuts, or beehive styles. Even today, the hair bangs remain a recognizable symbol of the decade. Other signature fashion elements include striped shirts, A-line and shift dresses, turtlenecks, knee-high boots, Mary Jane shoes, cat-eye sunglasses, headbands, and bow ties. Makeup was used to a minimal extent, typically only applied to the eyes or the lips.

### Yé-yé Boys

Although Yé-yé is primarily associated with young women, it was not inherently an all-girl movement either. Male artists also played an important role in shaping the genre. These "**Yé-yé Boys**" usually took on a romantic and sensitive image. Richard Anthony, for example, achieved widespread fame with his French-language covers of popular American songs, becoming a teen idol in the early 1960s. Serge Gainsbourg not only pushed boundaries with his erotic songs with strong messages such as "*Je t'aime moi non plus*" with his girlfriend Jane Birkin, but also wrote numerous hits for other Yé-yé singers, including France Gall's iconic "*Poupée de cire, poupée de son*." Another notable artist, Raphael, has become one of the most successful Spanish singers worldwide, known for his long on-going career and dramatic style. He represented Spain in the Eurovision Song Contest in 1966 and 1967, respectively with his songs "*Yo soy aquél*" in Luxembourg and "*Hablemos del Amor*" in Vienna. In Portugal, the Yé-yé movement was primarily represented by all-male bands, possibly related to women's limited opportunities under the *Estado Novo* regime.

## Music

### Notable Artists

The Yé-yé movement was a transnational phenomenon, with distinct but interconnected scenes across the Francosphere and Southwest Europe.

* **France:** France Gall, Françoise Hardy, Sylvie Vartan, Sheila, Johnny Hallyday, Jacques Dutronc, Serge Gainsbourg, Jacqueline Taïeb, Annie Philippe, Chantal Goya.
* **Italy:** Rita Pavone, Mina, Patty Pravo, Caterina Caselli, Gigliola Cinquetti, Adriano Celentano, Gianni Morandi.
* **Spain:** Karina, Marisol, Concha Velasco, Gelu, Rosalía, Jeanette, Joan Manuel Serrat, Lita Torelló.
* **Portugal:** Simone de Oliveira, Paulo de Carvalho, Tonicha, Daniel Bacelar, Os Conchas.
* **Quebec:** Claire Lepage, Pierre Lalonde, Michèle Richard, Tony Roman, César et les Romains.
