# Keller Synth

_Also known as: Tänzelcore (specific subgenre with heavier electronic influence)_

[Sensitive Content Notice](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Aesthetics_Wiki:Content_Warning_Policy "Aesthetics Wiki:Content Warning Policy") ⚠️

The following article contains and discusses content that may be distressing to some readers.  
**Reason for Warning:** This page documents a musical scene occasionally associated with far-right extremism, National Socialist imagery, antisemitic dogwhistles, hate speech, and the explicit glorification of drug abuse. Reader discretion is advised.

**Keller Synth** (derived from the German word *Keller*, meaning "cellar" or "basement"), frequently used interchangeably with **Tänzelcore** (from German *tänzeln*, meaning "to prance" or "dance"), is an underground electronic music subgenre that originated in the late 2010s. It fuses the medieval melodies of [Dungeon Synth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dungeon_Synth "Dungeon Synth") with aggressive elements from [Black Metal](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Black_Metal "Black Metal") and a deliberately unpolished, lo-fi production style.

The aesthetic utilizes a "deep-fried" medievalism featuring digital distortion and intentional crudeness. Unlike the somber tone of traditional Dungeon Synth, Keller Synth frequently pairs a high-contrast palette of neon green and black with absurdist imagery of woodcut wizards. The genre gained popularity on platforms like TikTok in the early 2020s, transitioning from an underground musical niche into a recognizable visual meme.

## History

Keller Synth's origins are primarily linked to the local [Black Metal](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Black_Metal "Black Metal") scene in the Saarland region of Germany, specifically its economic realities during the late 1990s and early 2000s. At the time, the area was experiencing significant industrial and economic decline following the closure of its historical coal mines and steel mills. This environment of rural isolation and economic depression directly shaped the scene's development. Unable to afford expensive studio time or traditional instruments, local musicians retreated into the basements (*Keller*) of cheap housing.

The foundational sound and visual aesthetic were pioneered out of this economic necessity by Andreas "B.S.o.D." Bettinger, whose works established the fusion of [Dungeon Synth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dungeon_Synth "Dungeon Synth") atmospheres with gritty black metal electronics and cheap drum machines instead of guitars. The musician Justin Reiter (Sigfrid) later expanded the style and is credited with coining the term "Keller Synth" to describe this basement-recorded, abrasive evolution of dungeon synth.

Andreas Bettinger (born August 1, 1980) heavily influenced the genre's thematic focus on severe substance abuse. According to biographical profiles within the metal community, Bettinger allegedly accumulated 147 offenses on his police record by 2013, 145 of which were drug-related, alongside isolated charges for violence and incitement of hatred. This extreme focus on narcotics is directly reflected in his own public musical output and personal ideology; he has been a documented advocate of heroin use, which is explicitly romanticized in his Keller Synth side project Heroin Macht Glücklich (Heroin Makes Happy).

The genre's association with internet meme culture was further solidified when the 1999 track "Misanthrop" by Blod Besvimelse (a project by Bettinger's late wife, Sandra) went viral on TikTok as the backing audio for the "Crawly the Gnome" trend.

Initially confined to German internet-based underground circles on platforms such as Bandcamp and RateYourMusic, the style transitioned from a localized niche into a global internet phenomenon during the early-to-mid 2020s. While the genre was created by Andreas Bettinger and Justin Reiter (Sigfrid, b. 2002), the aesthetic's expansion was driven by TikTok, where the juxtaposition of distorted medievalism and "raving wizard" imagery resonated with internet irony culture and drew ocassional comparisons to the visual style of artists like Yabujin (see also [Xpiritualism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Xpiritualism "Xpiritualism")/[Yabujincore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Yabujincore "Yabujincore")). This visibility triggered a massive influx of Generation Z producers, establishing active producers in countries like Indonesia (Tenebris), Brazil (Dementar), and Eastern Europe (Hexenmeister in Donetsk, Warlock Corpse in Kazakhstan). These international producers frequently integrate their regional folklore into the genre's thematic elements.

## Visuals

Keller Synth and Tänzelcore relies heavily on digital distortion and crudeness, frequently referred to as "shitty medievalism." It is primarily identified by a high-saturation neon green (or purple) on black color palette, resulting in images that resemble "deep-fried" early computer graphics or low-resolution bitmaps.

A central motif is the "Cocky Wizard" or "Prancing Wizard," which consists of classical woodcut illustrations digitally manipulated to appear as if they are dancing or raving. A specific recurring gesture is the "Nose Thumb," a traditional sign of mockery where a thumb is placed to the nose with wiggling fingers. This imagery is deeply rooted in Germanic trickster folklore, referencing figures like Till Eulenspiegel. This sign is often edited onto characters in album art to signal the genre's mischievous attitude.

Other common visual elements include the Triskelion, a three-legged sun face symbol often featured in lime green, and Amanita muscaria mushrooms. While mushrooms are depicted to reference foraging, the aesthetic is heavily tied to the romanticization of hard drug use. Reflecting the themes of B.S.o.D. projects like Heroin Makes Happy, the visual atmosphere frequently incorporates imagery of damp basements, ruins, and obscure rural German landscapes filtered to look alien and toxic, hinting at severe substance abuse. Typography typically involves illegible black metal logos mixed with crude, pixelated medieval fonts.

Another prominent mascot within the scene is the Blemmyae, a mythological headless humanoid with its face located on its torso. This figure gained recognition after appearing on the cover of an early split album between Corneus and Grollfried (projects operated by Bettinger and Sigfrid, respectively), representing a bridge between the genre's two founding figures.

In video formats, such as looping background media and music videos, artists frequently apply a continuous hue-shifting effect to these static graphics. This technique cycles the image through the entire neon color spectrum over time, which is then paired with extreme contrast and digital bitcrushing to maintain the genre's degraded, low-resolution aesthetic.

## Typography and Logos

Keller Synth features a highly distinct approach to typography that parodies traditional [Black Metal](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Black_Metal "Black Metal") aesthetics.

Band logos are intentionally crude, often appearing hand-drawn in MS Paint or sketched with deliberate sloppiness. These logos prioritize extreme illegibility, utilizing chaotic, squiggly linework that integrates esoteric symbols directly into the lettering, such as inverted crosses, pentagrams, wizard hats, and crescent moons.

Online, such as in adherents' usernames, track listings, and playlist titles (e.g., Sigfrid's official [𖤐⛈𓉢𝔗𝑎̈𝑛𝑧𝑒𝑙𝑐𝑜𝑟𝑒𓉢⛈𖤐🂠](https://open.spotify.com/playlist/3ouJmcXBoz1fY8cCbdNTQ4) Spotify playlist), this aesthetic translates into the heavy use of Unicode symbols, ASCII art, and Fraktur (medieval) text generators to create cluttered, heavily stylized text strings (e.g., 𝔗𝔞̈𝔫𝔷𝔢𝔩𝔠𝔬𝔯𝔢).

## Fashion

Because Keller Synth and Tänzelcore are predominantly internet-based aesthetics, they manifest primarily through cassette layouts, album art, band logos, and the curation of online profiles (such as by fans on SoundCloud and Discord). Consequently, the subculture lacks a standardized everyday wardrobe. However, a specific fashion style has emerged on TikTok. In these live-action video contexts, participants frequently combine archetypal fantasy garments, such as pointed witch hats or medieval tunics, with tactical and outdoor apparel. A prominent, recurring element in these videos is the use of military surplus utilizing Flecktarn, the standard disruptive camouflage pattern issued by the German armed forces.

## Music

### Characteristics

Musically, Keller Synth is distinguished from standard [Dungeon Synth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dungeon_Synth "Dungeon Synth") by its percussion and aggressive tone. It typically features cheap MIDI synthesizers mimicking medieval instruments like flutes and harpsichords played at high speeds, frequently layered with classic 1990s rave synthesizers like the Roland Alpha Juno to create buzzing, aggressive "Hoover" sounds. Vocals consist of high-pitched, pitch-shifted "goblin" voices or distorted shrieks buried in the mix. The genre relies on a deliberately "lo-fi" or "raw" production quality, mimicking the sound of music recorded on cheap cassette tapes in a basement.

The specific offshoot known as "Tänzelcore" places a much heavier emphasis on electronic music influences. Coined by Sigfrid and established around 2019 with Bergënot's *Moselfrankian Tänzelcore Madness*, Tänzelcore shifts away from [Black Metal](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Black_Metal "Black Metal") atmospheres to focus almost entirely on aggressive rhythmic elements. This variation heavily utilizes distorted kick drums derived from Hardcore Techno, [Gabber](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Gabber "Gabber"), and [Acid House](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Acid_House "Acid House"), often incorporating "amen breaks" or four-on-the-floor patterns pushed to extreme clipping levels through analog mixer emulators or distortion pedals.

## Criticism

### Far-Right Symbolism and Dogwhistles

The Keller Synth scene has faced scrutiny regarding the political affiliations of prominent figures. The genre's pioneers, including Sigfrid and B.S.o.D., have been accused of using far-right imagery and dog whistles in their work. For instance, Andreas Bettinger's history includes direct documented ties to the German National Socialist Black Metal (NSBM) scene, including live performances with the prominent NSBM band Absurd. Critics note that the "Nose Thumb" gesture (🤥🤚🤚) has a history of use in antisemitic caricatures, though it is also a childish taunt in German-speaking countries. Concerns have also been raised regarding the prevalence of symbols like the number 88 in usernames associated with the scene.

The use of symbols such as the three-legged sun (Triskelion) has also sparked debate. While the symbol has ancient Celtic and Sicilian origins, it has occasionally been co-opted by fringe political movements. The community is divided between those who view the imagery as "edgy" internet irony and those who see it as a signal of far-right alignment.

In response to the far-right associations of the genre's founders, a counter-movement has emerged within the Red and Anarchist Black Metal (RABM) community. Independent labels like Grime Stone Records and various underground artists actively produce anti-fascist Keller Synth and Tänzelcore. These creators attempt to reclaim the chaotic DIY production style of the music to express anti-capitalist themes and subvert genre expectations, proving the sound itself is not inherently tied to the political extremism of its early pioneers.

### Reception in the Dungeon Synth and Black Metal Scenes

Keller Synth and Tänzelcore frequently receive negative reception from the established [Dungeon Synth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dungeon_Synth "Dungeon Synth") and [Black Metal](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Black_Metal "Black Metal") communities. Many purists view the genre as an unserious offshoot lacking the atmosphere and musical rigor of traditional releases, often describing the style as relying entirely on shock value over substance. The scene is heavily criticized for its influx of younger audiences originating from TikTok and the [SoundCloud Rap](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/SoundCloud_Rap "SoundCloud Rap") sphere, leading to accusations that the genre caters to internet trend-hoppers (such as fans of [Yabujincore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Yabujincore "Yabujincore") and [Haunted Mound](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Haunted_Mound "Haunted Mound")) rather than dedicated listeners.

The deliberate blending of abrasive dance beats, cheap synthesizer riffs, and intentionally poor production is frequently dismissed by detractors as an embarrassing mockery of the parent genres. Furthermore, the extreme edginess of the aesthetic, where artists are perceived to be roleplaying as drug addicts or leaning heavily into internet irony, frustrates traditionalists who view it as a superficial bastardization of black metal history rather than a legitimate musical evolution.

In response to these critiques, proponents of the genre argue that Tänzelcore is not intended to be a traditional subgenre of Black Metal. Instead, they argue that it is an electronic fusion where [Black Metal](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Black_Metal "Black Metal") is merely one textural element alongside high-energy dance influences. Many fans and artists point to the rhythmic structures of Hardcore Techno and [Gabber](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Gabber "Gabber") as more accurate precursors to the Tänzelcore sound than conventional metal. This [rave](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rave "Rave") music lineage is visible in the discographies of the genre's founders, with Sigfrid utilizing projects like Carlos Esmeralda Jalapeño to directly incorporate the fast-paced melodies of Spanish [Mákina](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/M%C3%A1kina "Mákina") into the scene.
