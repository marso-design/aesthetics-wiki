# Criticism of Aesthetics

[Sensitive Content Notice](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Aesthetics_Wiki:Content_Warning_Policy "Aesthetics Wiki:Content Warning Policy") ⚠️

The following article contains and discusses content that may be distressing to some readers.  
**Reason for Warning:** This article contains in-depth discussions of sensitive and potentially triggering topics. These include the aestheticization and romanticization of eating disorders (anorexia), self-harm, abuse, drug use, and mental illness such as depression and trauma.

As the online presence of aesthetics on social media grows, many cultural critics and casual observers have commented on the negative effects of the community in general, specifically in how aesthetics are consumed and how it affects self-identity. There are also economic consequences on the growth of aesthetics, especially in regard to consumerism and the production of new products. Below are the various points critics have made, which includes a summary of the various points made in trending articles and video essays, as well as connections to previous works about beauty and consumerism that existed before internet aesthetics rose in popularity. These sources are also included for a more in-depth look into the arguments made by these people.

## Growth of Fast Fashion and Consumerism

One of the biggest issues that has come out of trying on and changing aesthetics is how the content made in the aesthetic is unsustainable. Many formats of aesthetic content emphasize hauls and styling videos with items coming from cheap fast fashion and home goods stores such as Shein, Amazon, and AliExpress. In these videos, the creator purchases a large sum of clothes, often making these videos a repeat feature of their channel.

Fast fashion and home goods are destructive to the environment and often employ unethical labor practices in order to keep up with demand and lower prices. Specifically, the consumption of water in cotton, presence of microplastics in polyester, dye runoffs into the environment, and greenhouse gases produced wrecks the environment, with these factories often being placed in poorer countries. Additionally, the material and construction of the garment makes these clothes unusable after a short length of time, and the wearer tosses it away and replaces it with another easily broken garment. This has a large impact on landfill waste, which is also important since polyester is not biodegradable.

The growth of the online aesthetics community encourages fast fashion consumption, in that trying on and disposing of fashion is a way to create content and have fun. The faddish nature of aesthetics is also an issue because a person may decide to try on an aesthetic, purchase a haul of different clothes and things for their room, then move onto the next aesthetic that catches their eye. Hyper-consumption can be a result of trying to copy aesthetics too quickly and without consideration for purchasing wisely.

## Performativity and the Crisis of Authenticity

One issue many people have pointed out is that as the aesthetics community becomes a larger part of a person's media diet, a person would more and more look at their life through the lens of an aesthetic. People in the community would continuously want to "be" an aesthetic and monitor and judge their own actions and change their behaviors accordingly. For example, a fan of Dark Academia might feel bad for wearing a sweatshirt while studying instead of a tweed blazer, even if nobody is around to see them not following that aesthetic. This self-surveillance results in poor mental health.

Many people also point out how aesthetics, especially feminine ones where body image and fashion are important components, reinforce issues of gender. Young women are often the target of "the male gaze," where there is an internal voyeur monitoring how cute, sexy, polished, etc. a girl is, even when there is no one around to perceive her. Many feminists discuss how when society imposes beauty onto women, many women live unnatural lives and can develop eating disorders, consumerist behaviors, etc., and many different internet aesthetics have this issue, such as on [Pale Grunge](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Pale_Grunge "Pale Grunge"), [Waif](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Waif "Waif"), [Morute](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Morute "Morute"), etc.

Another issue many have pointed out is how many people take on behaviors such as consuming certain media, having different habits, etc. is not because they genuinely enjoy the behavior, but rather because they want to be *perceived* as being a certain kind of person that is trendy, cool, mysterious, fashionable, etc. This is especially true for aspirational aesthetics such as [Dark Academia](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dark_Academia "Dark Academia") and [That Girl](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/That_Girl "That Girl"). In other words, these people are [posers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poseur), and people who naturally gravitate to and enjoy the media, fashion, hobbies, etc. before the aesthetic has become popular are often miffed at the inauthenticity of the people in it for the trend. Examples of these situations are when a person copies different outfits instead of being original, doesn't take on the real aspects of a subculture, and if their enjoyment of a fictional work is shallower and lacks the same critical analysis of a person who genuinely enjoys the work.

## Over-categorization

A significant criticism of the modern aesthetics community is the tendency toward over-categorization, where every image, object, activity, or even feeling is dissected and assigned to a niche micro-label. This impulse is often mocked in online posts that sarcastically ask for the "aesthetic" of mundane experiences like touching grass or drinking water. Critics argue that this compulsive need to categorize everything reduces the complexity of personal style, art, and daily life to a simplified and rigid checklist.

This approach is seen as reductionist because it can discourage a deeper engagement with the subject being categorized. For instance, a classic piece of literature might be valued not for its themes or prose, but simply as a prop that "fits" the [Dark Academia](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dark_Academia "Dark Academia") aesthetic. This flattens the rich, multidimensional aspects of culture into a set of superficial visual signifiers. This can create rigid boundaries that stifle personal expression and innovation, pressuring individuals to conform to a predefined "core" rather than developing a unique style that might fluidly combine different influences.

Furthermore, this over-categorization can lead to absurd and overly granular labels for everyday actions, such as the [Clean Girl](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Clean_Girl "Clean Girl") aesthetic for basic hygiene or the Sleepy Girl Mocktail trend. Critics contend that this phenomenon turns everyday life into a performance that must be constantly curated and labeled. This behavior is partly driven by social media algorithms, which reward clear categorization and hashtag usage, thus incentivizing users to place every aspect of their identity into a neat, discoverable box.

## Promotion of Exclusionary and Homogenized Ideals

A recurring and significant criticism of online aesthetic culture is that it often promotes a narrow and exclusionary set of beauty standards. While the sheer number of aesthetics might suggest diversity, critics argue that many of the most popular trends are overwhelmingly centered on a specific ideal that is thin, white, able-bodied, and affluent. This focus can reinforce harmful societal biases, marginalize people of color, and perpetuate unattainable ideals of beauty and lifestyle.

This critique is particularly potent in discussions of aesthetics like the [Clean Girl](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Clean_Girl "Clean Girl"), a minimalist trend defined by features such as slicked-back hair and gold hoop earrings. The central criticism is that the aesthetic takes elements long-popularized by Black, Brown, and Latina women and rebrands them as desirable or "clean" only when adopted by white influencers. As critics point out, the term "clean" itself is racially loaded, implicitly linking whiteness with purity and suggesting that women of color, who originated many of these styles, are conversely "unclean."

This exclusionary focus is also criticized for its frequent decontextualization and appropriation of other cultures. An example of this is "Naarmcore," a Melbourne-based streetwear aesthetic that appropriated "Naarm," the Indigenous Woiwurrung and Boonwurrung name for the area. The use of an Indigenous place name for a trend centered on white participants was criticized by Aboriginal communities for dismissing thousands of years of history and reducing their language to a fleeting aesthetic.

Similar critiques have been leveled against [Dark Academia](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Dark_Academia "Dark Academia") for romanticizing a colonial, Eurocentric vision of elite education, and [Cottagecore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Cottagecore "Cottagecore") for idealizing a pastoral lifestyle that often ignores the historical realities of colonialism and rural poverty.

## Glamorization of Harmful Lifestyles and Behaviors

One of the most significant and persistent criticisms of some online aesthetics is their tendency to romanticize or glamorize behaviors and conditions that are detrimental to both physical and mental health. Critics argue that by framing these issues within a visually appealing or emotionally compelling aesthetic, online communities can normalize, and in some cases encourage, dangerous lifestyles. This phenomenon is not new, with historical precedents in mainstream fashion and marketing, but its proliferation through social media has amplified its reach and impact, particularly among young audiences.

A recurring critique is the glamorization of mental illness, particularly depression and trauma. This was prominent in the "[sad girl](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Sadgirl "Sadgirl")" aesthetic of early Tumblr, which was criticized for presenting clinical depression as a fashionable state of melancholy and intellectual depth. This trend has evolved into more extreme forms, such as the "[morute](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Morute "Morute")" aesthetic, which juxtaposes graphic imagery of self-harm, bruises, and abuse with traditionally "cute" or feminine accessories like pink ribbons.

The primary criticism of Morute is that it minimizes the actual pain of its subject matter and can be intensely triggering for victims of abuse and those recovering from self-harm. Because such content was often not tagged appropriately, it could appear in otherwise innocuous searches, creating an unsafe online environment. While some creators argue that posting such imagery is a form of personal expression or a way of processing their pain, critics are skeptical of this claim. They argue that emulating these visuals can lead others to view self-harm as a beautiful or normal expression of pain. Furthermore, by intertwining trauma with a person's core identity and lifestyle, the aesthetic may make genuine healing more difficult. A similar romanticization is seen in the idealization of the "[yandere](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Yandere "Yandere")" trope from anime and manga, which frames obsessive, violent, and psychologically abusive behaviors as an intense and desirable form of love.

The aestheticization of extremely thin bodies has a significant history, most notably with the "[heroin chic](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Heroin_Chic "Heroin Chic")" trend of the 1990s. This fashion movement glorified an emaciated, pale, and sickly appearance that was implicitly linked to drug addiction, setting a dangerous standard for body image in mainstream media.

This trend continues in various online aesthetics, particularly within subgenres of the [Coquette](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Coquette "Coquette") aesthetic like "[waif](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Waif "Waif")" and "[Wonyoungism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Wonyoungism "Wonyoungism")." The Waif aesthetic is criticized for creating an in-group where having a medically unhealthy BMI is celebrated and starving oneself is presented as a desirable form of discipline. This not only encourages the development of anorexic behaviors but is also highly triggering for individuals in recovery from eating disorders. Similarly, the "Wonyoungism" trend, centered on K-pop idol Jang Won-young, has been widely condemned for perpetuating pro-ana (pro-anorexia) content under the guise of a "healthy lifestyle." The community promotes extremely low-calorie meals and fan-estimated body measurements that are severely underweight, associating the idol herself with harmful dieting practices that she has personally spoken out against.
