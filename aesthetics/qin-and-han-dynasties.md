**Qin and Han Dynasties** is the historical imperial aesthetic of China from 221 BCE to 220 CE.

## History

*Under Construction*  
In 221 BC, the Qin Dynasty established a centralized governing system. Craftsmen had to work at official state workshops, while the overall economy was constrained, so the general public didn’t have the money or manpower to do artistic creation. Additionally, the Qin Dynasty only existed for 14 years. Thus the most notable works of art were all created on the orders of the First Emperor of Qin.

The first emperor of Han, Liu Bang (256 or 247 – 1 June 195 BC), liked [Chu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chu_(state)) music. Because of this, the Han Dynasty also brought in art styles of Chu from pre-Qin times. The design of Han-dynasty lacquerware is believed to be shaped by these arts. Improvements in textile-making skills during the Han Dynasty brought about gorgeous silk fabrics.

The Han Dynasty’s economy flourished like never before, spawning big private landed estates and bustling markets. After the [Rule of Wen and Jing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_Wen_and_Jing) (180 BC – 141 BC), the lifestyle of the society moved from simple to lavish, chasing wealth and fancy lifestyles. Private wealthy individuals started funding art. Elaborate, costly burials became a nationwide trend. All of this stimulated artwork creation.

In 130 BCE, the Silk Road's initiation and spread by China's Han dynasty through exploration and conquests in Central Asia brought new techniques and motifs into China. Soda-lime glass following Mediterranean crafting formulas were found in Han tombs. Carvings of grapevines, camels and merchant caravans can be seen on pictorial bricks. Buddhism also came into China through the Silk Road, and the first Buddhist art emerged.

## Philosophy

The Qin Dynasty's ideology wad [**Fajia**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legalism_(Chinese_philosophy)), which made the Qin’s artistic taste simple and grand, using art to show the empire’s power. Architecture such as the Epang Palace (though only the groundworks were ever constructed) and Qin Shi Huang’s mausoleum were enormous in size.

**Confucianism** was the ideology of the Han dynasty, and the class-based hierarchy of ancient "ritual codes" worked its way into aesthetics. In Han, buildings favored a central-axis symmetrical layout, strict clothing rules told people’s social status apart, and pictorial bricks of horse-and-chariot travel organised characters by social rank. Compared to the pre-Qin Bronze Age, crafts of Han still had plenty of religious elements, but had less theocracy features, and many showed regular and secular life.

The **belief of an afterlife** spawned a significant burial art style. (Most artifacts dug up from the Qin and Han dynasties are burial goods because tombs were sealed tight from the outside.) Burial chambers were big and packed with grave items. The Terracotta Army was built to keep protecting the emperor in the afterlife, so they were made to look extremely realistic. Murals and paintings of Han dynasty tombs features various Chinese myths and mythical beings.

Belief in the **[wuxing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wuxing_(Chinese_philosophy))** heavily affected the symbolism of colors. Qin Shi Huang believed the Qin Dynasty belonged to the "Water Virtue" (水德), and black was the color linked to water. Thus black stood for nobility during Qin. When Emperor Guangwu set the Eastern Han Dynasty as "Fire Virtue" (火德), so red became the color of nobility instead.

### Lacquerware

The iconic color palette of Qin and Han lacquerware is **red** and **black.** Many vessels featured **rounded contours,** and **flared rims** inherited from prior bronze and pottery. The decoration is distinguished by its **abstract, spiraling lines of fluid motion.**

## Fashion

*Under Construction*  
Fashion of the Qin and Han dynasties centred around various forms of ***pao* (袍) robes**. The ***shenyi* (深衣) robe** was the most common and important garment, initially a daily outfit but evolved into formal ceremonial wear. The ***quju (曲裾) shenyi*** was common in the Western Han while the ***zhiju (直裾) shenyi*** was popular in the Eastern Han. Noble women favoured the ***duoma-ji* (堕马髻) hairstyle**, a tilted bun that hangs to one side. Various *[guan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mianguan#Han_dynasty)* hats displayed men's social status and rank. Jacquard brocades with complex structures is common due to advancements in loom technology.
