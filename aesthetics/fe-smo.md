# Feísmo

_Also known as: Canibalismo Urbanístico, Maltrato da Paisaxe_

**Feísmo** is a Galician-language term, roughly translating to "ugly-ism," used to describe a specific urban and architectural phenomenon found throughout Galicia, a region in northwestern Spain. It is also referred to as *Canibalismo Urbanístico* ("Cannibal Urbanism") or *Maltrato da Paisaxe* ("Maltreatment of the Landscape"), with the latter two terms preferred by architects and urban planners who consider "feísmo" too imprecise. One widely cited official definition describes it as "buildings, infrastructures and other human works with a high degree of mediocrity that somehow degrade their environment."

Unlike [Brutalism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Brutalism "Brutalism") or "true" architectural movements, feísmo is not an intentional aesthetic, but the accumulated result of decades of unregulated and unplanned construction. It is documented here because its results are visually consistent and immediately recognizable, producing a distinct built environment that has been extensively photographed, studied, and debated within Galicia. Experts broadly agree that the phenomenon ends at Galicia's administrative borders with Asturias, León, and Portugal, making it a geographically specific occurrence despite superficially similar conditions in neighboring regions.

## History

The roots of feísmo are generally traced to the 1960s, during the period of rapid industrialization and demographic upheaval in Spain known as the [Spanish Miracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_miracle). Galicia's population was undergoing significant emigration, primarily to South America and Western Europe, which meant that many construction projects were begun by families investing remittance income and subsequently abandoned when owners settled permanently abroad or ran out of funds. The result was a landscape increasingly punctuated by half-built concrete shells and structures frozen mid-construction.

Importantly, feísmo is not a product of poverty despite its appareance. Many of the structures associated with it were expensive to build, financed by emigrant savings or rural speculation. Rather, experts attribute it to a confluence of extremely dispersed rural settlement patterns, chronically vague and poorly enforced urban planning legislation, and a cultural attitude best represented by the Galician phrase *ti vai facendo* — loosely meaning "just get on with it" — which encouraged building without formal plans, professional oversight, or clear purpose. As traditional Galician building crafts declined and emigrant workers brought back foreign construction methods, new materials were combined haphazardly with old ones, producing structures that fit neither their local context nor any coherent imported style.

Feísmo as a named problem was recognized early. Galician intellectuals at the *Asemblea Nazonalista de Lugo* of 1918 were among the first to formally note the degradation of the built landscape. In 1994, a group of 48 architects formed an office specifically aimed at rehabilitating feísta structures using traditional Galician materials. By 2003, feísmo had become the single largest category of complaints filed with the *Valedor do Pobo* (Galicia's ombudsman). The first *Foro contra o Feísmo* was held in Ourense in 2004, with a follow-up in 2007. In 2011, the collective Ergosfera launched the project *Eu si quero feísmo na miña paisaxe!* ("I do want feísmo in my landscape!"), a line of research examining what qualities — if any — these structures possess beyond their notoriety.

In 2015, architects Pablo Ramil Regoe and Javier Ferreiro da Costa published a field classification identifying 34 distinct subtypes of feísta construction, ranging from the *Paries Nudum* (bare, unfinished facades) to the *Blasphemia* (tall buildings erected incongruously among low-rise surroundings) and the *Cóctel Galaico* (a combination of two or more feísmo subtypes in a single structure). More recently, some Galician municipalities have begun converting feísta buildings into surfaces for public murals, Carballo being the most cited example, partially reclaiming the built environment through street art.

## Visuals

Feísmo resulted from interruption and clash. The most immediately recognizable image is the unfinished concrete frame: a structure whose walls have been raised but whose upper floors remain open to the sky, with iron rebar protruding from the tops of columns in anticipation of additional storeys that were never added. These skeletal upper floors are a fixture of smaller Galician towns and rural villages, where they sit alongside centuries-old granite farmhouses and *hórreos* (traditional raised granaries), the contrast between weathered stone and raw, stained concrete being among the most photographed aspects of the phenomenon.

Facades in feísta construction are frequently left bare, meaning that exterior walls consist of exposed brick or unrendered cement block, sometimes for decades, while the interior of the building remains inhabited. Where facades are finished, the materials often mismatch: glazed ceramic tiles in colours typical of 1970s and 1980s Spanish domestic interiors applied to rural structures, aluminium window frames set into granite walls, or painted render applied over only part of a surface and left incomplete. The palette that results is dominated by the grey of raw concrete, the rust-brown of oxidising rebar, the dull orange of exposed brick, and the dark, moisture-stained grey of old granite — all against the deep green of Galicia's characteristically wet, Atlantic landscape, which makes the unfinished structures more conspicuous rather than less.

Height discontinuity is another recurring feature. Because urban planning regulations were either absent or unenforced for much of the twentieth century, individual landowners built to whatever height they chose, producing streetscapes in which a two-storey stone house sits directly against an eight-storey concrete block, the party wall of the latter left unrendered and exposed as a *medianera* — a blank concrete cliff face overlooking a neighbour's roof. In rural contexts, the equivalent problem is one of placement: buildings constructed on hillsides, at the edges of riverbeds, or in the middle of agricultural land with no relationship to the surrounding terrain or existing paths of access.

Ornamental excess appears in a distinct subtype — the *Torreón* — in which private homes are built with mock-medieval towers, crenellations, or castle-like stone detailing applied to otherwise unremarkable concrete structures, an expression of the ostentation that some returning emigrants brought back alongside their savings. Garden statuary, including religious figures, eagles, and lions rendered in painted cement, is a related motif documented under the *Aderezos* classification.

## Criticism

### Reception and Debate

Feísmo is a contested term even among those who agree the underlying problem is real. Architectural critics and urban planners associated with the *Maltrato da Paisaxe* platform have consistently argued that "feísmo" is misleading because it frames a systemic planning failure as a matter of taste, potentially placing moral blame on individual builders and families rather than on the legislative and institutional conditions that made such construction possible. In 2020, researchers Xosé Manuel Santos Solla and María de los Ángeles Piñeiro Antelo went further, questioning whether feísmo as a coherent category exists at all, arguing that the debate is "a blaming and self-interested discourse that originates from a media outlet."

A minority position, represented by architect Iago Carro among others, reads certain feísta practices, particularly the *Reciclaxe* subtype, in which found materials such as bed frames are repurposed as field fencing, as a form of vernacular resourcefulness rather than aesthetic failure.

### Aesthetic Appreciation

Although feísmo is widely disliked in Galicia for its perceived degradation of the rural landscape, some scholars and observers have begun to re-examine the phenomenon as an unintended, yet authentic, architectural record of Galicia's socio-economic evolution and the region's overall aesthetic/landscape. This perspective argues against the complete eradication of these structures, suggesting instead that they are a visceral, unplanned gallery of the region's 20th-century history. From this viewpoint, the structures represent a tangible archive of migration, changing economic fortunes, and the transition of rural Galician society, as well as the resilience of the locals, rather than mere architectural failures.

Some critics of the term argue that "*feísmo*" is an elitist construct, often popularized by national media outlets such as El País and La Voz de Galicia. From this perspective, the label is used to marginalize rural working-class aesthetics in favor of a curated, "touristic" vision of Galicia. There is a sense among some residents that the term is used by outsiders, particularly those from centralized power centers like Madrid, as a pejorative way to mock the region's development, failing to recognize that these structures were often the result of immense personal effort by families living through decades of economic uncertainty.
