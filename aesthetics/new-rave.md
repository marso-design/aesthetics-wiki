# New Rave

_Also known as: Nu-Rave, Neu-Rave_

**New Rave** (sometimes written as **Nu-Rave** or **Neu-Rave**) was a short-lived but highly hyped British music scene and subculture that peaked between 2006 and 2008. The term, coined by the music press, is a play on "[New Wave](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/New_Wave "New Wave")" and was used to describe a scene that blended guitar-based indie rock and dance-punk with the aesthetics of 1990s [rave](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rave "Rave") culture. Contrary to its name, the music was not actual rave or hardcore techno, but rather indie rock that incorporated electronic elements like synthesizers, sirens, and danceable beats.

The visual style and fashion involved appropriating the signifiers of rave (such as glowsticks, whistles, and neon or fluorescent clothing) and applying them to a typical [indie](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Indie "Indie") fashion of skinny jeans and band t-shirts. The scene, which included bands like Klaxons, Late of the Pier, Trash Fashion, New Young Pony Club, and Cansei de Ser Sexy (CSS), was known for its hedonistic, DIY, and chaotic live shows.

Despite the initial media hype, the New Rave movement faced significant backlash and was quickly dismissed by many as a joke. Critics argued that the term itself was an inauthentic marketing buzzword manufactured by the music magazine NME, rather than a genuine, organic subculture, cementing its status as a brief but memorable fad.

## History

New Rave emerged in the mid-2000s from the New Cross scene in London as a reaction against the prevailing indie rock climate, dominated by bands like The Libertines, The Rakes, and The Others. The mainstream [indie](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Indie "Indie") scene was considered musically stagnant and overly serious.

Inspired by a desire for something more exciting and inclusive, Klaxons, the band that protagonized the movement, began incorporating elements of electronic music and [rave](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rave "Rave") culture into their sound. Klaxons' founder, Jamie Reynolds, is credited with coining the term "New Rave." Angular Records founder Joe Daniel, who released Klaxons' early singles, helped popularize the term, though even he considered it more of a playful adaptation of existing trends than a distinct genre. Despite their role in its creation, Klaxons later distanced themselves from the New Rave label, calling it a "joke that got out of hand" and even banning glowsticks from their concerts in an attempt to curb the media hype.

The New Rave scene rapidly gained popularity in the United Kingdom thanks to its [DIY ethos](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/DIY_ethos "DIY ethos"), colorful fashion, and energetic club nights. It embraced a diverse range of influences, including house, [electroclash](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Electroclash "Electroclash"), grime, and dubstep, fostering an inclusive atmosphere that welcomed those who felt alienated by the prevailing [indie](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Indie "Indie") scene. This eclecticism contributed to a broader "new rave" dance scene that included artists like LCD Soundsystem, Simian Mobile Disco, Justice, M.I.A., !!! (Chk Chk Chk), and CSS, leading to a resurgence of interest in indie rock and a critical shift in dance music.

Key players in the New Rave scene included DJ collectives like Teens of Thailand, Silvelink, and Str8 Necklin, and designers like Cassette Playa and Carri Munden, who contributed to the scene's visual identity. The scene found its home in warehouse parties and clubs like Foreign, Chalk, and Troubled Minds, where attendees experimented with fashion and danced to eclectic mixes.

However, as New Rave gained mainstream attention, its [underground](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Underground "Underground") spirit started to disappear in favor of commercial appeal. Major labels signed bands like Shitdisco and New Young Pony Club, and the NME launched a "rave" tour featuring Klaxons, CSS, and The Sunshine Underground. This commercialization led to a dilution of the scene's original aesthetic and values. By the time the E4 series *Skins* featured New Rave-inspired fashion, the movement had already turned into a caricature of itself, becoming the butt of the joke in British media. The once-innovative styles were now mass-produced by retailers like Topshop and H&M.

## Visuals

The visual side of New Rave (seen in music videos, promotional materials, etc.) is characterized by high-contrast digital manipulation, 8-bit abstraction, and the aggressive use of CMYK and neon color palettes. Unlike the original [psychedelia](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Psychedelia "Psychedelia") of the 1960s, which was more organic and fluid, New Rave visuals utilize crushed blacks and blown-out highlights to create a "digital grit" effect. This is frequently achieved through heavy saturation and solarization, where skin tones are replaced by vibrant purples, cyans, and yellows, effectively turning human subjects into graphic elements.

Graphic design within the movement relies on lo-fi maximalism. Recurring motifs include pixelated 8-bit sprites, geometric lattices, and mirrored kaleidoscopic patterns that oscillate at high frequencies. Typography is typically bold and sans-serif, often featuring heavy drop shadows or "neon glow" outer strokes. The aesthetic also incorporates [Vectordelia](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Vectordelia "Vectordelia"), a style of graphic design using clean vector-based lines to create complex undulating patterns reminiscent of early computer screensavers or [Op Art](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Op_Art "Op Art").

In video and digital media, the aesthetic utilizes strobe-cutting to synchronize with high-BPM dance music. This is supplemented by the use of chroma-keying (green screening) to place subjects against flat, vibrant backgrounds or repeating GIF-like animations. The result is a flattened, non-linear visual space that prioritizes sensory overload and artificiality over realistic depth or lighting.

## Fashion

New Rave fashion was a high-chroma rejection of the "vagabond" and retro-rock aesthetics prevalent in the mid-2000s indie scene. The aesthetic was defined by the use of fluorescent pigments, light-refracting synthetic fabrics, and a silhouette that blended 1980s sportswear with 1990s club culture. Common motifs of the look included American Apparel lamé leggings, metallic fabrics, and oversized cotton T-shirts featuring bold, high-contrast slogans. Wayfarer-style sunglasses in neon acetates was a primary accessory, frequently paired with "nu-glow" items such as glowsticks and LED-integrated hardware.

The London-based label Cassette Playa, founded by Carri Munden, provided the technical blueprint for the movement’s "high-end" streetwear. Munden’s designs utilized silk prints and luxury fabrics to reinterpret 1990s [rave](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rave "Rave") and gaming iconography, creating a "psychedelic" aesthetic that moved beyond traditional DIY club wear. Similarly, the House of Holland brand popularized oversized T-shirts featuring neon rhyming slogans that referenced British pop culture figures. These garments were often paired with Nikes or high-top sneakers in clashing primary colors.

The movement maintained a strong DIY component rooted in the "[B-Boy](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/B-Boy "B-Boy")" and "[Club Kid](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Club_Kid "Club Kid")" traditions. This involved the manual customization of clothing using found objects, such as cardboard props, baby toys, and repurposed household textiles, notably "bed sheet hoodies" featuring 1980s cartoon motifs. This utilitarian approach allowed the aesthetic to remain accessible to a mainstream audience, as the "look" could be replicated with low-cost surplus gear and neon dyes.

Despite its mass-market adoption, the fashion was characterized by a specific masculine-feminine balance. It replaced the muted denim and leather of the preceding garage rock revival with vibrant hoodies and tracksuits, creating a flamboyant style that emphasized visibility within low-light club environments. By 2007, the aesthetic had shifted from an underground London subculture into a codified retail category, characterized by the "neon and big cartoon chains" look that eventually permeated mainstream fast-fashion outlets.

## Music

### Characteristics

New Rave is a hybrid of indie rock and electronic dance music. The production model is predicated on the integration of high-gain synthesizers and sequenced electronic beats with traditional rock instrumentation, specifically electric guitars and live drums. The movement functioned as a rhythmic response to the mid-2000s indie scene, adopting the high-contrast visual signifiers of the 1980s [Madchester](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Madchester "Madchester") and rave scenes.

The genre's development was linked to the growth of digital music platforms and overlaps with contemporaneous movements such as [Bloghouse](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Bloghouse "Bloghouse") and [Tecktonik](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Tecktonik "Tecktonik").

## Criticism

The primary criticism of New Rave centered on its musical disconnect from 1990s [rave](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rave "Rave") music. Critics and veterans of the original [Acid House](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Acid_House "Acid House") era argued that the movement lacked a genuine relationship to rave culture, classifying it instead as a derivative form of indie rock or dance-punk repackaged for a new demographic. Publications like The Guardian dismissed the genre as a manufactured "youthquake" promoted by magazines such as NME (UK) and TRAX (France) to maintain cultural relevance in the face of declining readership. This media-driven categorization led to accusations that the label was a vacuous fabrication, grouping disparate guitar bands together under a superficial neon umbrella.

The aesthetic's rapid commercialization further alienated music purists and underground clubgoers. By the summer of 2007, the fashion elements of the scene, such as glow sticks, shutter shades, and Day-Glo hoodies, had transitioned from East London warehouse parties to mainstream high-street retailers. This rapid retail saturation resulted in New Rave being mocked as a triumph of garish style over musical substance. Critics frequently criticized the subculture's demographic, characterizing its participants as affluent, middle-class youth appropriating working-class club culture traditions for a cynical and trend-conscious "costume party."

The intense backlash against New Rave ultimately turned the term into a toxic brand within the music industry. To avoid the stigma of a passing fad, the genre's founding acts consciously abandoned the fluorescent aesthetic for their sophomore releases; groups like Hadouken! shifted to monochrome visuals, while Klaxons moved toward psychedelia. Despite its rapid burnout, retrospective analyses frequently describe New Rave as the final localized and organically messy British youth movement to emerge before social media and trend-forecasting algorithms completely accelerated the lifecycle of subcultures.
