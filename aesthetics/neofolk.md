**Neofolk** is a post-industrial music subculture that emerged in the United Kingdom during the early 1980s out of [post-punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Post-punk "Post-punk") and [industrial](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Industrial "Industrial") music. It uses acoustic instrumentation, primarily acoustic guitars, piano, and strings, to explore European traditionalism, paganism, and the historical trauma of the 20th century. Where conventional folk music tends to preserve national custom, neofolk instead uses the acoustic folk form to stage apocalyptic lament and a rejection of modern, rationalist society, drawing heavily on 19th-century [Romanticism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Romanticism "Romanticism") and early 20th-century anti-modernist philosophy.

## History

Neofolk originated within the London post-industrial scene, through a circle of musicians who had previously worked in [punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Punk "Punk") and [industrial](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Industrial "Industrial") projects. Douglas Pearce and Tony Wakeford, both former members of the leftist punk band Crisis, formed Death in June in 1981, initially blending post-punk basslines with militaristic drumming. By the mid-1980s, alongside David Tibet of Current 93, they moved away from electronic instrumentation toward a sound sometimes described using terms including "apocalyptic folk," "folk-noir," and "dark folk," though these are not consistently treated as distinct subgenres so much as overlapping labels for the same movement. Pearce's own politics moved during this period toward the "Strasserite" wing of Nazism, an anti-capitalist strand of fascism built on opposition to both economic inequality and cosmopolitanism, and Death in June's use of Nazi-appropriated pagan symbols was constructed to sit ambiguously between irony and sincerity.

Tony Wakeford's subsequent project Sol Invictus, formed after his departure from Death in June, became one of the genre's foundational acts in its own right, combining the philosophy of Julius Evola and the German Conservative Revolution with Tolkien, Nordic paganism, and medieval iconography, in albums constructed so that their underlying message could pass unnoticed by a listener not looking for it. This approach reflected a broader current within the genre's founding generation, one scholar of the far right, Anton Shekhovtsov, terms "apoliteic": following Evola, an orientation that treats the struggle for political power as secondary to reshaping an individual's sense of identity and values, without requiring formal party politics. A parallel strand of French far-right philosophy from the late 1960s, sometimes called the European New Right, described a similar approach as "metapolitics," aiming to shift cultural identity as a route to shifting politics down the line.

Because these ideas were embedded rather than stated outright, and because Death in June's commercial success made fascist-associated neofolk highly visible within the wider scene, the genre developed a reputation, contested to this day, for being not merely infiltrated but founded by the far right. This produced an early and lasting split within the scene: some artists and audiences engaged with the ambiguous imagery in earnest, while others formed in explicit opposition to it. French [martial industrial](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Martial_Industrial "Martial Industrial") musician Eric Roger left Sol Invictus to found Gae Bolg once the band's continued alignment with organized racist politics became clear, and artist Sieben released the antifascist track "Rite Against the Right" in direct response to the growing fascination with fascist occultism in the scene.

By the 1990s, the center of the subculture shifted to continental Europe, particularly Germany, where neofolk combined British post-industrial aesthetics with German [Romanticism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Romanticism "Romanticism") and [Expressionism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Expressionism "Expressionism"). The Wave-Gotik-Treffen festival in Leipzig became a major venue for the genre during this period. This era also saw heathenry, the Nordic pagan revival built around the Æsir and Vanir pantheons, become closely associated with the genre; a "folkish" interpretation that treats the gods as ethnic archetypes specific to people of Northern European descent gained real traction among far-right adherents, prompting artists such as Wardruna to state explicitly that their music has no connection to ethnonationalist readings of the tradition.

## Visuals

Neofolk imagery is high-contrast and largely monochromatic, pairing archaic symbols with the physical remains of the 20th century's wars. The two most common motifs are runic alphabets, particularly the Algiz and Sowilo runes, and medieval heraldry, both used to signal an identity that predates industrialization. These are frequently placed alongside photographs of war memorials, statues, and military graveyards from the two World Wars. The photography frequently uses grain and film artifacts reminiscent of 1920s German [Expressionist](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Expressionism "Expressionism") cinema, placing the imagery of the past visibly on top of the present.

## Fashion

Neofolk fashion avoids the theatricality of [Goth](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Goth "Goth") in favor of a disciplined, uniform-based look that combines military surplus with the plainer dress of early-to-mid-20th-century intellectual life.

Standard attire includes field jackets in olive drab or field grey, combat boots, and military-grade canvas bags, often paired with turnleneck sweaters, double-breasted trench coats, and high-waisted trousers styled after early 20th-century philosophers and soldiers. Accessories are minimal: silver or iron runic jewelry, and historical military insignia such as the Totenkopf, worn deliberately to provoke a reaction rather than for decoration.

## Philosophy

Neofolk's worldview centers on a rejection of the modern world, often citing Julius Evola's *Revolt Against the Modern World* as a reference point. Its adherents argue that the Enlightenment and industrialization stripped life of its sacred dimension — a state Evola's own tradition calls "desacralization" — and that neofolk's revival of archaic symbol and myth is a response to that loss. This is paired with a belief that the essential character of Europe was destroyed in the wars of the 20th century.

The movement describes its own stance using the term "apoliteic": deeply engaged with political history and symbolism while refusing to align with any contemporary party. In practice this has produced a subculture that treats obscure history, traditionalist philosophy, and esoteric spirituality as markers of insider status, and that positions itself against what it sees as the shallowness of mainstream culture.

## Music

Neofolk is built on acoustic minimalism layered with experimental post-industrial texture. The primary instrument is the acoustic guitar, usually played in a repetitive strummed pattern that favors mood over technical display, layered with field recordings of wind, bells, or historical speech samples, and strings, to build a dense atmosphere.

Vocal delivery ranges from whispered spoken word to melodic baritone, generally low-toned and melancholic. Lyrically the genre returns to personal mysticism, historical tragedy, and the return of "old gods." Current 93 draws on nursery rhymes and Christian Gnosticism, while Death in June centers on Euro-nihilism and militaristic lament, producing music that reads as both intimate and vast in scope.

## Criticism

Neofolk is a subject of ongoing controversy over its use of symbols closely associated with 20th-century fascist movements: camouflage, runes, and military iconography. Some artists and defenders of the scene frame this imagery as deliberate provocation aimed at censorship and prevailing political sensibilities, though this justification is itself disputed rather than settled fact. That ambiguity has, in practice, attracted actual far-right participants into the scene, producing a documented and continuing backlash rather than an overreaction. While many artists maintain an "art over politics" stance, the scene remains split between factions moving toward militant traditionalism and others, such as the "A Blaze Ansuz" movement, actively trying to reclaim the runes for anti-fascist use.
