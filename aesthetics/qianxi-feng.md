**Qianxi Feng** (千禧风) refers to the most prevalent fashion styles in China during the 2000s. It blended international trends with local aesthetics. Rooted in casual comfort, they expressed individuality through bold colors, tailoring and styling. Fashion in this era not only reflected how China aligned itself with global fashion after joining the WTO, but also embodied the shifting values of the post-80s and post-90s generation in their pursuit of self-expression.

### Terminology

The aesthetic featured on this page, the mainstream fashion aesthetic of 2000s China, had no dedicated name when it was popular. On Chinese social media, the 2000s aesthetic itself is simply called "Y2K", a term that emerged alongside "Beauty of the Boom Years" (see subgenres) in post-2020 Chinese usage. In English, however, [Y2K](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Y2K "Y2K") is a broad term for all 2000s-era aesthetics. Thus, this wiki page uses a transliteration of the less used but also recognized term 千禧风.

## Subgenres

**Beauty of the Boom Years** (经济上行期的美), also known as **Boom-Era Nostalgia** in English, is a 2020s trending concept on Rednote (Xiaohongshu) and other Chinese social media that romanticizes the optimism, consumption, and visual exuberance associated with the period of rapid national and global economic growth of the 2000s.

The term **Beauty of the Boom Years** has gained traction among young Chinese users who curate vintage ads, Y2K fashion, and aspirational lifestyles as a nostalgic antidote to contemporary uncertainty.

## Criticism

Criticisms of 2000s aesthetics and fashion itself argue that it was by no means the diverse, open, and vibrant scene people often refer to today. Back then, the era was flooded with vulgar remarks objectifying women and twisted, decadent values. Many women dressed and groomed themselves solely to please others. Public aesthetic awareness had yet to awaken, and the general public was forced to conform to a single, rigid set of aesthetic norms. This was particularly pronounced when it came to body standards: the saying “There are no ugly women, only lazy ones” was pervasive, while the pursuit of “chopstick legs” and “A4 waists” became rampant, and weight-loss "snake oil" products sold extremely well.

The Boom-Era Nostalgia is criticised for being a marketing tool for influencers and brands to sell "boom-era vibes" for profit. Only profitable, easy-to-sell aesthetics are promoted, while working-class cultural memories are ignored because they do not generate capital.

The Boom-Era Nostalgia is criticized as a narrative hegemony. It tricks people into longing for the boom's return, but rapid economic growth is not the historical norm. Economic upswings/downswings do not guarantee individual opportunity: a boom does not mean you will succeed, and a downturn does not mean you have no chance. "Groups that did not fairly share in the dividends of high growth during the previous boom are now, in turn, being coerced into joining the collective nostalgia for the Beauty of the Boom Era."
