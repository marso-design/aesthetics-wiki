# Four Colors

**Four Colors** was a frequently used color scheme found in consumer products and consumer technology during the Mid-[2000s](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Category:2000s "Category:2000s") to the Mid-[2010s](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Category:2010s "Category:2010s"), first primarily seen around 2004 as a part of Apple's iPod "Silhouette" advertising campaign. It is a subgenre of [Superflat Pop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Superflat_Pop "Superflat Pop") and [Frutiger Aero](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Frutiger_Aero "Frutiger Aero") and prominently consists of the colors electric lime, sky blue, hot pink and neon orange. This color palette and the visuals of the aesthetic overlaps with [Bright Tertiaries](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Bright_Tertiaries "Bright Tertiaries"), which uses tertiary colors like teal, lime green, and orange or fuchsia, as well as flat humanist designs. **Four Colors** is popular in Japan with stores and brands such as Daiso, Sony and Fujifilm.

## History

### Origins (Late-1990s/Early-2000s)

**Four Colors**' roots dates back to the Late-[1990s](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Category:1990s "Category:1990s"), forming as parts of [Superflat Pop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Superflat_Pop "Superflat Pop"), [Y2K Futurism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Y2K_Futurism "Y2K Futurism"), and primitive [Frutiger Aero](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Frutiger_Aero "Frutiger Aero"). During this primitive period, **Four Colors** was primarily used for different-color variants of video game consoles, primarily the [Nintendo 64](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_64).

### Prime (2000s)

**Four Colors**' prime began in October 2003 when Apple began the "Silhouette" iPod advertising campaign. Apple reportedly spent $49.6 million on the ‘‘Silhouette’’ campaign between January and August of 2004, being majorly influential on **Four Colors**' widespread success and adoption in [the West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world). **Four Colors** continued to be heavily popular during the "[Frutiger](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Category:Frutiger_Family)" time period, generally seeing increased use in consumer technology (eg. [iPod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPod), [Wii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wii), [PlayStation 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PlayStation_2), [3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PlayStation_3), etc.) as well as miscellaneous advertising.

### Decline (Mid 2010s)

**Four Colors** continued to be popular until the mid-[2010](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Category:2010s "Category:2010s")s, when the [Frutiger Family](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Category:Frutiger_Family "Category:Frutiger Family") of aesthetics were in decline. Companies began focusing more on [Flat Design](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Flat_Design "Flat Design") and being uniform, omitting the personalization and eye-catching color combinations. By 2017, [Flat Design](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Flat_Design "Flat Design") and other aesthetics such as [Corporate Memphis](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Corporate_Memphis "Corporate Memphis") had completely phased out **Four Colors**, giving way to a new era.

### Japan (Post-2014/15)

**Four Colors** still sees varied degrees of success in Japan and is still relatively popular despite its decline in [the West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world).

## Visuals

The visuals of **Four Colors** consist of flat visuals that predominantly use four colors: Electric lime, sky blue, hot pink, neon orange; **Four Colors**' color palette is also noted as being a reference to the colors of different seasons in Japan, specifically: Pink = spring, green = summer, orange = autumn, and blue = winter. **Four Colors** can also host a wider range of colors too, forming a rainbow. However, the main colors are usually the ones mentioned above. These visuals are often utilized in conjunction with consumer products or corporate designs. **Four Colors** is also sometimes featured in video game box-art.
