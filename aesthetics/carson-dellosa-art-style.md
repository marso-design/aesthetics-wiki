# Carson-Dellosa Art Style

_Also known as: 90s Classroom Art_

The **Carson-Dellosa Art Style** (also informally known as **90s Classroom Art**) is an aesthetic associated with the classroom decorations and educational materials produced by the Carson-Dellosa Publishing Company. Although it began as a practical tool for primary school teachers, it is recognized today as a distinct aesthetic characterized by its highly consistent standarized character designs. The style is primarily synonymous with the 1980s, 1990s, and early 2000s educational zeitgeist in North America.

## History

The aesthetic was created in 1976 by Patti Carson and Janet Dellosa, two elementary school teachers in Ohio who began creating game-based teaching aids in their basement. After their work was discovered by the publishing firm McMillan at a trade show, the company expanded significantly, eventually moving to North Carolina and hiring a team of illustrators. These new artists were strictly trained to replicate the founders' original drawing style to maintain brand consistency across hundreds of products. By the late 1990s, the company was a dominant force in the educational market and was sold for $22 million in cash and $16 million in stock.

The style represents a shift in late-20th-century pedagogy toward positive affirmation and kindness. The illustrations frequently featured diverse characters, including the early inclusion of mobility devices like wheelchairs, as part of an effort to normalize disability within public education spaces following the passage of the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act.

### Contemporary Reception

In the 2020s, the style transitioned from a functional classroom background to a subject of online criticism and nostalgia. Discussions on platforms like Tumblr in 2015 began questioning the origin of the "same face" used on nearly every school decoration. By 2019, social media users successfully identified the Carson-Dellosa Publishing Company as the source of the imagery.

In 2022, the style became a prominent meme after Twitter users identified it as an art style that lacks personality or provokes irritation. This led to a wave of parody artwork where users "redrew" popular media characters or classical paintings, such as Saturn Devouring His Son, using the strict Carson Dellosa facial rules.

## Visuals

The most recognizable element is the "Carson-Dellosa face," which follows a rigid anatomical canon. Characters typically possess oblong eyes where the pupils sit at the bottom and turn outward, while the tops of the eyes turn inward toward the nose. These eyes are accented by two small high eyebrows that convey a state of perpetual surprise. The nose is almost always a shiny pink oval, and the mouth is an exaggeratedly rounded, thin smile that often includes small half-circles to indicate cheeks.

The aesthetic is applied universally across the Carson-Dellosa catalog, giving inanimate objects like seasons, holidays, and school supplies the same eager simpering expression. Thick black outlines, flat or subtly shaded primary colors, and open waving body language are intended to appear welcoming and non-threatening to children. This uniform look creates a world where every element, from a snowman to a pencil, shares a singular unvarying emotional state.
