**Shanzhai** (山寨) is an aesthetic that involves contemporary use that usually encompasses counterfeit, imitation, or parody products and events and the subculture surrounding them.

A specific symbol of Shanzhai are **phones**, which is why Shanzhai is sometimes associated only with phones.

## Origin

The term *shanzhai* was first used for its literal meaning, which referred to defensible mountain forts and strongholds, usually in areas on the outer reaches of imperial government control. During the [Song dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_dynasty) (960–1279), *shanzhai* came to describe groups of bandits who opposed and evaded the corrupt authorities to perform deeds they saw as justified. One of the most well known uses of *shanzhai* in this way is in the story *[Water Margin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_Margin).*

*Shanzhai* goods are often regarded as rebelling against the established commercial market, adopting the spirit of opposition and individuality that the term was originally associated with during the Song dynasty. Some *shanzhai* products are created with the intent to deceive buyers, although others are created with features not included in their respective authentic counterparts, or are created out of jest or parody (such as the [CCSTV New Year's Gala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CCSTV_New_Year%27s_Gala)). Unlike counterfeiters, producers of *shanzhai* goods typically do not conceal the nature of their product and purchasers are generally aware of the true nature of the goods.

The term "shanzhai" emerged around 2005, when several Chinese mobile phone manufacturers began developing original circuits and software embedded in phones whose appearance imitated that of major brands. As a phenomenon, it arises from the confluence of local factors ([Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China) culture, its laws and regulations) and international factors (the law of supply and demand in a globalized market). One of its most notable aspects is offering solutions tailored to [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China) consumers, such as carbonated drinks tailored to local tastes, while simultaneously reducing [production costs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_theory_of_value). This is made possible by manufacturers' in-depth knowledge of market demands and the country's industrial potential.

This technological environment, a driver of innovation and based primarily in the city of [Shenzhen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shenzhen), has given rise to a number of successful companies that were able to begin production by circumventing [intellectual property laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_property) and still offer original products. However, by March 2011, an estimated one-third of the city's phone companies, which were involved in making phones, were forced to close. This was partly due to falling prices of major brand phones, as well as the Chinese government's initiative to strengthen protection of local innovation and [international copyright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authors%27_rights).

## History

The reforms initiated by [Deng Xiaoping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deng_Xiaoping) in 1978 transformed [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China) into a "world factory," a system later dubbed "[Socialism with Chinese Characteristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism_with_Chinese_characteristics)." The creation of [special economic zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_economic_zone) (especially in [Shenzhen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shenzhen)) provided an infrastructure base where thousands of small workshops gained access to technology and assembly lines.

Shanzhai became a byproduct of this goal and lack of regulation at that time: workers who had learned to assemble Western technology began creating their own versions. However, unlike simple copies, Shanzhai gadgets often featured features not found in the originals (such as dual-SIM phones or high-power speakers), adapting them to the needs of the lower-income population.

## Visual

Most Shanzhai visuals come out of, no surprise, mainland China, which has had very loose enforcement of copyright laws, especially in the 2000s-2010s. Oftentimes they will take on the appearance of popular Western brands with some minor changes to not only convince locals that it's just as good as the Western brands, but also potentially give Western tourists something familiar to give them a little taste of home.

Shanzhai is constantly connected with [Kitsch](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Kitsch "Kitsch"), Bootleg Aesthetic, as well as with the aesthetics of the Asian market, Chinese market, [New Ugly](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/New_Ugly "New Ugly"), Shirpotryob and Chinese-World/New Silk Road Market (all may have a temporary name, excluding [New Ugly](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/New_Ugly "New Ugly")), their subgenres and in general often with the aesthetics of the market, which also connects it to Hong Kong Retro and Red-white-blue bag. The Chinese kitsch component makes it similar to [Fotonight Web](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Fotonight_Web "Fotonight Web").

A slightly similar concept in Russia: **Brendy-Oborotni** (Werewolf Brands).

## Fashion

Shanzhai fashion consists of bootleg versions of popular luxury fashion brands (Nike, Gucci, Hermes, Louis Vuitton, Cartier). If one wants to go for that [Boujee](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Boujee "Boujee") or [Hypebeast](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hypebeast "Hypebeast") look, but doesn't have a budget for name brands, the Shanzhai aesthetic can serve as an alternative, especially as Shanzhai variants become more and more indistinguishable from the genuine products they are based on.

In the post-Soviet space, counterfeit Adidas and other brands in general are known by the common name **Abibas**.
