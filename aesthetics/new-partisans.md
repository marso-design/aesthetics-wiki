# New Partisans

_Also known as: Novi Partizani_

[Sensitive Content Notice](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Aesthetics_Wiki:Content_Warning_Policy "Aesthetics Wiki:Content Warning Policy") ⚠️

The following article contains and discusses content that may be distressing to some readers.  
**Reason for Warning:** This page documents a music movement that deliberately appropriates and romanticizes the imagery of the Yugoslav Partisan struggle of World War II. The content features wartime imagery, militaristic fashion, and socialist political symbols (e.g., the red star and Socialist Realist art) to address underlying ethnic and political tensions in former Yugoslavia. Viewer discretion is advised.

The **New Partisans** (Serbo-Croatian: *Novi Partizani* / Нови Партизани) were a short-lived music and visual movement on the Yugoslav rock scene during the mid-1980s. Centered in Sarajevo, the movement was led by the established band Bijelo Dugme and younger acts Plavi Orkestar and Merlin.

The aesthetic is defined by a blend of mainstream pop rock with Balkan folk music, but its most distinctive feature was its heavy use of lyrics and imagery inspired by the Yugoslav Partisans of World War II and the state ideology of "Brotherhood and Unity." At a time of rising ethnic nationalism in post-Tito Yugoslavia, the New Partisans movement was a deliberately pro-Yugoslav, anti-nationalist cultural statement.

## History

The New Partisans emerged in the mid-1980s as a reaction to the perceived "emptiness" of the Yugoslav state’s socialist narrative. Following the death of Josip Broz Tito, the promises of socio-economic liberation and multi-ethnic equality were increasingly viewed by the youth as unfulfilled or vapid. This "hollowness" in the official national identity coincided with a rapid shift toward Western commodity culture and consumerism, which the movement’s proponents regarded as inauthentic and culturally disingenuous.

In response, bands from the Sarajevo scene, most notably Bijelo Dugme and Plavi Orkestar, sought to articulate what sociologist Dalibor Mišina defines as "original Yugoslavism." This was not a prescriptive endorsement of the ruling League of Communists, but an attempt to reclaim the "authentic" and "organic" roots of the Yugoslav identity from a failing institutional framework. By re-appropriating the romanticized and hyper-masculine imagery of the anti-fascist Partisan struggle, the movement positioned the shared memory of World War II as a radical alternative to both the encroaching Western consumerism and the rising tide of local ethno-nationalism.

The movement represented a cultural "bridge" between the era of Titoist state socialism and the eventual collapse of the republic. Performances and lyrics, such as those in Bijelo Dugme's "*Pljuni i zapjevaj moja Jugoslavijo*" (Spit and Sing, My Yugoslavia), utilized "radical Yugoslavism" to demand a return to the country's founding principles of brotherhood. However, as the political climate shifted toward the ethno-nationalist politics of the 1990s, the New Partisans' attempt to foster a unified, trans-ethnic identity through pop culture was ultimately marginalized. By 1988, the movement had largely dissolved as its key figures moved toward more conventional musical themes or localized national identities.

## Visuals

The visual art associated with the New Partisans movement was primarily codified through album sleeve design and the recontextualization of political iconography. This practice relied on the juxtaposition of Yugoslav socialist heritage with Western pop culture and classical art. Bijelo Dugme established the visual precedent in 1984 by utilizing Uroš Predić’s 1919 Romanticist painting, *The Kosovo Maiden*, as cover art. Their 1986 release, *Pljuni i zapjevaj moja Jugoslavijo*, shifted toward international leftist aesthetics, featuring a photograph of a Chinese socialist realist ballet.

Other prominent acts utilized parody and historical montage to define the movement's aesthetic boundaries. Plavi Orkestar’s 1985 debut, *Soldatski bal*, functioned as a direct visual appropriation of Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band, substituting the original figures with a curated assembly of Yugoslav historical personalities. In 1986, Merlin utilized a split-cover design for *Teško meni sa tobom*, placing a portrait of Marilyn Monroe in direct opposition to the Kozarčanka; the widely circulated World War II photograph of partisan fighter Milja Marin. These aesthetic choices functioned as a deliberate blurring of the lines between state-sanctioned heroism and commercial stardom.

## Fashion

The movement's fashion moved away from standard 1980s rock attire toward a militarized, folk-influenced silhouette. Performers utilized modified World War II-era uniforms and surplus army gear as primary stage garments. Key components of the wardrobe included heavy-duty military boots and *partizanke*; traditional hand-knitted wool socks historically associated with the Yugoslav Partisans.

The silhouette emphasized a "soldier-of-the-people" aesthetic, often incorporating stylized peaked caps and tunics that mirrored the Yugoslav People's Army (JNA) dress codes. The fashion was supplemented by the use of state symbols as accessories, such as the red star (*petokraka*). This aesthetic presentation was reinforced during live events through the integration of acoustic signals, including the broadcasting of "The Internationale" or the choreographed shouting of the state motto, "*Bratstvo i jedinstvo*" (Brotherhood and Unity), which functioned as a performative element of the band's style.

## Music

### Characteristics

The movement's music was a combination of mainstream pop rock and power pop with prominent elements of traditional Balkan folk music. The sound was melodic and accessible, but it was set apart by its lyrical content and the incorporation of historical musical themes. Bands often adapted melodies from old Yugoslav revolutionary anthems and Partisan songs, most notably Bijelo Dugme's cover of "Padaj silo i nepravdo" ("Fall, Oh Force and Injustice"). The lyrics were central to the movement, focusing on themes of Yugoslavism, the state motto of "Brotherhood and Unity," pacifism, and a romanticized nostalgia for the anti-fascist struggle of World War II.

## Criticism

The New Partisans movement encountered significant opposition from the Yugoslav music press and the more alternative-oriented public, primarily regarding the aesthetic's perceived lack of intellectual rigor. A major point of contention was the fusion of mainstream rock with folk motifs, which critics dismissed as [kitsch](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Kitsch "Kitsch"). This stylistic synthesis was frequently compared to "newly-composed folk music" (*novokomponovana narodna muzika*), a state-promoted genre often dismissed by the urban intelligentsia as a signifier of poor taste. New Partisan bands were accused of diluting the subversive potential of rock music into a commercially palatable and state-adjacent product by adopting these rural and populist elements.

Ethical and political hypocrisy was a secondary reason for the movement's negative reception. Critics argued that the "Partisan offensive" was a cynical marketing strategy rather than a real ideological commitment. Established, affluent musicians were seen as exploiting sensitive socialist and patriotic symbols (such as the *Kozarčanka* and the red star) to secure commercial longevity during a period of economic instability. This appropriation of revolutionary iconography by high-earning pop stars led to comparisons with [Communist Chic](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Communist_Chic "Communist Chic"), where the aesthetics of struggle were stripped of their radical utility and repurposed as fashion.

The movement's decline was accelerated by the deteriorating political climate in Yugoslavia. As the "Brotherhood and Unity" narrative lost its cultural hegemony, the movement's reliance on state symbols became increasingly untenable. By 1988, the primary proponents of New Partisans had abandoned the militarized folk-rock style in favor of more conventional pop or emerging nationalistic themes, effectively ending the movement’s presence in the Yugoslav zeitgeist.
