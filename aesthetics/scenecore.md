# Scenecore

_Also known as: Colorpuke_

[Sensitive Content Notice](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Aesthetics_Wiki:Content_Warning_Policy "Aesthetics Wiki:Content Warning Policy") ⚠️

The following article contains and discusses content that may be distressing to some readers.  
**Reason for Warning:** This page contains high-saturation imagery that may pose a photosensitivity risk. It also includes documented accounts of artist misconduct and lyrical themes involving references to self-harm, drug abuse, eating disorders, and interpersonal violence within the Scenecore music scene. Reader discretion is advised for the Music and Criticism sections.

**Scenecore** is an internet aesthetic and musical microgenre that emerged in the early 2020s. Rooted in the underground [Hyperpop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hyperpop "Hyperpop") SoundCloud scene, the movement appropriates the visual motifs of the 2000s [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene") subculture for a contemporary Gen Z audience. Whereas the original Scene subculture relied on physical gatherings and strict dress codes (specifically heavily hairsprayed and choppy hairstyles), Scenecore developed primarily through short-form video on TikTok and avatar customization in online games such as *Roblox*.

The style utilizes a maximalist pastiche of early 2000s internet culture, combining neon [Kandi](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Kandi_Raver "Kandi Raver") accessories and rainbow checkerboard patterns with [glitter graphics](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Glitter_Graphics "Glitter Graphics") (Blingee and Picmix) and meme culture (e.g., Epic Face and Nyan Cat). Both musically and philosophically, Scenecore separates itself from its 2000s predecessor by incorporating electronic production and maintaining an explicit rejection of "[cringe culture](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Cringe_Culture "Cringe Culture")." This worldview encourages the unironic display of previously stigmatized online interests, constituting a distinct internet subculture rather than a direct historical continuation of [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene").

## History

### Musical Origins (2019–2020)

Scenecore emerged as a musical microgenre between 2019 and early 2020, evolving directly from the underground Digicore, [Hyperpop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hyperpop "Hyperpop"), and Cloud Rap scenes on platforms such as SoundCloud. The genre was pioneered by internet-native artists like Kid Trash and h3artcrush, who began releasing tracks that were aggressive lo-fi reinterpretations of 2000s Crunkcore and Electro Power Pop.

The early production methodology relied heavily on interpolation. Producers sampled defining anthems from the original Scene and [Rave](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rave "Rave") eras, subjecting them to extreme digital distortion and bitcrushing. These sped-up, distorted melodies were then layered over high-velocity trap drum patterns and heavy kicks. Thematically, early Scenecore was intentionally hyperbolic, designed to exaggerate the darker aspects of 2000s internet culture. Lyrical content established a fixation on shock value, utilizing recurring motifs of vampirism, graphic self-harm, substance abuse, and intense digital isolation. Rather than a sincere continuation of the 2000s [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene") lifestyle, early Scenecore was a hyper-fixated, terminally online parody of it.

### Popularization (2020–Present)

As the COVID-19 pandemic began in early 2020, a broader wave of "[Y2K](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Y2K_Futurism "Y2K Futurism")" and "[McBling](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/McBling "McBling")" nostalgia began circulating on social media, occasionally referred to as the "Roaring 2020s" [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene") revival movement. During this period, the Scenecore musical microgenre collided with this growing nostalgia. The aesthetic rapidly migrated from niche SoundCloud production circles into algorithmic virality on platforms like TikTok.

This migration transitioned Scenecore from a localized music scene into a highly popular internet aesthetic. Unlike the original Scene subculture, which was heavily tied to physical gatherings at malls and specific salon hairstyles, Scenecore's style was developed primarily through digital avatar customization and short-form video. The aesthetic became closely associated with the Roblox community, not only through avatar customization but through the popularity of games like *Regretevator*. These games mirrored the Scenecore aesthetic through surrealist humor, chaotic "roulette" gameplay, and character designs featuring vibrant high-contrast colors and "unpleasant" gradients.

The "internet-first" nature of this revival altered the fashion constraints of the original [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene") subculture. Because the aesthetic was popularized by a younger generation during periods of lockdown, the high-maintenance hairsprayed styles and tight silhouettes of the 2000s were largely replaced by more accessible alternatives. Participants adopted baggier clothing, modern fast-fashion interpretations of [alternative](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Alternative "Alternative") fashion, and lower-maintenance hairstyles such as the wolf cut.

## Visuals

In digital edits and artwork, Scenecore is a maximalist pastiche of late 2000s and early 2010s internet culture, often overlapping with [Webcore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Webcore "Webcore") and [Glitchcore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Glitchcore "Glitchcore"). The most prominent recurring motif is the rainbow-colored checkerboard pattern, frequently utilized alongside neon animal prints, paint drips, and pixelated glitch textures. The aesthetic relies heavily on low-fidelity digital artifacts and image editing styles from the era, specifically incorporating [glitter graphics](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Glitter_graphics "Glitter graphics"), Blingees, and PicMix collages. The style also features a specific set of pop-culture iconography. While Scenecore retains characters like GIR from *Invader Zim* and Hello Kitty from the original [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene") subculture, it expands this roster to heavily feature Hatsune Miku, as well as characters from series like *My Little Pony* and *Monster High*.

## Fashion

Scenecore fashion represents a 2020s evolution of the original [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene") style, heavily influenced by contemporary internet trends such as [2020 Alt](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/2020_Alt "2020 Alt"), [E-Girl](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/E-Girl "E-Girl"), and [Animecore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Animecore "Animecore"). The wardrobe prioritizes a maximalist approach to layering, commonly featuring distressed skinny jeans or leggings worn under tutus and pleated skirts. Tops frequently consist of graphic t-shirts layered over long-sleeve striped shirts, alongside hoodies featuring animal ears or all-over prints. Footwear typically includes platform boots, colorful high-top sneakers, and decorated clogs.

Accessorizing is a common component of the style, characterized by the heavy accumulation of [Kandi](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Kandi "Kandi") bracelets, layered studded belts, chunky plastic jewelry, and colorful hair clips. The fashion also incorporates prominent [Kidcore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Kidcore "Kidcore") influences, utilizing items such as pacifiers, perler bead necklaces, and plushie backpacks. In contrast to the rigidly hairsprayed styles of the 2000s that were associated with [Emo](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Emo "Emo") and the original [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene") subculture, Scenecore hair allows for varied textures and cuts, including split-dye colors and wolf cuts. Makeup application routinely involves heavy colorful eyeshadow, drawn-on faux freckles, and intentionally smeared or graphic eyeliner.

## Philosophy

Scenecore's worldview is based around rejecting "[cringe culture](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Cringe_culture "Cringe culture")," a pervasive internet behavioral trend characterized by the mass harassment and mockery of niche hobbies, neurodivergent traits, and earnest self-expression. Because the primary demographic of Scenecore came of age during the peak of this harassment, the aesthetic is a deliberate reclamation of interests previously stigmatized as socially unacceptable. This includes the unironic integration of alterhuman identities, [furry](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Furry "Furry") subculture motifs (such as sparkledogs), hyper-specific media fandoms, and unapologetic LGBTQ+ pride (frequently manifested through the prominent display of pride flags) into their daily presentation. The phrase "cringe culture is dead" is a recurring slogan within the community, establishing an environment that prioritizes radical sincerity and maximalist self-expression over mainstream social conformity.

## Music

### Characteristics

Scenecore music is a derivative of [Hyperpop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hyperpop "Hyperpop"), Nightcore, and [Hands Up](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hands_Up "Hands Up"), integrating the high-BPM templates of original 2000s [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene") music with modern production. This style relies on aggressively pitched-up vocals, heavily distorted synthesizers, and the frequent incorporation of video game and anime audio samples. Lyrical themes deviate from the original [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene") bands by utilizing explicit and hyperbolic references to severe mental illness, drug abuse, gore, and physical violence for shock value.

Initial development in 2019 and 2020 was driven by SoundCloud artists such as h3artcrush, Rebzyyx, 15forevr, Kid Trash, and Xaxanity. The 2020 release of "*0n the fl00r*" by h3artcrush established the genre's early conventions, followed closely by collaborative tracks like "*Sacrifice*" by 15forevr and h3artcrush, and "*BLOOD! BLOOD! BLOOD!*" by Xaxanity. by 2023, the production methodology expanded to heavily sample late-2000s mainstream pop and Eurodance tracks. The genre frequently overlaps with adjacent microgenres such as [Krushclub](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Krushclub "Krushclub") and [Sigilkore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Sigilkore "Sigilkore").

## Criticism

### Relationship with Scene and Distinctions

The emergence of Scenecore has resulted in significant friction with members of the original [Scene](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Scene "Scene") subculture, predominantly Millennials who participated in the style between 2005 and 2014. This critique stems from the belief that Scenecore misunderstands and commodifies the original subculture's identity, leading to intense hostility.

While original Scene was based around a specific physical dress codes, most notably the heavily teased, "choppy" layered hair and raccoon-style eyeliner, Scenecore is a maximalist visual pastiche. It draws heavily from [Webcore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Webcore "Webcore") and [Y2K revivals](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Neo-Y2K "Neo-Y2K"), prioritizing digital art, modern internet culture, PicMix compositions, and "blingee" graphics over a singular physical hairstyle.

The two movements also diverge in their underlying ethos. The original Scene movement was often criticized for its insular nature and the frequent labeling of outsiders as "posers." In contrast, Scenecore is largely defined by the rejection of "[cringe culture](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Cringe_culture "Cringe culture")," leading to a more inclusive overlap with [furry](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Furry "Furry"), alterhuman, and LGBTQ+ identities. Musically, the original era was associated with Electronicore and Crunkcore (e.g., Dot Dot Curve, Millionaires), whereas Scenecore is defined by contemporary internet-based genres such as modern [Hyperpop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hyperpop "Hyperpop") and [Krushclub](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Krushclub "Krushclub").

A primary point of contention involves the perceived "sanitization" of the original subculture. Critics from the 2000s era argue that Scenecore reduces a lifestyle that was frequently controversial to a series of easily consumable pop-culture artifacts. By incorporating general memes like Nyan Cat and *Caramelldansen*, which were prevalent in the 2010s but not exclusive to Scene kids, Scenecore is seen by some as a historically inaccurate commodification of their identity. (See also: [2010s Meme Maximalism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/2010s_Meme_Maximalism "2010s Meme Maximalism"), for the offshoot of Scenecore incorporating these elements)

This has led to reciprocal hostility between the generations. Original participants frequently dismiss Scenecore as a superficial imitation, while Scenecore participants view this as "gatekeeping." The older generation's mockery of modern Scenecore trends, such as typing quirks and hyper-fixations, is often characterized by the younger demographic as a continuation of the same toxic social dynamics that the original subculture was once criticized for.

### Artist Behavior and Lyrical Content

The Scenecore music scene has been plagued by significant internal and external criticism regarding the conduct of its performers and the specific nature of their lyrics. A portion of the modern community is defined by its "call-out" culture, which emerged in response to documented instances of artist misconduct. Discussions within Scenecore spaces frequently center on allegations of financial scams, racism, and, in some fringe cases, the adoption of white supremacist dog-whistles and even grooming against specific prominent artists, leading many fans to prioritize "separating the art from the artist."

The lyrical content of the genre, particularly within the works of artists like SUICIDAL-IDOL, Rebzyyx, and BOY FANTASY, consistently utilizes graphic themes involving self-mutilation, eating disorders, and sexual violence. Critics and listeners have noted a lack of lyrical variety, with many songs repetitively focusing on Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD) and restrictive eating (EDs) as aesthetic props. Examples include the romanticization of sexual aggression in tracks like "*ecstacy*" and explicit references to suicide and cutting used for emotional resonance. Furthermore, the frequent mention of substance abuse, specifically MDMA and Xanax in the discography of artists like kets4eki, has led to claims that the genre normalizes severe trauma/addiction for algorithmic virality on TikTok.
