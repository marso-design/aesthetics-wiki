# Reggaetonero

_Also known as: Reguetonero_

[Sensitive Content Notice](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Aesthetics_Wiki:Content_Warning_Policy "Aesthetics Wiki:Content Warning Policy") ⚠️

The following article contains and discusses content that may be distressing to some readers.  
**Reason for Warning:** This page documents a subculture and music genre associated with themes of gender-based prejudice (misogynistic lyrics), violence, and criminality. The lyrical content is overtly sexual and centers on explicit erotic themes (e.g., the dance of perreo). Viewer discretion is advised.

**Reggaetoneros** (or **Reguetoneros**) are a youth subculture and lifestyle that originated in the Caribbean and Latin America in the mid-2000s, coinciding with the mass internationalization of the reggaeton music genre. This subculture is characterized by the combination of athletic streetwear, luxury accessories, and distinctive grooming and adherence to the customs and values disseminated by the music. The subculture is prominent throughout Spanish-speaking countries, particularly in Puerto Rico, the Dominican Republic, Colombia, and Panama.

## History

Reggaeton is an electronic music genre with origins in Panama and Puerto Rico during the late 1980s and early 1990s. Its creation is a product of transnational cultural fusion, combining Jamaican and Caribbean musical traditions with elements of American hip-hop and Latin American sounds.

### Origins and Early Development (1980s–1990s)

Reggaeton evolved primarily from two separate, but related, Caribbean scenes:

1. **Panama (Reggae en Español):** During the 1980s, Panamanian musicians began translating and adapting Jamaican Dancehall and Reggae songs into Spanish, a movement known as *Reggae en Español*. This was a key precursor, introducing the rhythmic and vocal style that would later characterize the genre.
2. **Puerto Rico (Underground/Dembow):** In Puerto Rico, producers and artists blended *Reggae en Español* with American [Hip-Hop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hip-Hop "Hip-Hop"), particularly the rapping style. This "underground" music, which was initially censored and circulated only on homemade cassettes in the barrios and guetos, utilized the foundational Dembow beat. The Dembow rhythm, a repetitive drum pattern, is widely attributed to the instrumental tracks from Jamaican dancehall (specifically the "Pounder" rhythm from Dennis the Menace Thompson in 1990), which became the rhythmic signature of Reggaeton. The term Reggaeton itself is often credited to Panamanian producer Michael Ellis or Puerto Rican artist Daddy Yankee in the mid-1990s.

Early productions, such as Playero 37 (1992) and The Noise (1993), launched the careers of pioneering artists like Daddy Yankee, Baby Rasta & Gringo, and Don Omar, though they often had to self-censor their lyrics and imagery to achieve any radio or television play.

### Massification and Internationalization (2000s)

The genre remained largely confined to underground Latin American communities until the mid-2000s, when it achieved global commercial success. This massification was driven by two key events:

* **Daddy Yankee's *Gasolina* (2004):** The success of this single, which reached international charts like the Billboard Hot 100, introduced Reggaeton's cultural baggage and sound to a massive global audience.
* **Blanqueamiento (Whitewashing) and Industry Support:** As the genre became commercially viable, the lyrical themes began to shift away from the raw social commentary of the underground toward more festive, sexually explicit, and romantic themes. Artists from middle-class backgrounds also joined the scene, broadening its appeal beyond the marginalized sectors of its origin. This process, known by some critics as *blanqueamiento* (whitewashing), helped the genre normalize and gain acceptance from major record labels.

The mass acceptance of Reggaeton in the 2000s and 2010s led directly to the formation and spread of the distinct Reggaetonero subculture across Latin America and the world.

## Fashion

The Reggaetonero aesthetic is a conscious projection of street credibility, social status, and sexuality. It is heavily influenced by the earlier fashion of [Hip-Hop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hip-Hop "Hip-Hop") and [Gangsta Rap](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Gangsta_Rap "Gangsta Rap"), emphasizing visible brand names, gold, and a focus on the body.

### Men

Masculine Reggaetonero fashion focuses on creating a powerful, often muscular, silhouette and displaying wealth or status symbols (flexing).
Outfits generally feature a foundation of baggy or wide clothing (influenced by rappers), paired with items that emphasize an athletic build. Common pieces include:

* Sports shirts (e.g., basketball jerseys or athletic wear).
* Suits or coordinating jackets, sometimes worn over a bare chest.
* Wide pants or jeans.

Grooming and Body:

* Haircuts are typically very short, nearly or completely shaved, though some styles leave a small, short tuft of hair on top.
* A muscular or toned physique is often sought after and displayed.
* Tattoos and piercings are common visual elements.

The accessories are the most defining element of the masculine aesthetic, often using high-contrast colors like black, white, and gold:

* Large, prominent gold or silver chain necklaces are essential, sometimes featuring crosses or anchors. This includes thick bracelets and watches, often embellished with diamonds or simulated diamonds.
* Sports caps (especially baseball style) are commonly worn.
* Sunglasses are frequently incorporated into the look, indoors and outdoors.

### Women

Feminine Reggaetonero fashion centers on a sensual, body-conscious style, designed for dancing and emphasizing a slim physique.

* Clothing is typically tight, fitted, and adjusted to accentuate the body, prioritizing a sensual and provocative appearance.
* Women often complement their outfits with flashy accessories similar to the men's style, including prominent necklaces and bracelets.

## Lifestyle

The subculture's lifestyle revolves around music, dance, and social assertion, often taking place in clubs, dance halls (*bailantas*), and neighborhood gatherings.

### Perreo

The defining activity of the Reggaetonero subculture is the dance style known as perreo (literally, "doggy style"). Perreo is a close-contact dance performed between partners and is characterized by overtly erotic and sensual movements, which sometimes imitate sexual postures. This dance is central to the subculture's expression of sexuality and is closely tied to the repetitive rhythmic base (Dembow) of the music.

### Social Assertion

Other key activities include going to nightclubs, hanging out at the beach, and flexing (showing off or displaying wealth and status through brands and jewelry) as a way to establish dominance and social standing within the group.

## Music

### Characteristics

The Reggaeton genre itself is a fusion of Jamaican [Reggae](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Reggae "Reggae"), Dancehall, and American [Hip-Hop](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hip-Hop "Hip-Hop"), with strong influences from Caribbean music and local Latin sounds like bomba and merengue house. Its characteristic feature is the distinctive, repetitive drum pattern known as Dembow (a variation of the Pounder beat).

## Criticism

Reggaeton and the Reggaetonero subculture are subjects of significant academic and public criticism, largely focused on lyrical content and its social implications. The most prominent criticism is the overt sexism and misogyny present in many of the lyrics. Critics argue that the songs, and the associated dance of perreo, objectify women and may glorify sexual harassment and gender-based prejudice. This has, at times, led to censorship attempts by government bodies in countries like Puerto Rico and Honduras.

The genre is often criticized for glamorizing criminality. Sociological studies have connected the subculture's aesthetic, vocabulary, and themes to street life and a perceived glorification of *bandolerismo* (criminality) and violence.

A separate aesthetic criticism often leveled against the music itself is that its reliance on the repetitive Dembow rhythm makes the music feel monotonous, unoriginal, or "cloned."
