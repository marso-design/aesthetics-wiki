Foundational Concept 🏛️

This article describes a **broad cultural concept**, not a specific visual aesthetic. It serves as an educational hub to explain ideas that influence many other styles on this wiki.

**Modernism** was a broad philosophical, cultural, and artistic movement that swept across Western society from the late 19th century through the mid-20th century. It was defined by a deliberate and radical break from the past and a search for new forms of expression. Arising from profound transformations in society (including rapid industrialization, urbanization, scientific advancements, and the psychological shocks of World War I) Modernism reflected a growing sense that traditional forms of art, literature, and social organization were no longer adequate to represent the realities of the modern world.

Modernism was driven by a spirit of experimentation. The poet Ezra Pound’s famous injunction, "Make it new," became a touchstone for artists across all disciplines. In literature, writers like James Joyce and Virginia Woolf rejected linear narratives in favor of fragmented forms like stream of consciousness to better capture the complex inner workings of the individual mind. In the visual arts, movements like [Cubism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Cubism "Cubism"), [Expressionism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Expressionism "Expressionism"), and [Surrealism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Surrealism "Surrealism") abandoned the goals of realistic depiction, instead exploring abstraction, subjective emotion, and the subconscious.

In architecture and design, a key tenet was "form follows function," a principle that stripped away historical ornamentation in favor of clean lines, geometric shapes, and new industrial materials like steel and glass, as seen in the work of the [Bauhaus](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Bauhaus "Bauhaus") school. Modernists were often utopian in their thinking, believing that art and design had the power to reshape society for the better. The movement's period of dominance is generally seen as ending after the devastation of World War II, which shattered its optimistic belief in progress and paved the way for the more skeptical and ironic worldview of **[Postmodernism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Postmodernism "Postmodernism")**.

## Art

In art, Modernism does not represent one particular style. It valued a rejection of traditional techniques. Modernism in art meant experimenting, which led to the use of new styles, materials, and techniques.

Art movements that are a part of Modernism include [Impressionism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Impressionism "Impressionism"), [Expressionism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Expressionism "Expressionism"), [Fauvism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Fauvism "Fauvism"), and [Cubism](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Cubism "Cubism").

## Architecture

"Form follows function" was a saying that best represented modernism in architecture. This meant a rejection of excessive ornamentation, the use of asymmetrical design, an analytical approach to the function of buildings, and innovation. The use of new materials - including reinforced concrete, curtain walls, steel frames, and ribbon windows - was also a part of Modernism.

Modernism in architecture includes [De Stijl](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/De_Stijl "De Stijl") and Bauhaus.

## Writing

Like all aspects of Modernism, writers also focussed on a rejection of tradition. This led to the development of unreliable narrators, as well as the use of stream-of-consciousness, multiple perspectives, and interior monologue.
