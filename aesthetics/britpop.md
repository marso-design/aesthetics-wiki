# Britpop

_Also known as: British Pop_

**Britpop** was a British music and cultural movement that emerged in the early 1990s and dominated UK popular culture during the mid-1990s. Musically, it produced bright, catchy alternative rock that deliberately referenced British pop music of the 1960s (like The Kinks and The Beatles) as a direct reaction to the dour themes and dominance of American-led [grunge](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Grunge "Grunge").

The aesthetic, which became a key part of the larger cultural moment known as [Cool Britannia](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Cool_Britannia "Cool Britannia"), was defined by a strong emphasis on British national identity and working-class pride. Britpop translated its sonic reverence for the past into a distinctly British style that merged elements of [Mod](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Mod "Mod") culture, [casuals](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Casuals "Casuals"), and [punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Punk "Punk")-derived irreverence.

## History

The term "Britpop" was coined by the media and became part of a larger British cultural movement known as "[Cool Britannia](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Cool_Britannia "Cool Britannia")," which aimed to celebrate British culture and creativity. This period saw a major resurgence of interest in British fashion, art, and politics, with the music scene being at the forefront of this revival.

The most prominent bands associated with Britpop were Oasis, Blur, Suede, and Pulp, often referred to as the “big four” of the movement. While each band maintained a unique style, they shared common traits such as catchy melodies, sharp lyrics, and a deep reverence for past British pop sounds. For instance, Oasis drew heavily from The Beatles, while Blur was influenced by The Kinks and the [Mod](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Mod "Mod") subculture.

The peak of Britpop is often marked by the famous chart battle between Blur and Oasis in 1995, which the media dubbed **“The Battle of Britpop.”** This rivalry received extensive coverage and was symbolic of the movement's large-scale cultural and musical impact, making it a defining feature of 1990s British pop culture.

### Successors

Britpop as a whole largely fizzled out around 1997–98 due to several significant cultural and musical events: Blur released *Blur* (1997), marking a departure toward alternative rock; Radiohead’s *OK Computer* (1997) shifted the scene toward a more futuristic and experimental sound; Oasis's highly anticipated *Be Here Now* (1997) was widely criticized as overproduced; the death of Princess Diana in August 1997 marked a cultural turning point; and the Spice Girls debut in 1996 recaptured pop's supremacy on UK radio. Pulp's final album, *This Is Hardcore* (1998), was often seen as representing a "comedown" from the Britpop party.

This decline led to the emergence of successor movements:

* **Post-Britpop:** This derivative form was popular in the late 1990s to mid-2000s, with groups like Coldplay, Travis, Radiohead, and Keane. While inspired by their predecessors, these bands favored contemplative, sensitive songwriting styles and drew less from 1960s British guitar groups, often looking instead to American indie music.
* **British Indie Rock Revival:** This mid-to-late 2000s movement, which was a local counter to the New York City indie scene, featured bands like The Libertines, Franz Ferdinand, and Arctic Monkeys. These acts were promoted as a rawer, post-punk-influenced reaction to the perceived "sterile" nature of Post-Britpop, though the later, less-original acts in this movement were mockingly labeled "Landfill Indie."

## Fashion

The Britpop aesthetic was defined by accessible, everyday pieces that elevated British heritage brands and subcultural uniforms. This **"Mod-ual"** (Mod + Casual) look was championed by the "big four" bands: **Oasis, Blur, Suede, and Pulp.**

### Garments and Brands

The look was driven by a blend of working-class street style and 1960s [Mod Revival](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Mod_Revival "Mod Revival") fashion, resulting in a cohesive aesthetic that became synonymous with the era's "[lad culture](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Lad "Lad")":

* The most iconic piece was the **parka** (often hooded, in khaki or olive green), popularized by Liam Gallagher of Oasis. Also essential was the Harrington jacket; a cropped utility jacket borrowed directly from 1960s [Mods](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Mod "Mod"), often worn by Damon Albarn.
* Track jackets and low-profile **Adidas sneakers** (like the Samba) were staples, directly adopting the look of British football [casuals](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Casuals "Casuals") (or "terrace wear"). Football shirts were worn frequently to signal regional patriotism and working-class pride.
* The subculture provided a massive commercial boost to classic British brands, including **Fred Perry** (polo shirts with the laurel wreath logo), **Burberry** (signature check pattern), and **Dr. Martens**.
* More intellectual acts, such as Jarvis Cocker of Pulp, favored **slim-silhouette suits** with a slightly retro, high-waisted fit, paired with skinny ties or pointed shoes, blending dandyism with working-class snark.

### Styling and Image

* The signature hairstyle was the **"Mod cut"**; a voluminous, helmet-like trim, often featuring a fringe that required specific grooming.
* The **Union Jack** became the most prominent visual symbol of the movement, used on clothing, album covers, and stage backdrops to project a sense of national pride and defiance.

## Music

### Characteristics

The genre was defined by its reverence for past British pop sounds and its distinct sonic approach. The four most prominent acts associated with the genre were Oasis, Blur, Suede, and Pulp (often referred to as the "big four").

Oasis famously drew heavily from The Beatles, while Blur was influenced by the Kinks and mod subculture. The sound was generally guitar-based, characterized by catchy hooks, sharp lyrics that often referenced British life and culture, and a distinct lack of the dour themes that dominated American alternative rock at the time.
