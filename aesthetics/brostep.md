# Brostep

_Also known as: American Dubstep_

**Brostep** is an aggressive subgenre of dubstep that peaked in the United States between 2010 and 2015. Diverging from the sub-bass focus of early UK dubstep, brostep shifted the genre toward a louder, mid-range-heavy sound characterized by mechanical synth noises and intense bass drops. Culturally, the genre became closely associated with American frat life, energy drink branding, and the early 2010s competitive gaming scene.

Although the term was originally coined as a pejorative insult by purists who felt the style had been "ruined" by "[bros](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Bro "Bro")," it was eventually reclaimed by the community to describe the specific era of maximalist North American electronic dance music (EDM) popularized by artists like Skrillex and Excision.

## History

The lineage of Brostep begins with the original Dubstep sound emerging from South London in the early 2000s. Artists like Skream and Benga pioneered a sound focused on sub-bass frequencies; low-end vibrations meant to be felt physically in a club rather than just heard. However, as the genre traveled across the Atlantic, it began to change.

The transition point is often attributed to the UK producer Rusko around 2010. His album *O.M.G.!* and tracks like "*Woo Boost*" introduced a snappier, louder production style that moved away from atmospheric dub toward pure energy. This shift laid the groundwork for the American explosion.

The defining moment for Brostep arrived with the release of Skrillex's *Scary Monsters and Nice Sprites* in late 2010. The EP was a massive departure from traditional dubstep, featuring dirty production, vocal chops, and a distinct lack of the original genre's subtlety. It became a cultural phenomenon, effectively serving as the "big bang" for the genre. Following this, the aesthetic became the dominant sound of the American festival circuit, with producers like Excision and Datsik pushing the sound to become even heavier and more metallic, often described as "robots fighting" or "*Transformers* sex."

## Visuals

The visual aesthetic of Brostep (primarily seen in album art and unofficial YouTube music uploads) was just as loud and aggressive as the music. It embraced a "toxic" or [industrial](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Industrial "Industrial")-inspired aesthetic that mirrored the jagged mechanical sounds of the bass. The imagery was heavily influenced by sci-fi, horror, and gamer culture.

The color palette was dominated by neon green (often referred to as "Monster Energy Green" due to its association with energy drink marketing), deep blacks, electric blues, and purples. Key motifs included gas masks, biohazard symbols, skulls, shattered glass, and dystopian machinery. The "toxic" aesthetic was ubiquitous; album covers often featured radioactive warning signs or monster-like characters wearing tactical gear and respirators, creating a visual shorthand for the music's "filthy" sound.

A specific visual trope of the era was the "Dubstep Joker." Images of the Joker from *The Dark Knight*, heavily edited with high contrast and neon effects, became the de facto mascot for YouTube dubstep compilations, symbolizing the chaotic and destructive nature of the "drop." In the 2020s, this image was revived and turned into the "Jonkler" brainrot meme.

## Culture

Brostep was not just a music genre but a convergence of media cultures. It served as the soundtrack for what has been described as the "Military Entertainment Complex" (MEC), where hyper-masculine entertainment in the form of first-person shooter video games and action movies intersected with music.

The genre became closely associated with the "[MLG](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/MLG "MLG")" (Major League Gaming) era of the internet. During the early 2010s, gaming montages (specifically for *Call of Duty*) almost exclusively used Brostep tracks. The aggressive and stuttering rhythm of the music matched the fast-paced adrenaline-fueled gameplay of shooters, creating a permanent association between a heavy bass drop and a "headshot" or "killstreak" in the minds of a generation.

This era also marked a shift in how crowds interacted with electronic music. Unlike the sway-and-bob dance style of traditional dubstep, Brostep crowds engaged in "mosh pits" and "raging," behaviors borrowed from [heavy metal](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Metalhead "Metalhead") and [hardcore punk](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Hardcore_Punk "Hardcore Punk") shows. This physical aggression aligned with the "[Bro](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Bro "Bro")" demographic, consisting of predominantly young men in fraternities or gaming circles who treated the music as an endurance test of loudness and intensity.

## Slang

* **The Drop:** The climax of a Brostep track. The song builds up tension with rising pitch and speed before "dropping" into a chaotic, heavy, half-time bass section.
* **Wub:** An onomatopoeia for the Low-Frequency Oscillator (LFO) bass sound (wub−wub−wub). Originally used as a way to mock the genre.
* **Filthy / Dirty:** The highest compliment in the genre. Describes a sound that is distorted, grimy, and abrasive.
* **Face-Melting:** Used to describe a track or bassline that is overwhelmingly loud and intense.
* **Transformers Sex:** A derogatory (but accurate) descriptor for the metallic, screeching, mechanical sound design of mid-range bass.
* **Rail Riding:** A practice at live shows where fans stand at the front barrier (the rail) and headbang violently.

## Criticism

The rise of Brostep created a massive schism in the electronic music world. "Old school" fans of UK Dubstep viewed Brostep as a bastardization of the genre. The primary criticism was sonic: where original dubstep focused on sub-bass (frequencies below 60Hz that rattle the chest), Brostep shifted the focus to the mid-range (wobbling, screeching sounds that assault the ears).

Critics argued that Brostep lacked groove and space, replacing the meditative qualities of the original sound with a "sugar rush" of noise. Rusko, despite being credited with birthing the "aggressive" dubstep style, criticized the direction the genre took, stating that the new wave was just "someone screaming in your face for an hour" and that the fans simply wanted the "most disgusting, hard, dirty, distorted music possible" rather than the nuance of the original sound.

The term "Brostep" itself was an insult used to mock the "frat bro" audience that Skrillex and Excision attracted, implying that the music had been dumbed down for a mainstream, hyper-masculine American audience that didn't understand the roots of UK [rave](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rave "Rave") culture.
