**Princecore** is a subgenre of [Royalcore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Royalcore "Royalcore"), a more masculine variant of [Princesscore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Princesscore "Princesscore") and a less mature version of [Kingcore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Kingcore "Kingcore"). Princecore is visually extremely similar to [Ouji](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Ouji "Ouji") and Aristocrat in [Lolita](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Lolita "Lolita") culture. (It also seems to take from theater and [ballet](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Balletcore "Balletcore") costumes.)

A majority of Princecore writing is heavily centered around fictional prompts centering relationships between princes and [knights](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Knightcore "Knightcore"), or princesses/other princes. Though less common, fictional relationships between princes and female knights also make up a part of the Princecore aesthetic. There are generally two sides of Princecore, one being [elegant](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Elegant "Elegant") and mature, the other slightly more childish and young with [fantastical](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Fantasy "Fantasy") aspects.

Ironically, it’s mostly women who indulge in this aesthetic—although these kinds of clothes are still worn by men in ballet and theater. It is also popular amongst MLM (man loves men) and genderqueer individuals.

## Visual

Visuals are shared with other Royalcore genres like Princesscore and [Queencore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Queencore "Queencore") in that castles, crowns, and ornate gardens are present. Princecore may rely more on paper and quills or pens to suggest poem writing to lovers and may be softer than [Kingcore](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Kingcore "Kingcore"). Examples are listed in the gallery.
