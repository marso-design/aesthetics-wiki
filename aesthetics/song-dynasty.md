# Song Dynasty

_Also known as: Song China_

Needs Work 🛠️

**Song Dynasty** is a cultural aesthetic encompassing the diverse styles and sensibilities that emerged and flourished within China during the Song dynasty, from 960 to 1279. This period saw remarkable economic prosperity, the rise of neo-Confucianism, and a profound emphasis on simplicity, natural elegance, and scholarly refinement in art, poetry, and ceramics.

Song dynasty aesthetics enjoy broad recognition in China's modern cultural appreciation, though their actual influence remains relatively limited.

## History

*Needs Work. Currently parts of this section are only outlines that link important concepts to their corresponding period. These concepts should be explained in the Philosophy and Visuals sections, not the History section.*

The Song Dynasty (founded 960 CE) took "valuing culture over martial power" (崇文抑武) as its state policy because of how the [Tang Dynasty](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Tang_Dynasty "Tang Dynasty") fell to separatist warlord regimes. Intellectuals enjoyed an extraordinarily high social standing because the first emperor, [Zhao Kuangyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Taizu_of_Song) (reign: 960–976 CE), set up a civil service system to "share state power with scholar-officials" (与士大夫共天下) and encouraged military officials to study as well.

The Song court upheld relaxed cultural policies. The royal ancestral precept by Zhao Kuangyin swore never to put scholar-officials to death for their remarks. As Confucianism flourished again, Buddhism and Taoism were also officially acknowledged and supported by the state.

Mid-Northern Song saw the rise of literati painting (文人画, pinyin: *wenrenhua*) when scholar-officials like Su Shi (1037–1101 CE) advocated using painting as an expression for the artist’s character and emotions like poems.

During the late Northern Song, under the reign of [Zhao Ji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Huizong_of_Song) (reign: 1100–1125 CE), a monarch blessed with extraordinary artistic talent, Song aesthetics attained its zenith at the official court level. He established the Imperial Academy of Painting (翰林图画院) and presided over the compilation of authoritative art classics, including *Manual of Calligraphy of the Xuanhe Era* (《宣和书谱》) and *Manual of Paintings of the Xuanhe Era* (《宣和画谱》). He created the Slender Gold Script (痩金体) in calligraphy, and ordered court craftsmen to create the *Tianqing* glaze color which he saw in his dream.

After the Song court fled south to Hangzhou after the Jingkang Catastrophe of 1127 CE, and many people losing their northern homeland, the artistic mindset was generally shifted.

## Philosophy

*Needs Work*  
[Neo-Confucianism (理学)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Confucianism) was the most representative school of thought in the Song Dynasty. It pursued the ideal of investigating things to fathom their ultimate principles (格物穷理), maintaining that one must observe all worldly matters meticulously to uncover the inherent Heavenly Principle (天理) within them.

The Song Dynasty saw the golden age of [Chan Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zen). Its philosophy of "No reliance on written words; direct insight into one’s original mind" (不立文字，直指本心) significantly influenced literati and artists of the era.

Under the Song's policy of valuing culture over martial power (崇文抑武), scholar-officials (文人士大夫) became the backbone of society. With multiple social identities, their aesthetic tastes set the dominant fashion of the time.

Influence of [Daoism philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoism) shaped Song aesthetics' emphasis on naturalness, subtlety and understated elegance.

A strong antiquarian trend arose in the Song period. Epigraphy and bronze studies (金石学) boosted research on [ancient bronze](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Bronze_Age_China "Bronze Age China") ritual vessels, bells and stone stelae (钟鼎碑刻).

### In Painting

The influence of Neo-Confucianism in the Song Dynasty on painters generally falls into two categories. Academic court painters (院体画家) focused on rigorously depicting the laws of nature through the external pursuit of investigating things to attain knowledge (格物). Literati painters (文人画家) laid stress on expressing personal sentiments and philosophical reflections via the internal cultivation of disciplining one’s mind (格心).

The independent evolution of literati painting during the Song Dynasty was influenced by the growing self-awareness of scholars, who integrated many philosophies. Zen Budhhist thoughts encouraged literati painters to seek spiritual relief and personal freedom in their brush-and-ink creations, giving rise to the *xieyi* (写意) freehand style . The *mo-hua mo-gu* (墨花墨骨) flower-and-bird painting school that adopted ink wash instead of gorgeous colours was inspired by Daoism's pursuit of nature and pristine simplicity.

## Visuals

The visual characteristics of Song Dynasty aesthetics are that they appear clean, quiet, and elegant.

### Paintings

Song dynasty painting favored subdued tones over vibrant colors. The Song paintings we see today are all left with an aged, muted appearance. The visual elements of paintings vary according to traditional thematic categories:

**Landscape**  
Song dynasty landscape painting features *shuimo (水墨, lit. "water ink")* as the primary medium, substituted with limited plant and mineral pigments. Realistic texture of rocks and tree barks are drawn with brush techniques called *cun-fa* (皴法) and different gradations of ink. Architecture is sometimes drawn with the *jiehua* (界画) technique to be precisely scaled.

Common settings are **high mountains, deep valleys, streams, waterfalls, mist, riverbanks, lakes, snowscapes, rainy scenes, pines, willows, bamboo, reeds, rocks,** and **sunrises or sunsets**. Buildings and figures like **pavilions, boats, fishermen, scholars, woodcutters,** and **herders** are depicted but play secondary roles to the natural environment.

Northern Song paintings feature mountains that fill the whole scroll from top to bottom, with dense forests and detailed rocks. Sometimes a path, bridge, or tree are drawn to create visual flow. People, buildings, or animals are tiny details. Southern Song paintings feature large reserved white spaces of mist, water, or the sky, with the main content shifted towards the corners and sides. Human figures are bigger and more important.

**Flower-and-Birds**  
Animals and plants carry symbolic or naturalistic roles. Common motifs include **reeds, water grasses**, blossoming branches of **plum, orchid, bamboo, chrysanthemum, lotus, peony, hibiscus,** and **peach**, and fruits like **loquat and pomegranate.** Animal life features **golden pheasants, mynas, orioles, sparrows, mandarin ducks, egrets, butterflies, bees, dragonflies, kingfishers, fish, shrimp,** and **crabs.** Other elements are minimal, limited to **Taihu rocks, railings, lattice windows,** and **flower stands.**

Court paintings are smooth and detailed, and use bright and rich colors. Gongbi (工笔, fine brushes) and mogu (没骨, not outlining) are commonly used techniques. Literati paintings are mostly drawn in ink, and favor xieyi (写意) techniques. The content they draw often sybolize nobleness in literature.

**Figures**  
Song dynasty figure paintings often place characters in settings such as **courtyards, studies,** or **market streets**, featuring interiors like **tables, painted screens,** and displayed **porcelain wares**, and objects like **scrolls, books, brushes, inkstones, *qin*** (琴) **zithers, wine vessels,** or **tea cups.**

The figures depicted range from **scholarly gentlemen** and **monks** to **children, maidservants, elderly men,** and occasionally **foreigners**. Their actions involve **discussing, reading, painting, playing the qin, tasting tea, drinking wine, paying visits, playing,** or **strolling idly.**

### Ceramics

Song dynasty ceramics are celebrated for their refined forms and subtle glazes. The five major kilns of Song Dynasty developed distinctive visual languages that reflect the period’s aesthetic ideals of simplicity, elegance, and naturalism. A revival of ancient bronze vessel shapes started in Song dynasty out of a longing for ["Three Dynasties"](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Bronze_Age_China "Bronze Age China") ideal governance

**Ru (汝) Kiln** features the signature ***Tianqing* (天青) glaze** with a matte, jade-like surface on the pale incense-ash grey body. **Crab*-*claw crackles** (蟹爪纹) subtly vein the glaze.

"Ru kiln light-Tianqing censer" (汝窑淡天青釉弦纹三足樽式炉)

"Ru kiln Tianqing brush washer" (汝窑天青釉圆洗)

"Ru kiln Tianqing tray" (汝窑天青釉三足樽承盘)

**Guan (官) Kiln** exhibits **celadon** or **pale-celadon glazes** with a glossy, glass-like finish. Large **ice-crackle patterns** (冰裂纹) cover the surface. The rim appears purple due to a thin glaze over dark clay, while the unglazed foot shows iron-black.

"Guan kiln celadon flower pot" (官窑青釉方花盆)

"Guan kiln celadon brush washer" (官窑青釉圆洗)

"Guan kiln pale-celadon vase" (官窑粉青釉弦纹瓶)

**Ge (哥) Kiln** is distinguished by its “**golden and iron wires**” (金丝铁线, a combination of large and small crackles stained yellowish and dark brown). Glazes are celadon with an oily, soft sheen. Like Guan, it shares purple rim and iron-black foot details.

### Calligraphy

The visual elements of Song dynasty calligraphy emphasize expressive brushwork and natural structure. Strokes vary in pressure, producing **contrasts between thin, swift lines and thick, heavy dots**. Characters often **tilt slightly**, breaking strict symmetry, while **extended horizontal or vertical strokes** create dynamic spacing. The overall composition prioritizes rhythmic flow over rigid alignment, reflecting the literati ideal of spontaneous personal expression (尚意).

*The Cold Food Observance* by Su Shi

*Poem of Fragrance* by Zhao Ji

*Poem on the Hall of Pines and Wind* by Huang Tingjian

### Architecture

Northern Song wooden buildings are finely structured with complex layouts and diverse decorations. Small wooden fittings such as **caisson ceilings, shrines, doors, windows, scripture cabinets** and **railings** grew more elaborate and fancy. **Glazed materials** and **colorful paintings** made architectural tones rich and gorgeous, and **jade-style decorative** patterns took the lead in painted ornaments. **Stone and wood carvings** were used in more architectural areas, graded by complexity and developed into an independent professional craft. Southern Song wooden architecture had strong local characteristics, mostly adopting hall-style frames with a neat, elegant and simple style.

### Women's Fashion

Song dynasty women's clothing features clean, vertical lines and understated elegance.   
**Tops**

* an inner ***Song-mo (宋抹)* breast wrap** that is tucked into the skirt.
* A ***duijin-shan* (对襟衫) jacket** (characterised by parallel collars and side slits) of variable length must be worn over the breast wrap. The jacket could be tucked into the skirt if it's short.

**Bottoms**

* include various high-waisted wrap **pleated skirts** and **trousers**.

**Outerwear**

* **he-xiu (貉袖) jackets** (casual)
* sleeveless or short-sleeved ***Song-beixin (宋背心)* vests** (casual)
* ***beizi (褙子)* coat** (formal)
* ***changyi (氅衣)* robe** (formal)

**Makeup**

* Noblewomen often adorned their faces with tiny **pearls** applied to the cheeks, forehead, and temples.

## Activities

*Needs Verification*  
The Four Elegances of the Song Dynasty (宋四雅, known as 四般闲事 at the time) refer to the four quintessential cultural practices that defined the elegant lifestyle of Song Dynasty (960-1279) literati and upper-class society.

### Fen Xiang

Fen Xiang (焚香, burning incense) was an essential practice in Song households, used for both practical and spiritual purposes.

1. Selecting incense: High-quality agarwood (沉香), sandalwood (檀香), or blended incense (合香) was chosen.
2. Preparing the incense burner: A ceramic or bronze censer (香炉) was filled with fine sand or ash to hold the incense coil or stick.
3. Burning method: Song connoisseurs preferred the "indirect burning" (隔火焚香) technique—heating the incense with a charcoal disc separated by a layer of ash to produce a steady, smoke-free fragrance.
4. Appreciation: The subtle aroma was enjoyed slowly, often while reading, painting, or meditating.

### Diancha

Diancha (点茶) was the dominant tea ceremony of the Song Dynasty, evolving from the Tang Dynasty's jiancha (煎茶, boiled tea) method and reaching its zenith during this period.

1. Grinding: Compressed tea cakes (茶饼) were first roasted to release their aroma, then ground into an extremely fine powder using a tea mill (茶碾).
2. Preparing the tea bowl: A small amount of tea powder was placed in a black-glazed tea bowl (建盏), with a ladle of hot water added to form a paste.
3. Whisking: Boiling water was poured in a thin stream while whisking vigorously with a bamboo tea whisk (茶筅) to create a thick, frothy layer on top.
4. Cha Bai Xi: Masters would create intricate patterns on the foam surface using spoons and water, known as "tea painting art" (茶百戏), "divided tea" (分茶), or "water painting" (水丹青).

### Gua Hua

Gua Hua (挂画, hanging paintings) was more than just decoration—it was a formal practice of art appreciation and cultural exchange.

1. Selecting paintings: Song literati collected and displayed landscape paintings (山水画), flower-and-bird paintings (花鸟画), and calligraphy works (书法).
2. Seasonal matching: Paintings were chosen to complement the season or occasion—landscapes in spring, chrysanthemums in autumn, etc..
3. Display method: Paintings were carefully mounted on silk scrolls and hung on plain walls to avoid distractions.
4. Appreciation gathering: Friends and guests would gather to admire the works, discuss artistic techniques, and exchange poems inspired by the paintings.

Song dynasty scholars admiring a painting, from *Depiction of Ancient Artifacts* (宋人博古圖). ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_the_Song_dynasty#/media/File:Detail_宋人博古圖_Depiction_of_Ancient_Artifacts.png))

### Cha Hua

Cha Hua (插花, flower arranging) in the Song Dynasty developed a distinct style that emphasized simplicity, elegance, and spiritual symbolism, different from the more elaborate Tang Dynasty arrangements.

1. Selecting materials: Flowers and branches were chosen based on seasonality and symbolic meaning, sometimes combined with fruits, vegetables, or dried branches to create a harmonious composition.
2. Container choice: Simple ceramic vases, bamboo tubes, or stone basins were preferred over ornate ones.
3. Arrangement principles: Followed the "three main branches" (三主枝) structure representing heaven, earth, and man, with minimal flowers to highlight natural beauty.

## Modern Revivals and Recognition

Among the general public in China, the label of the "weak Song" puts people off taking the initiative to explore and gain an in-depth understanding of Song-dynasty aesthetics. History textbooks from People's Education Press devote far more space to recording military crises than to introducing the dynasty’s aesthetic culture. Mainstream popular media set against the Song backdrop, such as *The Legend of the Condor Heroes*, also features far more content about military conflicts than artistic and aesthetic traditions. People readily acknowledge the dynasty’s splendid cultural legacy, yet harbor subtle doubts about that legacy’s value on account of its ineffective military defense.

Public understanding of the Song within China is now slowly transitioning from national historical grief to one centered on cultural and aesthetic appreciation, recognising that its economy, culture and fine domestic arts once hit unparalleled heights by global standards at the time. In recent years, particularly since the late 2010s and into the 2020s, driven by a resurgence of interest in traditional Han Chinese culture and the appreciation of younger generations for the aesthetics of the Song dynasty, new garments are being produced in the style of Song-dynasty Hanfu.

The growing popularity of Song Dynasty aesthetics in China is partly driven by rising cultural confidence and interest in indigenous traditions. Because Song aesthetics was the source of inspiration for many Japanese concepts, such as Zen, the revival as a rediscovery of a native Chinese aesthetic vocabulary sometimes serve as an alternative to Japanese cultural influence.

At the same time, the pervasive preoccupation with national peril in Song literary works produced an enormous body of patriotic writing that rarely registers with overseas collectors, whose interests are largely confined to antique craft pieces, paintings and calligraphy. Internationally, the aesthetics of the Song dynasty are highly respected, especially among historians, artists, designers, architects, and ceramic collectors. This reputation is disproportionately high relative to its name recognition among the general public.
