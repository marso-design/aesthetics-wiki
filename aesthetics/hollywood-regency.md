# Hollywood Regency

_Also known as: Regency Moderne_

**Hollywood Regency** is an interior design, architectural, and landscape design aesthetic named after the movie-making industry of southern California, most famously represented by the glamorous homes and estates of actors and actresses during Hollywood's "Golden Era," roughly the 1920s through the 1950s. The style is more ornamental, eclectic, and maximalist than [Art Deco](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Art_Deco "Art Deco"), relying on bold color and contrast alongside metallic and glass accents to signify both opulence and comfort. Lavish interiors depicted on the silver screen during this period prompted moviegoing audiences to emulate the look in their own homes, and the style remains a current and lively area of design work both inside and outside of southern California.

## History

The term "Hollywood Regency" is thought to have originated with the interior designer Dorothy Draper sometime in the 1920s, though other sources place the style's emergence and popularization closer to the following decade. Draper and decorator William "Billy" Haines are credited as the style's central popularizers, dressing the homes of movie stars in a larger-than-life fashion, while Draper in particular became known for her use of black and white checkerboard floors alongside scrollwork and plasterwork drawn from [Rococo](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Rococo "Rococo") and [Baroque](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Baroque "Baroque") ornament, adapted toward a more maximalist and less repetitive sensibility. The architect John Elgin Woolf, working alongside his partner Robert Koch, is separately credited with establishing the style's architectural elements by synthesizing nineteenth-century French, Greek Revival, and [Modernist](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Modernism "Modernism") influences for a clientele of Hollywood stars including Cary Grant, Judy Garland, and Katharine Hepburn.

The author Rochelle Greayer described the Hollywood Regency style as:

> "*Hollywood Regency is glitz and glamour covered in lacquer, chrome, and mirrored finishes. Every detail is meant to convey luxury and there is always the feeling that people should look good in the design— particularly if they are wearing satin bathrobes and sipping a cocktail.*"
> ― Rochelle Greayer

## Visuals

Hollywood Regency interiors favor bold jewel tones such as topaz blue, emerald green, amethyst pink, and citrine gold, often set against black and white checkerboard flooring, and the sunburst wall mirror or clock in either silver or gold is one of the style's most consistent recurring fixtures. Surfaces lean toward the reflective and the glossy, with lacquered finishes, mirrored panels, and chrome or gold metallic accents used throughout a room, while furniture favors sleek, slim-profiled silhouettes arranged with a strong attention to symmetry, such as matched pairs of chairs flanking a sofa. Sumptuous materials including velvet, fur, silk, polished stone, and lucite are treated as staples of the style, and animal figures and animal-print textiles, such as zebra and leopard patterns, appear regularly as accent pieces. Lighting fixtures are similarly ornamental, with crystal chandeliers and exaggerated floor lamps often finished in metallic or gemstone detailing. Architecturally, the style associated with John Elgin Woolf is marked by mansard rooflines, Doric columns, leaded oval glass windows, and a front entry treatment known as "Pullman doors."

While Hollywood Regency shares a slim-profiled furniture silhouette with [Mid-Century Modern](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Mid-Century_Modern "Mid-Century Modern") design, which emerged roughly a decade later, the two styles diverge in finish and material: Mid-Century Modern favors natural wood and a less ornate approach, whereas Hollywood Regency leans toward glossy, shiny surfaces and heavier ornamentation. Contemporary interior designers including Kelly Wearstler, Miles Redd, and Carleton Varney continue to work in a modern take on the style.
