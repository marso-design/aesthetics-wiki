**Totalitarian Kitsch** is a term that describes the aesthetic of state-enforced optimism, produced by and for authoritarian regimes to deny political complexity, suppress dissent, and manufacture collective emotional consensus. The term was coined by Czech novelist Milan Kundera in his 1984 novel *The Unbearable Lightness of Being*, and has since been applied by scholars to the art, architecture, cinema, and mass spectacle of regimes including Stalinist Soviet Union, Nazi Germany, Mao's People's Republic of China, Fascist Italy, and contemporary North Korea. Unlike other forms of [kitsch](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Kitsch "Kitsch"), its defining characteristic is not technical incompetence or low production values, but a deliberate suppression of reality: it is an aesthetic that refuses to acknowledge death, failure, doubt, or the existence of the unacceptable. As Kundera's narrator puts it, "the aesthetic ideal of the categorical agreement with being" — a world in which everything that contradicts the official image of life has been abolished.

Although the aesthetic manifests differently across regimes and contexts, its underlying logic is consistent: [kitsch](https://aesthetics.fandom.com/wiki/Kitsch "Kitsch") is used as a tool for political mobilization, and the state becomes the sole arbiter of sentiment. Where ordinary kitsch produces uncritical emotional warmth, totalitarian kitsch weaponizes that warmth to foreclose dissent. It is, in Kundera's formulation, "a folding screen set up to curtain off death."
